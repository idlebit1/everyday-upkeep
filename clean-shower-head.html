Descale Your Shower Head

Every shower pushes hard water through dozens of pinhole nozzles, and the dissolved minerals it leaves behind harden into limescale right at the openings. One by one the jets clog, so the spray goes weak, splits into stray sideways streams, or dribbles where it used to drum. The fix costs almost nothing: slip a bag of white vinegar over the head, let it soak, scrub the nozzles, and rinse. Twice a year — or whenever the flow drops — and the shower comes back to full, even pressure.

🗓️ Typically every 6 months ⏱️ ~10 min hands-on 🧴 About $0–$5 of white vinegar 🚿 Restores full flow & pressure

Why it matters

Tap water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium, and every nozzle on a shower head is a tiny pinhole where that water evaporates and the minerals are left behind. Over months they harden into limescale that narrows and plugs the jets. Here's what that quiet crusting costs you:

Clogged nozzles, sideways spray

Limescale plugs the jets one by one, so the once-even spray goes weak, breaks into stray sideways streams that miss you and hit the wall, or splits into a fine mist. The classic sign your head is scaling shut.

A worse shower, every day

A home for biofilm and bacteria

The damp, mineral-crusted inside of a shower head is a textbook spot for slimy biofilm — and studies have found bacteria, including Mycobacterium, colonizing neglected heads. Descaling clears the scale that shelters it.

Slime you can't see

Full flow and pressure, restored

Open the jets back up and the head sprays the way it did when it was new — strong, even, drumming pressure across every nozzle. It's the difference a quick soak makes, no plumber and no new fixture required.

Like-new spray

The cheapest fix in the bathroom

No special chemicals, no tools, no skill. A jug of white vinegar and a plastic bag handle it — call it a dollar or two a year. There's almost nothing in the house that pays back this well for this little effort.

~$0–$5, a few minutes

How often should you descale it?

"Every 6 months" is the sensible default — but hard-water homes scale up fast and may need it every few months, while soft water can stretch the interval. The truest signal of all is the spray itself. Answer four questions:

1. How hard is your water?
The white, chalky crust on faucet tips, the kettle, and the shower glass is limescale — the same stuff plugging the nozzles. The more of it you see around the bathroom, the faster the head clogs.
2. How's the spray right now?
Look at the head while it runs: dead nozzles, a split or sideways stream, or a noticeably weaker drum than you remember all mean scale is already winning — descale now, then reset the clock.
3. Fixed or handheld head?
A handheld unscrews easily for a full soak; a big rain head has many more nozzles to keep clear. Both descale the same way — the bag method works on whatever you can't easily remove.
4. What's the finish?
Plain chrome and stainless shrug off vinegar. Some coated, brushed brass, oil-rubbed bronze, or specialty "anti-scale" finishes can dull or discolor — check the maker and keep the soak short on those.

    How to descale your shower head

    Hands-on time is maybe ten minutes — the rest is the bag soaking while you do something else. Read the techniques first (especially the finish warning and the no-removal bag trick), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Before you start — finishes, soak time, and a slippery tub

    • Check the finish before you soak. Plain chrome and stainless are vinegar-safe. Some coated, brushed-brass, nickel, oil-rubbed-bronze, or specialty "anti-scale" finishes can dull, etch, or discolor with acid — check the manufacturer, and on those, keep the soak short (30 minutes, not overnight) or skip vinegar entirely.
    • Don't over-soak delicate finishes or rubber parts. For standard chrome a long soak is fine; for anything coated or questionable, less time is safer. Vinegar is mild, but acid plus hours plus a soft finish is how you trade scale for a stain.
    • Rinse thoroughly afterward. Flush plenty of fresh water through the head and wipe the outside dry so no vinegar lingers on the finish or in the spray.
    • Mind your footing. A wet tub or a step stool to reach a high fixed head is a fall waiting to happen — take it slow, and dry the floor first if you can.
    • Never mix vinegar with bleach or any chlorine cleaner — the combination releases toxic chlorine gas. Vinegar on its own is all you need here.

    🧰 What you'll need

    White vinegar A sturdy plastic bag (freezer/zip-top) A rubber band or zip tie An old toothbrush A cloth or towel Optional: wrench + plumber's tape (to unscrew the head) ~10 min hands-on, plus soak time

    📐 Four things worth seeing first

    No need to remove it — bag it

    Fill a sturdy plastic bag with white vinegar and slip it up over the head until every nozzle on the spray face is under the surface. The whole soak happens in place — no tools, no unscrewing, no plumber.

    Secure it and let it soak

    Cinch the bag around the neck with a rubber band or zip tie so it stays put. Let it sit 30 minutes to overnight — longer for heavy scale on chrome, but keep it short (about 30 min) on brass or coated finishes.

    Scrub the nozzles, rub the tips

    After the soak, work an old toothbrush over the spray face to clear loosened scale. On heads with soft rubber nozzle tips, rub each one with your thumb or finger — the limescale plugs pop right off the flexible nubs.

    Flush, then wipe dry

    Run hot water through the head for a minute to flush out the scale you just loosened, watching the spray come back to full and even. Then wipe the finish dry so no vinegar or water spots linger.

    Signs it's overdue — and when to call a pro

    A descaled head sprays strong and even within minutes of finishing. These are the signals it's scaling shut now — and the cases where the spray won't come back no matter how long you soak, which point past the shower head itself:

    • Weak, uneven, or sideways spray. The clearest sign of scale. Dead nozzles, stray streams hitting the wall, or a fine mist where there used to be a strong drum all say it's time to soak.
    • Visible white crust on the nozzles or spray face. That chalky buildup you can see is limescale, and there's more inside. A descale clears it; left alone it only spreads.
    • Still weak after a thorough descale? The problem is upstream of the head. A clogged inlet screen or filter washer at the connection is the first thing to check — a quick clean there often does it.
    • Low pressure at every fixture, not just this shower. If the whole house is weak, it's not the head — it could be a pressure-regulator, a partially closed main valve, or a supply issue. That's a plumber's diagnosis.
    • A failing shower valve or cartridge. Weak or temperature-erratic flow that descaling doesn't fix can be a worn mixing cartridge or valve behind the wall — a plumbing repair, not a cleaning job.
    • A cracked head or one that leaks no matter what. If the body is cracked, or it drips from a seam or the connection even after you re-tape the threads, replace it — a new basic head is inexpensive.
    • Pro tip: if you're on a private well or notice rusty or sediment-heavy water, the buildup may be iron or grit rather than just limescale — worth having your water tested, and a whole-house filter can spare every fixture in the house.

    Common questions

    How long should I soak it — and can I leave it overnight?

    For standard chrome or stainless, 30 minutes clears light scale and an overnight soak handles heavy buildup with no harm. The longer the soak, the more it dissolves, so when in doubt go longer — on plain chrome. The exception is finish: some coated, brushed-brass, nickel, oil-rubbed-bronze, or specialty "anti-scale" heads can dull or discolor with prolonged acid contact, so keep those to about 30 minutes (or skip vinegar and use the manufacturer's recommended cleaner). If you're not sure what your finish is, start with a short soak and check before going longer.

    My pressure is still low after descaling — how do I fix it?

    Work outward from the head. First, unscrew the head and check the small inlet screen or filter washer where it meets the shower arm — it's a common grit and scale trap, and a quick rinse or toothbrush often restores the flow on its own. Many heads also contain a flow restrictor (a small plastic disc); in the U.S., federal rules cap shower heads at 2.5 gallons per minute, so removing or tampering with the restrictor isn't legal and can affect your water bill and any warranty — replace the whole head if you want more flow rather than gutting one. If the pressure is low at every fixture in the house, the cause is upstream — a pressure regulator, a partly closed valve, or a supply problem — and that's a plumber's call.

    How do I clean a handheld shower head?

    Two easy options. The bag method works exactly as it does on a fixed head — tie a bag of vinegar over the spray face. Or, because a handheld unscrews from its hose easily, just detach it and drop the whole head into a bowl or bucket of vinegar so it soaks fully submerged; that's often the most thorough route. Either way, scrub the nozzles afterward, rub any rubber tips, check the inlet screen at the hose connection, and rinse well. Wrap the threads with fresh plumber's tape if it weeps when you reattach it.

    How often should I do this in a hard-water area?

    Hard water is the whole story here — the more dissolved minerals, the faster the nozzles crust over. In a genuinely hard-water home (heavy white scale on faucets, glass, and the kettle), every 2–3 months is realistic, and the spray itself will tell you: when it starts going weak or uneven, it's time, whatever the calendar says. Soft water or a whole-house softener can stretch the interval well past six months. A handheld squeegee on the glass and a quick wipe of the head after showers slows buildup between descales.

    Can I use something other than vinegar?

    Yes. A commercial limescale or descaling solution (citric, sulfamic, or lactic acid) works the same way and can be gentler on delicate finishes — follow its label, and it's the safer pick for coated or specialty heads the manufacturer flags. Citric-acid powder dissolved in warm water is a low-odor middle ground. In a pinch, lemon juice is a weaker natural acid that'll handle light scale with a longer soak. What you should not do is mix vinegar with bleach or any chlorine cleaner — that combination releases toxic gas. Plain white vinegar remains the cheapest, most reliable choice for ordinary chrome and stainless heads.

    Should I take the shower head off, or just bag it in place?

    For a fixed head, bagging it in place is easiest and works completely — no tools, no risk of cross-threading when you reattach. Removing the head pays off when scale is heavy (a full submerged soak in a bowl reaches the internals better), when you want to clean the inlet screen and filter washer, or for a handheld that pops off in seconds. If you do unscrew it, wrap the threads with fresh plumber's tape before reattaching and hand-tighten plus a gentle nudge with a wrench — don't crank a chrome or plastic fitting hard enough to crack it.
