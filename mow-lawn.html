Mow Your Lawn

Mowing isn't just tidying up — it's the single biggest thing you do to your lawn, and the easiest to get wrong. Cut at the right height with a sharp blade and you grow thick, green turf that crowds out weeds on its own. Scalp it short, or let it get shaggy and hack it back, and you stress the grass, bare the soil, and hand the weeds an opening. The trick isn't a calendar — it's following the grass's growth and never taking off more than a third at a time.

Why it matters

Mowing is the lawn chore you do the most — and most lawn troubles trace straight back to how it's cut. Here's what mowing right (and what mowing wrong) really does:

Scalping stresses the grass

Cut too short and you strip the leaf the plant feeds on, shock the roots, and expose bare soil to the sun. A scalped lawn browns out, bakes dry, and recovers slowly.

Brown patches & stress

Short turf invites weeds

Weed seeds need sunlight on bare soil to sprout. Tall, dense grass shades the ground and starves them out — mowing height is the cheapest weed control you have.

$100 – $400/yr weed control

The right height grows deep roots

Taller blades feed bigger root systems. A lawn kept at its proper height roots deep, finds its own water, and shrugs off the heat that flattens a close-cropped one.

Drought resilience, for free

A dull blade tears, not cuts

A sharp blade slices clean. A dull one shreds the tips, which dry out and turn brown or whitish across the whole lawn — and the ragged wounds let disease in.

$5 – $20 to sharpen

Growth, not the calendar

Grass doesn't grow on a fixed schedule — it sprints in cool, wet spells and stalls in heat or drought. Mow when it has grown, not because it's the weekend.

Follow the grass

Clippings feed the lawn

Mulched clippings break down fast and hand back a quarter of the lawn's nitrogen — a free feeding every mow. They don't cause thatch; they just disappear.

~25% of your fertilizer, free

The one-third rule

Never remove more than one-third of the grass blade in a single mow. The blade is the plant's solar panel — cut off more than a third at once and you starve the roots, force the lawn to burn stored energy regrowing leaf, and leave it open to weeds, drought, and disease. If the lawn got away from you, don't scalp it back in one pass: cut a third now, let it recover for a few days, then cut again. This one rule does more for a lawn than any bag of fertilizer.

What's your mowing plan?

"Mow every weekend" is a habit, not a rule. The right cadence follows how fast the grass is actually growing, and the right height depends entirely on your grass type. Three quick questions:

1. What kind of grass do you have?
Hint: northern U.S. lawns are usually cool-season; southern lawns are usually warm-season. This sets your cut height more than anything else.
2. How fast is the grass growing right now?
If you're cutting noticeably more than a third off every week, it's peak growth. If the lawn looks the same for two weeks, it's slow.
3. Do you mulch or bag your clippings?

Cool-season grass grows hardest in spring and fall (mow often) and stalls in summer heat (raise the height an inch, mow less). Warm-season grass does the opposite — it peaks through summer. In drought or dormancy, raise the deck and back off. For cool-season lawns, the last fall mow can go slightly shorter to deter snow mold.

    How to mow your lawn

    A mow is 20–60 minutes on an average yard, most of it walking. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ A mower is the most dangerous tool in the yard — treat it like one

    • Never reach under the deck or clear a clog with the engine running or the battery in. Shut it off, wait for the blade to fully stop, and pull the spark-plug wire or remove the battery first. Mower blades spin fast enough to take fingers.
    • Walk the lawn first and clear it — sticks, rocks, toys, and hidden hoses become high-speed projectiles. Keep kids and pets indoors while you mow.
    • Wear closed-toe shoes, eye protection, and ear protection. No sandals, ever. A discharged stone or a slip near the deck is how people lose toes.
    • On slopes, mow across the hill with a walk-behind (so it can't roll back onto you) — but up and down with a riding mower (so it can't tip sideways).
    • Let a hot muffler and engine cool before refueling or storing, and never refuel a running or hot mower.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Mower with an adjustable-height deck String trimmer for edges Safety glasses Ear protection Closed-toe, sturdy shoes A sharp blade (sharpened once a season)

    📐 Five techniques worth knowing

    Set the deck for your grass

    Match the cut height to your grass type, and mow on the higher end in heat or drought. Most people cut far too short — when in doubt, raise the deck a notch. Taller grass means deeper roots.

    Mow dry — and only a third

    Mow when the grass is dry: wet grass clumps, clogs the deck, tears unevenly, and spreads disease. And keep to the one-third rule. If it got tall, step it down over two or three mows.

    Change direction each mow

    Mow the same lines every time and the wheels carve ruts while the grass leans into a permanent "grain." Rotate the pattern — north-south, then east-west, then a diagonal — so blades stand up straight.

    Mulch the clippings back in

    Leave short clippings on the lawn ("grasscycling"). They break down within days and return roughly a quarter of the lawn's nitrogen for free. They don't cause thatch — only over-fertilized, scalped lawns do.

    Keep the blade sharp

    A sharp blade slices each tip clean; a dull one bruises and tears it, leaving a brown-white haze over the whole lawn a day or two after you mow. Sharpen about once a season — or sooner if the lawn looks ragged.

    Signs something's off — and when to call a pro

    • Ragged, brown or whitish tips a day after mowing. That's a dull blade tearing the grass instead of slicing it. Sharpen the blade — it's a fast, cheap fix that transforms the cut.
    • Ruts, tracks, or a worn "striped" look in the same lines. You're mowing the same direction every time, or mowing on wet, soft ground. Rotate your pattern and wait for the grass to dry.
    • Brown patches right after you cut. Usually scalping — the deck is set too low for the season. Raise it, and step shaggy grass down over several mows instead of one.
    • Clumps of wet clippings smothering the lawn. Mowing wet leaves mats that block light. Let it dry, or bag it when you can't wait.
    • A large, steep, or complicated lot. Big acreage or steep banks are slow, sweaty, and a real tip-over and projectile risk. A mowing service (about $35–60 a cut) is often worth it for size or slope alone.

    Common questions

    How short should I cut my lawn?

    Match your grass type and never break the one-third rule. Cool-season grasses (tall fescue, ryegrass, Kentucky bluegrass) want roughly 3–4 inches; warm-season bermuda likes 1–2.5 inches, zoysia 1–2 inches; St. Augustine wants 2.5–4 inches. Mow toward the high end of the range during heat or drought. Whatever your target, set the deck so you're only taking the top third off — if that means mowing more often, mow more often.

    How often should I really mow?

    As often as the grass needs it to stay inside the one-third rule — not on a fixed weekly schedule. In peak growth (cool-season spring and fall, warm-season summer) that's usually about weekly, sometimes every five days. In slow stretches it drops to every 10–14 days. In summer heat, drought, or dormancy, mow less and raise the height. The lawn tells you when; the calendar just reminds you to check.

    Should I mulch or bag the clippings?

    Mulch, almost always. Short clippings break down within days and return about a quarter of your lawn's nitrogen needs — a free feeding every mow — and they don't cause thatch. Bag only when the grass got too long and the clippings come off in heavy clumps that would smother the lawn, or when there's a fungal disease you don't want to spread. Otherwise let them fall.

    Can I mow wet grass?

    Avoid it. Wet grass clumps and clogs the deck, the blade tears rather than slices, the cut comes out uneven, and you can spread fungal disease while you walk. Soft, wet soil also ruts and compacts under the wheels. Wait until the dew has burned off or the lawn has dried after rain. If you absolutely must, bag the clippings and clean the deck after.

    When do I stop mowing for winter?

    Keep mowing as long as the grass is actively growing. For cool-season lawns that's often into late fall; once growth truly stops with the cold, you're done for the year. A common move is to make the last fall cut for cool-season grass slightly shorter than usual — it helps the lawn shed moisture and deters snow mold over winter. Warm-season grass simply goes dormant and brown; stop mowing once it stops growing.

    What about the very first mow of spring?

    Wait until the grass is actively growing and the ground has firmed up — mowing soggy spring soil leaves ruts. Make sure the blade is sharp first (spring is the ideal time to sharpen). If the lawn is taller than you'd like, still follow the one-third rule and bring it down gradually over a couple of mows rather than scalping it in one pass.

    Mowing follows the grass — not a tidy weekly reminder.

    Weekly in spring, less in the summer heat, paused in drought, plus a blade to sharpen once a season — the right mowing rhythm shifts all year, which is exactly why a rigid calendar reminder gets ignored.
