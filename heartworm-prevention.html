Keep Your Pet on Heartworm Prevention

Heartworm is the rare pet disease where the prevention is almost trivial and the disease is a nightmare: a single mosquito bite can plant a parasite that grows into foot-long worms living in your dog's heart and lungs, and treating it is grueling, costly, and dangerous — while for cats there's no approved treatment at all. The good news is that a tiny monthly dose, given year-round, stops it cold. The whole game is consistency: the same day, every month, without a gap. This guide explains how that works and why an annual test goes with it — the product and the schedule, though, are your veterinarian's call, since these are prescription medications.

Why it matters

Heartworm is one of those risks that feels abstract until it isn't — a quiet mosquito-borne parasite that gives no warning until the worms are already established in the heart and lungs. The reason vets are so insistent about prevention is that, on this one disease, the gap between "easy to prevent" and "agonizing to treat" is enormous. Here's what keeping your pet on prevention actually buys you (and your vet sets the exact product and schedule):

One mosquito bite is all it takes

Heartworm spreads when a single infected mosquito bites your pet, depositing larvae that migrate to the heart and lungs and grow into worms up to a foot long. It's deadly, brutal to treat, and gives no early warning — which is why prevention isn't an add-on. With heartworm, prevention is the whole game.

A deadly, mosquito-borne parasite

It works monthly — and backward

A preventive isn't a forcefield; it works by clearing out the larvae your pet picked up over the previous month. That "looking backward" design is why the calendar matters so much: each dose only covers the weeks behind it, so the same day every month — with no skipped or late doses — is exactly what keeps the chain unbroken.

A gap leaves larvae to mature

An annual test is required

Vets won't refill prevention without a yearly heartworm test, and it's not bureaucracy. The test confirms your pet is negative before dosing — because giving preventives to an already-infected dog can be dangerous — and it catches any silent infection from a missed or late dose early, while it's still far easier to treat.

Confirms negative · catches gaps

Treatment is grueling and costly

If prevention lapses and a dog tests positive, treatment often runs $1,000–$4,000 and means months of painful injections and strict crate rest, with real risk to the animal. For cats it's worse: there is no approved heartworm treatment at all — only prevention. The cheap monthly dose is the only sure way out.

$1,000–$4,000 · no cat cure

How often does your pet need it?

The honest answer is the same for nearly everyone: monthly, and — under modern guidance — year-round, even where winters are cold. Heartworm preventives are prescription, so your vet picks the product and confirms the testing; this quiz just builds the case for the cadence to bring to that conversation. Answer four quick questions:

1. Dog or cat?
Both dogs and cats need prevention, but the products differ and a dog's product can be dangerous for a cat — and remember there's no treatment for cats, so for them prevention is the only protection there is.
2. What are your winters like?
It's tempting to stop dosing once it frosts, but mosquitoes survive indoors and in warm spells, winters are unpredictable, and a year-round habit is far easier to keep than a seasonal one — which is why vets now recommend it everywhere.
3. Where do they stand right now?
A pet that's been steadily dosed and recently tested can simply continue, but a lapsed, newly adopted, or unknown-history pet needs a vet heartworm test before (re)starting — restarting blind can be unsafe.
4. What's their exposure like?
More time outside, travel to warmer regions, and a mosquito-heavy neighborhood all raise the odds of a bite — but even mostly-indoor pets get bitten, so the recommendation doesn't actually change: still monthly, still year-round.

    How to keep your pet on heartworm prevention

    The hard part isn't the dose — it's never missing it. The routine is simple: test first, choose the right product with your vet, give it on a set day every month (ideally year-round), and keep a record so the annual test and the next dose never slip. Read the safety notes and techniques first, then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Your vet decides — this is general info, not veterinary advice

    • Heartworm preventives are prescription. Your veterinarian picks the right product and dose by species and weight — and what's safe for a dog can harm a cat (and vice versa). Never give one pet's product to another, and never guess at the dose for a growing puppy or a pet that's changed weight.
    • A negative heartworm test is required before starting or restarting. Giving preventives to a dog that's already infected can trigger a dangerous reaction. That's why your vet tests first — and tests again if there's ever been a lapse — before putting your pet (back) on prevention.
    • If you miss a dose, don't panic — but do call. Give the missed dose as soon as you remember, then call your vet about the catch-up and a follow-up heartworm test (often about six months out, since that's the protection gap a late dose can open).
    • Many products cover more than heartworm. Plenty of preventives also handle fleas, ticks, or intestinal worms. Coordinate with your flea & tick treatment so you don't accidentally double up on the same active ingredient — let your vet sort out the combination.
    • Confirm it actually went down. A spat-out chewable is a missed dose. Watch your pet swallow it, give it with food per the label, and if they vomit shortly after, ask your vet whether to re-dose.
    • This page is general information, not veterinary advice. Product choice, dosing, and testing all depend on your individual pet — your veterinarian sets the plan.

    📐 Five things worth understanding

    Test first, every year

    Prevention starts with a heartworm test — annually, and again before you start or restart after any lapse. It confirms your pet is negative (dosing an infected dog can be dangerous) and catches a silent infection early, while it's far easier to treat.

    Choose the product with your vet

    Preventives come as a monthly chewable, a monthly topical, or a vet-given injectable that lasts 6 or 12 months. All are prescription and sized to species and weight — your vet picks the form that fits your pet and your routine.

    Give it on a set day, monthly

    Pick one day a month and protect it with a recurring reminder. Give the dose with food per the label, and actually watch your pet swallow it — a chewable spat out behind the couch is a missed month, and on this drug the calendar is everything.

    If you miss a dose

    Give the missed dose the moment you realize, then call your vet — a late dose can open a protection gap, so they'll often want a follow-up heartworm test in about six months to be sure nothing slipped through. Don't just skip ahead and hope.

    Coordinate & keep the record

    Many preventives also cover fleas, ticks, or intestinal worms, so check what your product includes and coordinate with the rest of your pet's parasite plan to avoid doubling up. Then log each dose and the annual test, so a missed month can't hide.

    When to check in with your vet

    Heartworm prevention runs in the background — until one of these moments makes it worth a call. In every case, your veterinarian is the decision-maker; this list is just what tends to trigger that conversation, since the product and the schedule are always theirs to set:

    • A missed dose or any lapse in coverage. One late or skipped month can open a window for larvae to mature. Give the dose, then call your vet — they'll usually want a catch-up plan and a follow-up heartworm test about six months out.
    • No recent heartworm test. If it's been more than a year, or you can't remember the last one, it's time. Vets won't refill prevention without it, and the test is what catches a silent infection before it becomes a crisis.
    • A newly adopted or unknown-history pet. A rescue, a stray, or any pet whose past dosing you can't confirm should be treated as untested. Your vet will test before starting prevention, because dosing an already-infected dog can be dangerous.
    • Signs of possible infection. A persistent cough, unusual lethargy, exercise intolerance, or weight loss in a dog warrant a vet visit — these can be late signs of heartworm. Cats may show coughing or asthma-like signs; for them, with no treatment available, early detection matters even more.
    • A move or a long trip to a warmer region. Relocating to or traveling through a high-mosquito, high-heartworm area changes the risk picture. Tell your vet so the prevention and testing plan keeps up with where your pet actually is.
    • A growing puppy or a weight change. Dosing is by weight, so a puppy outgrowing its bracket — or any pet that gains or loses significantly — may need a different size. Don't re-size on your own; let your vet confirm.
    • Pro tip: the annual heartworm test happens at the annual vet checkup, so the easiest way to never let prevention lapse is to bundle it with that visit — alongside the other recurring pet-health tasks like flea & tick treatment (often the very same combo product) and keeping vaccinations current.

    Common questions

    What is heartworm, and how do pets get it?

    Heartworm disease is caused by a parasitic worm (Dirofilaria immitis) spread by mosquitoes. When an infected mosquito bites your pet, it deposits microscopic larvae that migrate through the body over several months and mature into worms that can reach a foot in length, living in the heart, lungs, and associated blood vessels. There they damage the vessels and strain the heart, eventually causing coughing, fatigue, and — untreated — heart failure and death. Critically, your pet doesn't catch it from other animals; it always comes through a mosquito bite, which is why even a mostly-indoor pet is at some risk and why prevention, not avoidance, is the answer. The disease is found across nearly all of the U.S., and a single bite is all it takes, so vets treat year-round monthly prevention as the standard of care.

    Do indoor cats really need heartworm prevention?

    Many vets recommend it, yes. Mosquitoes get indoors, and studies have found that a meaningful share of cats diagnosed with heartworm were reportedly indoor-only. The stakes are also higher for cats for one stark reason: there is no approved treatment for heartworm in cats. In dogs, an infection can at least be treated (grueling and expensive as that is); in cats, the options are largely supportive care and hoping the worms don't cause a fatal reaction. Because prevention is the only real protection a cat has, and because the products are safe and inexpensive, the math leans heavily toward keeping even indoor cats on a monthly preventive. As always, your veterinarian makes the call for your specific cat — but "indoor" isn't the free pass it's often assumed to be.

    Do I really need it year-round, or just in mosquito season?

    The modern recommendation is year-round, everywhere — including cold-winter climates — and there are good reasons. Mosquitoes survive indoors and during warm spells, winters are increasingly unpredictable, and the parasite's timeline doesn't line up neatly with the calendar, so a "safe" off-season is hard to pin down. Just as important, year-round dosing is far easier to keep as a habit than stopping and restarting each year, which is exactly when doses get forgotten. Many year-round products also cover intestinal worms (and sometimes fleas), so there's a benefit even in months without mosquitoes. If you live somewhere with truly hard winters and want to discuss a seasonal approach, that's a conversation for your vet — but the default they'll almost always recommend is twelve doses a year, every year.

    Why do I need an annual test if I never miss a dose?

    It feels redundant, but there are real reasons, and most vets require it before refilling prevention. First, no preventive is 100% — a dose can be spat out, vomited up, or given a little late without you noticing, any of which can leave a gap. Second, heartworm preventives only kill the early larval stages; if an infection somehow established itself, continuing to dose an infected dog can be dangerous, so the test confirms you're on safe ground. Third, the test catches an infection early, while treatment is far more straightforward and less costly than it is once the disease has advanced. Think of the annual test as the safety check that makes the whole prevention strategy trustworthy — it's cheap, quick, and usually folded into the regular wellness visit.

    What happens if I miss a dose?

    Don't panic, but don't ignore it either. Give the missed dose as soon as you remember, and then call your veterinarian. Because preventives work "backward" — clearing out the larvae from the previous month — a late or skipped dose can let some larvae move past the stage the medication can catch, which is the gap that matters. Your vet will typically advise resuming the monthly schedule and scheduling a follow-up heartworm test about six months later, since it takes time for an infection to become detectable. A single late dose is usually fine when handled promptly; the danger is in repeated gaps or in quietly skipping ahead and assuming all is well. This is exactly the situation a monthly reminder is meant to prevent — and why so many people lean on an app to hold the date.

    Is there a treatment for cats — and does prevention cover other parasites?

    For cats, the hard truth first: there is no approved drug to kill heartworms in cats. The treatment used in dogs is unsafe for cats, so feline care is limited to managing symptoms and supportive care while hoping the infection resolves without a fatal complication. That's precisely why prevention is so heavily emphasized for cats — it's the only protection they have. For dogs, treatment exists but is grueling: a series of injections, strict crate rest for months, and a bill that often runs $1,000–$4,000, with real risk along the way. On the brighter side, many heartworm preventives are combination products that also cover intestinal worms (roundworms, hookworms) and sometimes fleas or ticks, so one monthly dose can handle several parasites at once. Just coordinate with your flea & tick treatment so you don't double up — and let your vet choose the product, since these are prescription medications.
