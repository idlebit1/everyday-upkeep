Run a Dishwasher Cleaner Cycle

Here's the thing nobody tells you about a dishwasher: it doesn't clean itself. Every load leaves a little grease, food film, and hard-water scale behind, and over the months that residue coats the tub, clogs the spray-arm jets, and turns into a musty biofilm that breeds in the filter and door seals. Then one day the dishes come out gritty, the glasses look cloudy, and there's a faint sour smell every time you open the door. The fix is a one-minute habit: pull and rinse the filter, then run a hot, empty cleaning cycle — a cleaner tablet, citric acid, or a cup of plain white vinegar — once a month. The grease lifts, the scale dissolves, the funk washes away, and the machine goes back to actually cleaning your dishes.

🗓️ Typically every month ⏱️ ~1 min of hands-on time ✨ Cleaner dishes, no smell 💸 Costs ~$0–1 a cycle

Why it matters

A dishwasher washes everything but itself. Every load sheds a little grease and food into the tub, every fill leaves a little mineral scale behind, and all of it slowly collects in the places water doesn't scour — the filter, the door seal, the corners, the spray-arm jets. Left alone, that buildup is what turns a workhorse appliance into one that smells, runs weak, and sends dishes back out dirty. Here's what it does:

Grease film coats everything

Fats and food residue build a sticky layer on the tub walls, the racks, and the inside of the door. Once that film is there it redeposits on your dishes mid-wash, so they come out feeling tacky or gritty — and the whole machine starts to smell faintly of old food.

Dishes come out dirty & greasy

Scale clogs the spray jets

Hard water leaves mineral deposits that slowly plug the little holes in the spray arms. With half the jets blocked the wash goes weak and patchy — water can't reach every dish — so loads come out unevenly cleaned and you start running things twice.

Weak, patchy washes

Biofilm makes it smell

Trapped food and standing moisture in the filter, the door gasket, and the corners grow a slimy biofilm — the source of that sour, musty, "wet-dog" odor. Open the door and the smell hits you, and it can transfer onto the dishes you thought were clean.

A sour, musty smell

A clean machine lasts longer

Grease and scale don't just dirty the tub — they strain the pump, clog the drain, and shorten the life of the parts. A machine cleaned on schedule washes better, drains freely, and dodges the clogs and breakdowns that lead to a repair bill or an early replacement.

Clogs, repairs & early death

How often should you run one?

Once a month is the sensible default for a cleaner cycle. But hard water that scales fast, a machine you run packed every day, a scrape-only habit that leaves more food behind, or a dishwasher that already smells all push toward monthly or sooner — while soft water and light use can stretch you to every two or three months. Answer four quick questions:

1. Do you have hard water?
Hard water leaves mineral scale that clogs the spray jets and clouds glasses fastest, so hard-water homes benefit most from a regular cleaner cycle (and a rinse aid in between).
2. How heavily do you run it?
Every load deposits a little grease and food in the tub and filter, so a dishwasher run daily and packed full builds up residue several times faster than one run a couple of times a week.
3. Do you rinse dishes before loading?
Scrape-only is fine for washing, but more food and grease end up in the filter and tub, so the machine itself needs cleaning a little more often than a pre-rinse household's does.
4. How's the machine right now?
A smell or filmy dishes means buildup has already taken hold — start with a cleaning cycle now and run them more often until it's back to fresh, then settle into a maintenance rhythm.

    How to run a dishwasher cleaner cycle

    It's two tasks, in order: first deal with the filter — pull it, rinse the gunk out under hot water, and clear the spray-arm holes — then run the actual cleaner cycle in an empty, hot machine. Do both and the machine comes out genuinely clean; skip the filter and the cleaner cycle is fighting the dirtiest part the whole time. Read the techniques first, then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Run it safe — empty, alone, and never mixed

    • Run the cleaner ALONE — empty machine, no detergent, no dishes. A cleaner cycle is meant to scour the tub, not wash a load. Detergent or dishes in there just blunt it and can give you a sudsy mess; pull everything out first, including the silverware basket.
    • Never mix vinegar (or any cleaner) with bleach. Combining acids or ammonia-based products with bleach releases toxic chlorine gas. Pick one cleaner per cycle — vinegar or a commercial cleaner or bleach — and never two at once.
    • Check your manual before using vinegar. A few manufacturers discourage vinegar on certain rubber seals and gaskets over time. If yours does, use a dishwasher cleaner tablet or citric acid instead — they do the same job without the worry.
    • It's a hot cycle — open the door carefully. A cleaning cycle runs at the hottest setting, so steam billows out when it finishes. Crack the door, let the steam clear for a minute, and keep little hands away before you reach in.
    • Don't pour cleaner directly on the heating element. Vinegar or cleaner splashed onto the exposed element at the bottom of the tub can bake on or pit it. Put vinegar in a cup on the top rack, and tablets or powder in the dispenser or on the tub floor — not on the element.
    • Wipe up standing water before you start. If there's water pooled in the bottom, that's a drain issue worth checking — don't just run more cycles on top of it. Clear the filter and sump first, and see a pro if it keeps coming back.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Pull & rinse the filter first

    Twist out the round filter at the bottom, rinse the trapped food and grease off under hot water (a little dish soap and an old toothbrush help), and poke any clogged spray-arm holes clear with a toothpick. This is the separate-but-essential filter-cleaning step — do it before the cycle.

    Run the hottest empty cycle

    Put a cleaner tablet or packet in the dispenser, or stand a cup of plain white vinegar upright on the top rack, then run the longest, hottest cycle with the machine otherwise empty — no detergent, no dishes. The heat and acid lift grease and dissolve scale together.

    Baking-soda rinse to deodorize

    After the cleaner cycle, sprinkle about a cup of baking soda across the floor of the tub and run a short, hot rinse. The soda neutralizes lingering odors and gently brightens the interior, so the machine comes out smelling genuinely fresh, not just acid-clean.

    Wipe the seal & the edges

    The spray never really reaches the door gasket, the rim, the bottom edge of the door, or the detergent dispenser — exactly where biofilm and old soap collect. Wipe them down with a damp cloth (an old toothbrush gets into the gasket folds) so the corners aren't undoing the cycle's work.

    Clear the jets & the drain

    Finish by checking that the spray-arm holes spin freely and aren't plugged — poke any stubborn ones clear — and that the drain and sump at the bottom are clear of debris. Free-flowing jets and a clear drain are what keep the next loads coming out clean and the water draining away.

    Signs it's overdue — and when to call a pro

    A healthy dishwasher is quietly invisible: dishes come out clean and dry, the door opens to fresh-smelling steam, and the water drains away with no fuss. These are the signals it's overdue for a cleaning — and the handful of problems that aren't a cleaning job at all and are worth an appliance tech:

    • Dishes still gritty, filmy, or spotty after a cycle. The first sign the machine itself needs cleaning. If a full cleaner cycle plus a fresh filter doesn't fix it, the issue may be the spray arms, the water temperature, or a worn part — worth a closer look.
    • A smell that won't quit. If you've run a cleaner cycle, scrubbed the filter, and wiped the gasket and it still smells, check the drain hose for a clog or a high loop issue — persistent odor often traces to water that isn't fully draining.
    • Standing water in the bottom after a cycle. A little is normal; an inch of dirty water that doesn't drain is a clog — in the filter, the sump, the drain hose, or the air gap. Clear the filter and sump first; if it keeps pooling, it's a drainage problem for a pro.
    • It won't drain, fill, or finish — or throws an error code. Error codes, a cycle that stalls, or the machine not heating point to a failed pump, valve, heating element, or control board. That's a repair, not a cleaning — note the code and call an appliance technician.
    • Leaks onto the floor or under the cabinet. Water escaping the door or pooling beneath the unit means a worn gasket, a cracked tub, or a loose hose connection. Catch it early before it damages the flooring or cabinetry — have a tech inspect it.
    • Grinding, screeching, or loud new noises. A healthy dishwasher hums. A grinding or screeching pump usually means debris in the impeller or a failing motor — stop running it and get it looked at before it seizes.
    • Pro tip: a monthly cleaner cycle and a clean filter prevent most service calls in the first place. If you do call a tech, mention the last time you cleaned it and ran a cleaner cycle — it helps them rule out simple buildup fast.

    Common questions

    A cleaner cycle vs. cleaning the filter — which do I need?

    Both, and they're different jobs. Cleaning the filter means physically pulling out the round basket at the bottom of the tub, rinsing the trapped food and grease off under hot water, and putting it back — that's the single biggest fix for a smelly machine or dishes that come out gritty, and it's worth doing every week or two. A cleaner cycle is the second half: running an empty, hot wash with a cleaner tablet or vinegar to scour grease and dissolve hard-water scale from the tub, the spray arms, and the corners the filter doesn't touch. Do the filter first, then run the cleaner cycle on top of it — neither one fully replaces the other, and together they keep the machine genuinely clean.

    Vinegar or a commercial dishwasher cleaner?

    Both work; it's mostly preference and what your manual allows. Plain white vinegar — a cup, upright, on the top rack — is nearly free and dissolves grease and light scale well, which is why it's the classic DIY choice. Commercial cleaners like Affresh or citric-acid packets cost about $0.50–1 a cycle and tend to cut heavy grease and stubborn mineral scale a little more aggressively, and they're formulated to be gasket-safe. The one real caution is that a few dishwasher makers discourage vinegar on certain rubber seals over time, so check your manual; if yours does, citric acid or a commercial cleaner gives you the same result without the worry. Either way, run it alone — empty, no detergent, no dishes — and never mix it with bleach.

    How often should I run a cleaner cycle?

    About once a month is the sensible default. Tighten toward monthly or even every couple of weeks if you have hard water that scales fast, run the machine daily and packed, scrape rather than rinse your dishes, or it already smells or films your dishes — all of those load the machine faster. You can stretch to every two or three months if you have soft water, run it lightly, and rinse dishes well, since less grease and scale accumulate. In between full cleaner cycles, the highest-value habit is rinsing the filter every week or two — that's where most of the funk lives. Wiping the gasket now and then and using a rinse aid in hard-water homes round it out.

    Why does my dishwasher smell?

    Almost always trapped food and standing moisture growing a biofilm — and the number-one place it hides is the filter at the bottom of the tub. Pull and rinse the filter first; that alone clears most odors. If it lingers, run a hot cleaner cycle with vinegar or a cleaner tablet, wipe down the door gasket and the bottom edge of the door (biofilm collects in the folds), and finish with a baking-soda rinse to neutralize what's left. A smell that survives all of that usually points to water not fully draining — check the drain hose for a clog or a missing high loop, since standing water in the line goes stagnant. Leaving the door cracked open between loads to let the tub dry out also keeps the funk from coming back.

    Why are my glasses coming out cloudy?

    There are two very different causes, and the test is simple: soak a cloudy glass in white vinegar for a few minutes. If it comes clear, the cloudiness is hard-water mineral film — fixable with a rinse aid, a cleaner cycle to descale the machine, and more frequent cleaning. If it stays cloudy, that's etching: tiny permanent pits in the glass caused by soft water plus too much detergent over many washes, and unfortunately it can't be reversed. To prevent further etching, use less detergent, skip the pre-rinse so the detergent has food to work on, and don't over-soften your water. So: cloudy-but-fixable means descale and use rinse aid; cloudy-and-permanent means adjust your detergent habits going forward.

    Should I pre-rinse dishes — and why is there water in the bottom?

    You don't need to pre-rinse; just scrape off the big chunks and load. Modern detergents and sensors are actually designed to have some food residue to grab onto, and a heavy pre-rinse can lead to over-etching and poorer cleaning. The trade-off is that scrape-only sends a bit more food into the filter, so plan to rinse the filter and run a cleaner cycle a little more often. As for water in the bottom: a small amount of clean water sitting in the sump is completely normal — it keeps the seals from drying out. What's not normal is an inch of dirty, smelly water that doesn't drain after a cycle; that's a clog in the filter, sump, drain hose, or air gap. Clear the filter and sump first, and if it keeps pooling, it's a drainage problem worth a pro's look. While you're at it, the same hard-water minerals are at work in your coffee maker and worth descaling, and a monthly kitchen deep clean is the natural time to fit all of it in.
