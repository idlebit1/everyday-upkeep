Replace Your Contact Lenses on Schedule

A contact lens looks the same on day one and day thirty — which is exactly the trap. Out of sight on the surface of your eye, proteins, oils, and debris slowly coat the lens, it lets through less oxygen, and microbes get a foothold. Wear one past its time and you're not just risking discomfort; you're sharply raising the odds of a serious eye infection — the kind that can scar the cornea and threaten your sight. The fix is refreshingly simple and not up for debate: follow the exact replacement schedule your eye doctor prescribed — daily, two-week, or monthly — and pair it with clean hands, fresh solution every time, and a clean case. Do that, and your eyes stay clear, comfortable, and safe.

🗓️ Daily, 2-week, or monthly 👁️ Protects against serious infection 💧 Fresh solution every time 💸 Far cheaper than an eye infection

Why it matters

A contact lens sits directly on the most exposed part of your eye, all day, every day you wear it. That's an intimate, demanding job for a thin sliver of plastic — and it's why lenses come with a hard replacement date that isn't a sales gimmick. Here's what an overworn lens is actually doing, and why the schedule earns its keep. This is general information, not medical advice — your eye doctor sets your schedule.

Deposits build up

Over days or weeks, proteins from your tears, natural oils, makeup, and airborne debris coat the lens. It grows cloudier, less comfortable, and a worse oxygen barrier — so the same lens you barely noticed at first starts to feel gritty, foggy, and dry by its last days.

Cloudy, gritty, dry by day's end

Overwearing invites infection

An overworn lens sharply raises the risk of serious eye infections and corneal ulcers — the kind that can scar the eye and threaten your sight. A starved, deposit-coated cornea is exactly the terrain those infections need. This is the reason the schedule exists.

Corneal ulcers, possible vision loss

"Stretching" lenses is false economy

Wearing a lens an extra week to save a few dollars feels thrifty, but an eye infection costs far more — an urgent visit, medicated drops, lost work, and the real risk of permanent damage — than a fresh box ever would. The cheap option is following the schedule.

An infection costs 10× a fresh box

Schedule and hygiene go together

A fresh lens handled with dirty hands, topped-off old solution, or stored in a grimy case still invites trouble. The replacement date and the hygiene around it are one routine — clean hands, fresh solution every time, and a clean case that you also replace on schedule.

Fresh lens, dirty habits = still risky

How often should you replace them?

There's no guessing here: your eye doctor prescribed a specific lens type with a specific schedule — daily disposables, two-week, or monthly — and that schedule is the answer. This quick check just confirms which one you're on and flags any habits worth tightening up. Whatever it says, your prescription rules — when in doubt, ask your optometrist.

1. What lens type did your eye doctor prescribe?
This is the one that sets your schedule. Daily disposables get tossed every night and never reused; two-week and monthly lenses are replaced on day 14 or day 30, counted from when you first wore each lens.
2. Be honest about your wear habits
Stretching the schedule or closing your eyes on a lens — even a quick nap — are exactly the habits that raise infection risk. If this is you, it's worth tightening up and mentioning it to your eye doctor.
3. How do you handle solution and the case?
Solution is single-use: it disinfects once, then it's spent. Topping off old solution and keeping a tired, biofilm-coated case are quiet ways to undo all the good of fresh lenses.
4. How do your eyes feel by the end of the day?
Dryness, redness, or irritation can mean lenses are being overworn, the fit or material isn't ideal, or something needs attention. It's a flag to follow the schedule closely and check in with your eye doctor.

    How to stay on schedule (and the hygiene that goes with it)

    There are really two halves to this: replacing each lens exactly when your eye doctor said to, and handling lenses cleanly in between. Read the safety box first — it's the part that protects your sight — then work through the techniques and the checklist. Your progress saves automatically. And keep up the rest of your replace-on-schedule personal care while you're at it — your toothbrush and makeup brushes (both hygiene items people stretch too long).

    ⚠️ Your eyes first — defer to your eye doctor, and respect infection risk

    • This is general information, not medical or eye-care advice. Follow the exact replacement schedule and wear time your eye doctor prescribed, and see them for your fit and prescription. Their instructions outrank anything here.
    • Never overwear lenses past their schedule. "It still feels fine" is exactly how an overworn lens sneaks up on you — the deposits and oxygen loss are happening whether or not you feel them yet. Replace on time, every time.
    • Never sleep or nap in lenses unless they're specifically prescribed for overnight wear. Closed-eye wear is a major infection risk — your cornea gets even less oxygen and trapped microbes multiply. Take them out before bed (and before a nap).
    • Always wash and dry your hands before touching lenses. Use soap and water and dry on a lint-free towel. Whatever's on your fingers goes straight onto the lens and into your eye.
    • Use fresh solution every single time — never top off old solution. And never use water or saliva. Tap and well water can carry Acanthamoeba, a microbe that causes a rare but devastating, vision-threatening eye infection. Solution disinfects once; reusing it doesn't.
    • Don't shower, swim, or use a hot tub in your lenses. Water against a lens is the same Acanthamoeba risk. If lenses must stay in around water, wear tight goggles, and many eye doctors suggest dailies you can toss right after.
    • Replace the lens case about every 3 months. Rinse it with fresh solution (never water), and air-dry it upside down on a clean tissue between uses. A grimy case grows biofilm that re-contaminates clean lenses.
    • If your eye is red, painful, light-sensitive, watery, or your vision blurs — take the lens OUT and contact your eye doctor promptly. These can be early signs of infection, and early care protects your sight.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Know your schedule

    Daily disposables = a brand-new pair every day, never reused. Two-week and monthly lenses are replaced on day 14 or day 30, counted from the first time you wore each lens — not from when you opened the box. Whatever your eye doctor prescribed is the rule.

    Hands first

    Wash with soap and water, then dry on a lint-free towel before you touch a lens. Whatever is on your fingers ends up on the lens and in your eye, so clean, dry hands are the non-negotiable first step every single time.

    Clean and store right

    For reusable lenses, rub-and-rinse each lens with fresh solution, then store it in a clean case filled with fresh solution — never water, never topped-off old solution. Daily disposables skip all this: toss them every night and start fresh tomorrow.

    Replace the case

    Swap the lens case about every three months — even a clean-looking one grows biofilm. Between uses, rinse it with fresh solution and air-dry it upside down on a clean tissue, never sealed wet, so it isn't recontaminating your lenses each night.

    Track and reorder

    Mark the replacement day — an app, a calendar, or a note — and keep to it even when the lens "still feels fine." Reorder a fresh supply before you run out, so you're never stuck stretching an old lens because the new box hasn't arrived.

    Signs to see your eye doctor — promptly

    A healthy lens routine is quiet: comfortable eyes, clear vision, no second thoughts. These are the signals to stop, take the lens out, and get eye care — some of them urgently. Your optometrist or ophthalmologist sets the schedule and the plan throughout; this list is about knowing when to reach for them.

    • Redness, pain, or light sensitivity — take the lens out and seek eye care promptly. Along with excessive watering, blurry vision, or a feeling that something's stuck even after you've removed the lens, these can be early signs of a serious eye infection. Don't wait it out; early treatment protects your sight.
    • Vision that blurs or won't sharpen with the lens in. A lens that won't settle into clear vision, or vision that's hazier than usual, is worth a same-day call — especially paired with any redness or discomfort.
    • Ongoing dryness or discomfort by day's end. If your eyes are routinely dry, red, or tired in lenses, that's a flag. Your eye doctor may adjust your schedule, switch you to a different material, or suggest daily disposables.
    • You keep wanting to overwear them. If you're regularly tempted to stretch lenses past their date, tell your eye doctor — there may be a lens type or supply plan that fits your life better and keeps you safely on schedule.
    • It's time for a new prescription or fit check. Contact-lens prescriptions expire, and your eyes change. A current exam keeps your prescription, fit, and lens choice right — and is required to reorder.
    • You wear lenses around water and can't avoid it. If swimming, showering, or hot tubs in lenses is unavoidable, ask your eye doctor about goggles and daily disposables — water against a lens carries real infection risk.
    • Bottom line: your eye doctor sets the replacement schedule and wear time. When something feels off, defer to them — it's your sight, and it's not worth a guess.

    Common questions

    How often should I replace my contacts?

    Exactly as often as your eye doctor prescribed — the lens type tells you the schedule. Daily disposables are worn once and thrown away every night, never reused. Two-week lenses are replaced on day 14, and monthly lenses on day 30, counted from the first time you wear each lens, not from when the box was opened. Whatever your prescription says is the rule; this guide can't substitute for it. If you're not sure which type you have or what your schedule is, check the box or, better, ask your optometrist or ophthalmologist — it's a quick question and worth getting exactly right.

    What happens if I wear them too long?

    Two things stack up, and neither is visible at first. Deposits — proteins from your tears, oils, makeup, and debris — build a film on the lens that makes it cloudier, less comfortable, and a worse oxygen barrier. At the same time, the cornea is getting less oxygen than it needs, and microbes have more time to establish themselves. Together that sharply raises the risk of a serious eye infection or a corneal ulcer — the kind that can scar the eye and threaten your sight. The deposits and oxygen loss are happening whether or not the lens still "feels fine," which is exactly why the schedule isn't optional. Replace on time, every time.

    Can I sleep in my contacts?

    Not unless your eye doctor specifically prescribed lenses for overnight (extended) wear — and even then, follow their exact instructions. For ordinary daily-wear lenses, sleeping or even napping in them is a major infection risk. When your eyes are closed, the cornea gets even less oxygen, tears don't flush the surface, and any microbes trapped under the lens have a warm, still place to multiply. That combination is one of the biggest drivers of contact-related eye infections. The safe habit is simple: take your lenses out before bed and before naps, every time. If you keep falling asleep in them, tell your eye doctor — there may be a better option for you.

    Can I use water or top off old solution?

    No to both. Never rinse or store lenses in water — tap, bottled, distilled, or well water — and never use saliva. Water can carry Acanthamoeba, a microbe that causes a rare but devastating eye infection that's notoriously hard to treat and can threaten your sight. That's also why you shouldn't shower, swim, or use a hot tub in your lenses. And never "top off" the old solution in your case by adding a splash of fresh: solution disinfects once and is then spent, so a top-off leaves a weak, contaminated mix. Empty the case, refill it with fresh solution every single time, and let the case air-dry upside down between uses. Use only the contact-lens solution your eye doctor recommends.

    How often should I replace the lens case?

    About every three months, even if it looks clean. Cases quietly grow a biofilm — a slimy layer of microbes — on their inner surfaces that fresh solution alone won't fully remove, and that film can recontaminate clean lenses every night. Between uses, empty the case, rinse it with fresh solution (never water), and stand it upside down on a clean tissue to air-dry, rather than sealing it wet. Then swap in a brand-new case on a regular schedule; many lens boxes even include one. A fresh case is cheap insurance, and it's an easy thing to track right alongside your lens replacement day.

    Daily vs. two-week vs. monthly lenses — what's the difference?

    Mostly how long you wear each lens before replacing it, and how much care it needs in between. Daily disposables are worn once and thrown out the same night — no cleaning, no case, no solution, and the lowest day-to-day infection risk because every day starts with a sterile lens; they tend to cost the most per day. Two-week and monthly lenses are reusable: you rub-and-rinse them with fresh solution and store them in a clean case overnight, replacing them on day 14 or day 30. They cost less per day but add solution and case care to the routine. None of these is "better" in the abstract — the right choice depends on your eyes, your budget, and your habits, which is exactly the conversation to have with your eye doctor, who'll prescribe the type and the schedule that fit you.
