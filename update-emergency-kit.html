Update Your Emergency Kit

Building an emergency kit feels like the hard part — but a kit is really a subscription, not a purchase. Water goes stale, food and meds expire, batteries drain, kids outgrow the packed clothes. Once a year, rotate it all so the kit you reach for actually works.

🗓️ Full refresh once a year ⏱️ 45–60 min per refresh 💧 1 gal water / person / day 💸 Refresh ≈ $25–75 vs rebuild $150–300

Why it matters

Disasters don't schedule themselves, and a kit only counts if it works on the day nothing else does. These are the ways an untouched kit quietly stops being a kit:

No warning, no do-over

Earthquakes, wildfires, and grid failures arrive on their schedule, not yours. Ready.gov plans around being on your own for at least 72 hours — the kit is what makes that survivable instead of scary.

First 72 hours: you're it

Water & food expire

Self-filled water containers grow stale and risky within months; canned and packaged food creeps past its dates a few items at a time. An expired pantry in a backpack is dead weight.

Stale water = no water

Batteries die in the drawer

Alkaline cells self-discharge and leak; the flashlight and radio they power fail with them. A blackout is the worst possible time to learn your only light is a corroded tube.

Dark house, dead radio

Meds lose their punch

First aid supplies dry out and prescriptions, ointments, and even sunscreen lose potency past their dates. The med you'll need most in a crisis is the one most likely to have expired.

Weakest when needed most

Kids outgrow the kit

The spare clothes, shoes, and diapers you packed fit the kid you had then. A year is one or two full sizes for a small child — and formula and baby food churn even faster.

1–2 sizes per year

Documents & cash go stale

Insurance policies renew, phone numbers change, accounts move — and the emergency cash has a way of getting "borrowed." Stale copies cost you days when you're filing claims from a shelter.

Claims stall without them

How big should your kit be?

The core list is the same for everyone — water, food, flashlight, NOAA weather radio, first aid, meds, documents, cash. The amounts and the rotation pace depend on who's in the house and what your region throws at you. Answer five questions:

1. How many people share your home?
2. What's your region's headline hazard?
3. Any pets?
4. Kids in the house?
5. Daily prescriptions or medical devices in the household?

    📌 Make it stick: a yearly task with no anchor quietly stops happening. Pair the refresh with a date you already observe — the day you test smoke alarms, the start of your storm season, a birthday, or New Year's weekend.

    The cost of keeping it alive

    The expensive part is the first build. After that, a yearly refresh is mostly groceries and batteries — and it's the only thing standing between your kit and rebuilding from zero. Typical U.S. estimates:

    The refresh barely costs anything because most of it isn't spending — it's rotating. Food that's a year from expiring moves into the pantry and gets eaten; fresh cans take its place. Skip that loop for three or four years and the whole kit expires as a unit. And the real cost of a dead kit isn't the dollars — it's standing in a dark house during the event you bought it for.

    How to run the refresh

    Set aside an hour, empty the kit completely onto a table, and work through the rotation below. The checklist is the heart of this page — your progress saves automatically, so you can split it across an evening or two.

    ⚠️ Four rules before you start swapping things

    • Never store water in used milk or juice jugs — sugars and proteins can't be fully washed out and feed bacteria. Use commercially bottled water or food-grade containers.
    • Don't taste-test doubtful food. A can that's bulging, leaking, rusted through, or deeply dented at a seam goes in the trash, not in your mouth.
    • Dispose of expired meds properly — a pharmacy take-back box or drug take-back day, not the kitchen trash where kids and pets can reach them.
    • Empty everything, every time. The expired items are always the ones hiding at the bottom of the bag. A half-checked kit is how surprises survive for years.

    📐 The rotation table — what turns over, and how fast

    Water

    Self-filled containers get dumped, rinsed, and refilled on a schedule. Commercially bottled water stays sealed — just check the printed date and keep it cool and dark.

    Replace self-stored every 6–12 months

    Food

    Check every date. Anything within a year of expiring rotates into the kitchen to be eaten; fresh, no-cook, low-salt replacements go in. Confirm the manual can opener is still there.

    Check yearly · rotate, don't toss

    Batteries & electronics

    Actually switch on the flashlight and NOAA weather radio — don't just eyeball them. Swap tired batteries, top up the power bank, and keep spares sealed in original packaging, outside the devices.

    Test yearly · quick check every 6 months

    Meds & first aid

    Check dates on everything: prescriptions, pain relievers, ointments, sunscreen, repellent. Restock whatever migrated to the bathroom during the year. Pair prescription rotation with a refill so kit meds stay newest.

    Yearly · meds every 6 months if prescriptions

    Documents & cash

    Re-copy anything that changed: IDs, insurance policies, bank and medical info, the family contact card. Count the cash — small bills, since card readers and ATMs go down with the power.

    Update copies yearly · after any change

    Clothing & sizes

    One full change of clothes per person, sturdy shoes, and warm layers — in sizes that fit now, suited to the season ahead. Kids' sets usually need swapping every refresh; infants' far more often.

    When to do a full rebuild instead

    Sometimes the right move isn't a refresh — it's emptying the bag and starting over. Rebuild from scratch when:

    • You used the kit in a real event. Restock everything immediately afterward, not at the next annual date — half-used kits are the ones that fail twice.
    • The kit is 5+ years old and was never refreshed. At that point assume everything perishable is gone; auditing item by item takes longer than restarting from the core list.
    • You moved to a different hazard region. A Minnesota winter kit and a Gulf Coast hurricane kit overlap maybe halfway. New region, new plan, new contents.
    • The household changed shape: a new baby, a new pet, someone with new medical needs, or an elderly parent moving in all change what 72 hours of self-sufficiency requires.
    • The storage spot failed: water damage, pests, or a summer in a hot garage or car trunk (heat ages food, water containers, and batteries fast). Fix the spot, then rebuild what it spoiled.
    • You bought a pre-made kit and never opened it. Unbox it, learn what's inside, and add what they never include: your meds, your documents, water beyond a token amount, and cash.

    Common questions

    How much water do we actually need?

    The standard guidance (FEMA/Ready.gov and the Red Cross) is one gallon per person per day — half for drinking, half for cooking and basic hygiene — for at least three days. The Red Cross recommends a two-week supply for sheltering at home. Add more for hot climates, children, nursing mothers, anyone ill, and a half-gallon or so per day for each pet.

    Go-bag vs. shelter-in-place kit — which one is this?

    Both, ideally. A go-bag is a portable 3-day kit you can grab in minutes when you must leave (wildfire, hurricane evacuation): water, food, meds, documents, cash, flashlight, radio, clothes. A shelter-in-place supply is the bigger, heavier stash for riding out an event at home — up to two weeks of water and food, plus sanitation. Most households keep one go-bag per adult and a home supply in a closet, and refresh both on the same annual date.

    Do I need a car kit too?

    If you drive regularly, yes — a small one. Jumper cables, a flashlight, a first aid kit, water, a few snack bars, a phone charger, and a blanket; add an ice scraper, sand or cat litter for traction, and extra warm layers in winter climates. Refresh it when you refresh the main kit, and remember car interiors run hot: food, water, and batteries stored there age fastest of all.

    What should I store water in?

    Easiest: unopened commercially bottled water, stored cool and dark. If you fill your own, use food-grade water containers (the blue jerry-can style) or thoroughly washed 2-liter soda bottles — never milk or juice jugs, which can't be fully cleaned of sugars and proteins. Sanitize containers, fill from a treated municipal tap, label the date, and replace every six months to a year.

    Does bottled water really expire?

    Sealed, commercially bottled water doesn't become unsafe at its printed date — that date is about taste and packaging quality, not safety. Stored cool, dark, and away from chemicals (not next to the gas can), it keeps far longer. The practical rule: keep store-bought water within its date when convenient, and be strict about the 6–12 month loop only for water you bottled yourself.

    Where should the kit live?

    Somewhere cool, dry, dark, and reachable in under a minute — a hall closet or under a bed beats the attic, the damp basement corner, or the hot garage. Everyone in the house should know exactly where it is. If your headline hazard is earthquakes, keep sturdy shoes and a flashlight by each bed too; broken glass underfoot is the classic first injury.

    How much cash, and why small bills?

    A common target is $100–300 in a mix of fives, tens, and twenties, plus a roll of quarters. When the power's out, card readers and ATMs are out with it — and a store that's open may not be able to break a hundred. Count it at every refresh; emergency cash has a way of getting borrowed for pizza night.
