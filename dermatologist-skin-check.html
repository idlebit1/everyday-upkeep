Keep Up Your Skin Checks

Skin cancer is one of the most common cancers there is — and one of the most treatable when it's caught early, while a changing spot is still small and shallow. The catch is that it hides in plain sight: a new freckle, a mole that's quietly shifting shape, a rough patch that won't heal. The upkeep habit is two simple, gentle parts. Keep up a professional skin check on the schedule your doctor sets — a dermatologist looks over your whole skin, including the places you can't see — and do your own quick self-exam about once a month, using the ABCDE rule so you notice a new or changing spot between visits. Add everyday sun protection, the root-cause prevention, and you've built the whole habit. This is general information, not medical advice — your doctor decides what's right for you.

Why it matters

Skin cancer is the most common cancer there is — and, caught early, one of the most beatable. The whole game is noticing change early, while a spot is still small and shallow. A professional check finds what you can't, your own eyes cover the months between, and sun protection stops the problem at its source. Here's why the habit is worth keeping up — this is general information, not medical advice:

Early = highly treatable

Melanoma and the other skin cancers, found early, are usually very curable — small procedures, excellent odds. Missed and left to grow, they turn dangerous. That gap between "caught early" and "caught late" is exactly what regular checks close, which is why they genuinely save lives.

Dangerous if it's missed

Changes are the signal

It's rarely a spot that was always there — it's the new one, or the mole that's quietly changing in size, shape, or color. That change is the alarm bell. A professional's trained eye and your own regular look are what catch it while it's early and still easy to treat.

A new or changing spot

You can't see all your skin

Your back, your scalp, the soles of your feet, behind your ears — there's a lot of you that you simply can't inspect. A dermatologist checks those blind spots at a full-body exam, and your monthly self-exam (with a mirror or a partner) covers everywhere else.

Blind spots go unwatched

Prevention is the other half

Most skin cancer traces back to UV — the sun and tanning beds. So sun protection isn't separate from this habit, it's the root-cause maintenance: daily broad-spectrum sunscreen, shade, clothing, and a hat mean fewer worrying spots to find in the first place.

UV is the root cause

How often should you get checked?

There's no single answer — it depends on your risk factors and history, and your doctor decides. As general guidance, a professional skin check about once a year is common, more often for people with more risk factors or a history of skin cancer, and some lower-risk people need it less often. Answer four quick questions for a starting point to talk over with your doctor — and remember, a spot you're worried about means go now, not at your next routine visit.

1. Do you have skin-cancer risk factors?
Fair skin, lots of moles, a history of blistering sunburns or tanning-bed use, and a personal or family history of skin cancer all raise risk — and generally push toward more frequent professional checks. Your doctor weighs these for you.
2. How much sun do you get?
UV exposure is the main driver of skin cancer, so heavy lifelong sun — an outdoor job, a sunny place, lots of time outside — adds up. It's also the part you can do the most about, with everyday sun protection.
3. Is there a spot you're worried about right now?
This one overrides everything else. A new, changing, itching, bleeding, or non-healing spot isn't a "schedule it sometime" situation — it's a reason to see a doctor promptly, whatever your routine interval is.
4. When was your last professional skin check?
If it's been a long time — or you've never had a full-body skin exam — a first check is a reasonable thing to bring up with your doctor, especially if you have risk factors. It sets a baseline your future checks compare against.

    The cost of skipping it

    A professional skin check is one of the cheaper things a specialist does — often around $100–200 out of pocket if you're paying, and frequently covered by insurance, especially with risk factors or a referral. Your own self-exam is completely free. Set that against a skin cancer caught late, which is harder, costlier, and far more serious to treat. Coverage varies, so check your plan:

    How to keep up your skin checks

    The habit has two halves: keep up professional checks on your doctor's schedule, and do your own self-exam about monthly using the ABCDE rule, so you catch a new or changing spot between visits. Read the safety note and the ABCDE guide first, then work through the checklist below. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Not medical advice — your doctor decides · plus the ABCDE self-exam

    • How often YOU need a professional check depends on you — your doctor decides. It's set by your risk factors and history, not a one-size rule. A yearly exam is common when you have risk factors; lower-risk people may need one less often. Any general ranges here are just a starting point for that conversation.
    • Do a self-exam about monthly, in good light, with a mirror (or a partner's help). Check everywhere — including the scalp, ears, between fingers and toes, the soles of your feet, and your back. A full-length and a hand mirror, or someone to look at what you can't, covers the spots you'd otherwise miss.
    • Watch for the ABCDE warning signs of melanoma: A — Asymmetry (one half unlike the other); B — Border (irregular, ragged, or blurred); C — Color (varied, or several colors in one spot); D — Diameter (larger than a pencil eraser, about 6 mm — though smaller can matter too); E — Evolving (any change in size, shape, or color, or a spot that itches, bleeds, or won't heal).
    • Trust the "ugly duckling." A spot that simply looks different from all your others is worth attention, even if it doesn't tick every ABCDE box. Your moles tend to resemble each other — the odd one out is the one to watch.
    • Take dated photos of anything you're tracking. A clear, dated photo of a mole makes "is this changing?" a real comparison next month instead of a guess. Note where each one is.
    • See a doctor promptly for anything worrying — don't wait for your routine appointment. A new spot, a changing one, or a sore that itches, bleeds, crusts, or won't heal is a reason to get in soon, not to wait months for your next scheduled check.
    • Prevent it at the source with everyday sun protection. Broad-spectrum sunscreen, shade, protective clothing, and a hat — and no tanning beds — cut your UV exposure, which is what causes most skin cancer in the first place.
    • This is general information, not medical advice. Nothing here diagnoses, treats, or replaces a professional. For anything about your own skin, your risk, or your schedule, defer to a doctor or dermatologist.

    📐 Five techniques worth knowing

    Keep up the professional check

    Book the full-body skin exam your doctor recommends, and keep it on the calendar — more often if you're higher-risk or have a history of skin cancer. The dermatologist checks everywhere, including the spots you can't see yourself. Your doctor decides how often.

    Do a monthly self-exam

    About once a month, in good light with a mirror (or a partner's help), look yourself over head to toe — scalp, ears, between fingers and toes, soles, and back included. It's free, quick, and it's what catches a new or changing spot between professional visits.

    Judge with ABCDE + ugly duckling

    Ask of any spot: is it Asymmetric, does the Border look irregular, is the Color varied, is the Diameter bigger than a pencil eraser, is it Evolving? And does it look different from your other moles — the "ugly duckling"? Any yes is worth a doctor's look.

    Photograph & track it

    For any spot you're watching, take a clear, dated photo and note where it is. Next month, comparing photos turns a hazy "is it different?" into an actual side-by-side — and gives your doctor something concrete if you do go in.

    Prevent — and act promptly

    Cut the root cause with everyday sun protection: broad-spectrum sunscreen, shade, clothing, a hat, and no tanning beds. And if a spot is new, changing, itching, bleeding, or won't heal, don't wait for your routine visit — see a doctor promptly.

    Signs to see a doctor promptly — don't wait

    Most spots are harmless, and this isn't a diagnosis — it's a general list of the changes that are worth a doctor's look soon, rather than at your next routine visit. When in doubt, get it checked; that's exactly how early, treatable skin cancers get caught. This is general information, not medical advice:

    • A brand-new mole or spot, especially one that appears in adulthood or looks different from all your others (the "ugly duckling"). A new spot that stands out is worth a prompt look.
    • A mole that's changing — growing, or shifting in shape or color. Any of the ABCDE signs (asymmetry, an irregular border, varied color, a diameter bigger than a pencil eraser, or evolving over weeks) is a reason to get in.
    • A spot that itches, bleeds, crusts, or won't heal. A sore that keeps scabbing and reopening, or a spot that itches or bleeds without an obvious cause, deserves attention — don't wait for it to "settle down."
    • Anything you're anxious about. If a spot is worrying you enough that you keep checking it, that's reason enough to have it looked at. Peace of mind is part of the point.
    • Keep up professional checks if you're higher-risk. A personal or family history of skin cancer, fair skin with lots of moles, or heavy past sun exposure all mean staying on the schedule your doctor sets matters more, not less.
    • When to go NOW rather than schedule: a spot that's rapidly changing, bleeding, or clearly new and unusual isn't a "next available" situation — call and ask to be seen soon, and say what you're seeing.

    Common questions

    How often should I get a skin check?

    There's no single right answer — it depends on your risk factors and history, and your doctor decides. As general guidance, a professional full-body skin exam about once a year is common, and people with more risk factors (fair skin, lots of moles, past sunburns or tanning-bed use, or a personal or family history of skin cancer) or an existing history of skin cancer are often checked more frequently. Some lower-risk people may need it less often. The honest answer is to ask your doctor or dermatologist what makes sense for you, and treat any range you read — here or anywhere — as a starting point for that conversation, not a prescription. This is general information, not medical advice.

    What is the ABCDE rule?

    ABCDE is a simple way to remember the warning signs of melanoma when you look at a mole or spot. A is for Asymmetry — one half doesn't match the other. B is for Border — the edges are irregular, ragged, notched, or blurred. C is for Color — the color varies, or there are several colors (brown, black, red, white, blue) in one spot. D is for Diameter — it's larger than a pencil eraser, about 6 mm across, though smaller spots can still matter. E is for Evolving — it's changing in size, shape, or color over time, or it's started to itch, bleed, or crust. It pairs well with the "ugly duckling" idea: a spot that simply looks different from your others. None of these confirms anything on its own — they're a prompt to have a spot checked. This is general information, not medical advice.

    How do I do a self-exam?

    Give yourself about ten minutes in a well-lit room, roughly once a month, when you're already undressed — after a shower is easy. Look over your whole body head to toe: face, ears, and neck; your chest and belly; arms, including the undersides and between your fingers; hands and nails; legs, the tops and soles of your feet, and between your toes. Use a full-length mirror for the front, and a hand mirror (or a partner) for your back, the backs of your legs, your buttocks, and your scalp — part your hair to see it. The point isn't to diagnose anything; it's to get familiar with your own skin so you'd notice a new spot or a change. Photograph anything you want to track, and see a doctor for anything that worries you. This is general information, not medical advice.

    What does a dermatologist skin check involve?

    A professional skin check is usually quick, painless, and non-invasive. You'll change into a gown, and the dermatologist examines your skin from head to toe — scalp, face, ears, torso, arms, legs, hands, feet, and the spots you can't easily see yourself. They often use a dermatoscope, a small lighted magnifier, to look closely at any spot of interest. If something looks worth a closer look, they may photograph it to track over time or, occasionally, take a small sample (a biopsy) for the lab — a minor in-office procedure. Most visits end with reassurance and, if needed, a plan for anything they want to watch. Exactly what happens is up to your clinician. This is general information, not medical advice.

    Is skin cancer serious? Is it treatable?

    Skin cancer is serious — melanoma in particular can be dangerous if it's not caught in time — but it's also one of the most treatable cancers when it's found early. Caught early, while a spot is small and shallow, skin cancers including melanoma are usually very curable, often with a straightforward procedure. The outlook gets worse the longer a cancer goes unnoticed and has time to grow or spread, which is exactly why early detection matters so much and why regular professional checks and monthly self-exams are worth the small effort. But how serious any particular spot is, and how it should be treated, is a question only a doctor can answer for you. This is general information, not medical advice.

    How do I lower my risk?

    Because most skin cancer comes from UV exposure, sun protection is the biggest lever you have. Use a broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or higher and reapply it (keep your sunscreen and daily face SPF fresh, since they lose effectiveness past their date); seek shade, especially in the middle of the day; wear protective clothing, sunglasses, and a wide-brim hat; and avoid tanning beds entirely — they're a known cause of skin cancer, not a "safe" tan. Being extra careful to protect kids from sunburn matters too, since childhood sun exposure adds up over a lifetime. Alongside prevention, keep up your self-exams and professional checks so anything that does appear is caught early. This is general information, not medical advice — talk to your doctor about your own risk.
