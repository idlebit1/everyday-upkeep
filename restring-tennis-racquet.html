Restring Your Tennis Racquet

Here's the thing almost nobody tells recreational players: your strings are dying long before one of them snaps. From the day a racquet is strung, the strings begin to soften and lose tension — a little every week — so the sweet spot goes mushy, the ball flies unpredictably, and, worst of all, the racquet transmits more shock straight into your wrist, elbow, and shoulder. Dead strings are a quiet, well-documented contributor to tennis elbow. There's a classic rule that keeps you honest: restring about as many times a year as you play per week. Play three times a week? Restring roughly three times a year — and always the moment a string breaks, notches deep, shifts out of place, or just feels flat and lifeless.

Why it matters

Most people think of strings as a break-only problem: play until one snaps, then get it restrung. But a tennis string is a spring, and springs wear out even when they never fail outright. Long before anything breaks, the string bed goes soft — and that softness costs you in ways that show up in your game and, eventually, in your arm. Here's what dead strings actually do:

Strings lose tension & go dead

From the day they're installed, strings slowly soften and lose tension — a little every week, even sitting in the bag unused. The crisp, springy response fades into something mushy and dull, so the racquet feels unpredictable long before a single string snaps.

A soft, lifeless string bed

You lose control & power

Worn, notched, and shifted strings don't grip and rebound the ball the way fresh ones do. The response gets inconsistent — the same swing gives you different depth and spin shot to shot — so control drifts away and you start over-hitting to make up for it.

Erratic depth, spin & control

It's an arm issue, too

Low-tension, dead strings absorb less of the ball's impact, so more shock travels into your wrist, elbow, and shoulder. That extra jolt, repeated thousands of times, is a real contributor to tennis elbow — fresh, appropriately soft strings are gentler on the joint.

Extra shock → tennis elbow

The rule keeps you honest

Because strings die on a schedule whether or not they break, the classic rule of thumb ties restrings to how much you play: restring per year about as often as you play per week. It turns a vague "when it snaps" habit into a real maintenance rhythm.

Playing on strings months past their prime

How often should you restring?

The classic rule is simple: restring as many times per year as you play per week — play three times a week and you're due for roughly three restrings a year. About every six months is the sensible default for a regular player. But your string type, how much you play, the current state of the bed, and your arm all shift that. Answer four quick questions:

1. How often do you play?
This is the heart of the rule: restrings per year should roughly match plays per week. The more sessions you log, the faster the strings wear and lose tension — and the more restrings you'll want on the calendar.
2. What kind of string is in the racquet?
Natural gut and multifilament feel wonderful but lose tension faster, so they want refreshing sooner. Polyester is durable but it also goes dead — often while looking perfectly fine — so poly players especially can be fooled into playing on stale strings.
3. What shape is the string bed in right now?
A broken or fraying string, deep notching where the strings cross, or mains that have slid out of their grid all mean restring now. A dead, mushy feel counts too — even an unbroken bed can be well past its prime.
4. Any arm sensitivity or elbow trouble?
If your arm is a concern, don't let dead strings linger — they transmit the most shock. Fresh strings, a softer string type, and a slightly lower tension are all easier on the elbow, so a sensitive arm favors restringing sooner and choosing gently.

    The cost of skipping it

    A restring is one of the cheapest tune-ups in sport: roughly $15–40 in labor plus about $5–30 for the string itself (natural gut sits at the pricey end), so call it around $20–60 all-in each time.     How to keep your racquet freshly strung

    Restringing itself is a machine job — precise, even tension across the whole bed — so the "how-to" for almost everyone is really knowing when and getting it done right: learn the rule, read the strings, hand it to a stringer, match the string and tension to your game and arm, and track the date. Read the notes first (especially the machine and whole-racquet points), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Leave it to a stringing machine — and mind your arm

    • This is a job for a stringing machine. A shop, club, or home stringing machine pulls each string to a precise, even tension and clamps it in place. Don't try to hand-tie or "repair" a broken string — uneven tension warps the string bed, plays badly, and can even stress the frame.
    • One string breaks? Restring the whole racquet. Don't just replace the string that snapped. The rest are worn and stretched, and a single fresh string among old ones makes the tension uneven. A full restring is the only way to get a consistent, predictable bed back.
    • Match the string and tension to your game — and your arm. Lower tension and softer strings (natural gut or multifilament) are gentler on the elbow; stiff polyester at high tension is the harshest setup there is. If you have any arm pain, ask a stringer or coach about a softer string and lower tension — and get a fresh restring, since dead strings make tennis elbow worse.
    • An old racquet from the closet is almost certainly due. Strings lose tension even sitting unused, so a racquet that's been in a bag or closet for a season or two is playing dead no matter how new the strings look. Restring before you take it out for real.
    • Keep it out of extreme heat. A hot car in summer can warp the frame and slacken the strings. Store the racquet indoors, out of direct heat, so it holds its tension and shape between sessions.
    • No machine? A shop does it fast and cheap. Unless you've invested in your own machine and learned to use it, hand your racquet to a stringer. Most restring quickly, often for a modest fee, and the even, accurate tension is well worth it.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you go

    Know the rule

    Restring per year about as often as you play per week — three sessions a week means roughly three restrings a year. It's a schedule you keep even when nothing's broken, because the strings die on their own timetable.

    Read the strings

    A broken or fraying string, deep notching where the strings cross, mains that have moved out of their grid, or just a dead, mushy feel — any of these means it's time. Don't wait for a snap; the feel usually fails first.

    Restring the whole racquet

    Take it to a stringer (or use a stringing machine) and replace all the strings, not just the one that broke. That's the only way to get an even, predictable bed back — a single fresh string among old ones plays worse, not better.

    Match string & tension

    Pick a string type and tension for your game and, above all, your arm — softer and lower is easier on the elbow, stiffer and higher gives control but more shock. Lean on your stringer or coach; they'll steer you to the right setup.

    Care & track

    Keep the racquet out of the heat, note the restring date, and reset your schedule by the play-per-week rule. A quick log is all it takes to catch the next restring before the strings quietly go dead on you again.

    Signs it's time — and when to see a stringer or coach

    A fresh string bed is crisp, lively, and predictable; a tired one lets you down in ways you can feel long before it fails. These are the signals to restring now — and the arm situations worth a conversation with a stringer or coach:

    • A broken or fraying string. The obvious one — but treat it as a signal to restring the whole racquet, not to patch the single string. The rest are worn and the tension is now uneven.
    • Deep notching where the strings cross. Run a finger over the bed; if the mains and crosses have sawed grooves into each other, the strings are near the end and about to break — restring before they do.
    • Strings shifting out of place. If you're constantly straightening mains that slide sideways, the string has lost its grip and its tension. That wandering bed plays inconsistently and it's telling you it's done.
    • A dead, mushy, or unpredictable feel. Even with nothing broken, if the racquet feels flat, the sweet spot has gone soft, or the same swing gives different results, the tension's gone. Trust the feel — it fails before the string does.
    • Lost control or power. Balls sailing long, dropping short, or refusing to bite for spin the way they used to often trace back to dead strings, not your technique. A fresh restring is the cheapest fix to try first.
    • Arm, wrist, or elbow pain. If you have arm or elbow soreness, ask a stringer or coach about a softer string and lower tension, and get a fresh restring — dead, stiff strings transmit the most shock and worsen tennis elbow. Persistent pain is worth a doctor or physio, too.
    • Pro tip: if you play enough to restring several times a year, buy string on a reel and build a relationship with one stringer. They'll dial in the string and tension that suit your game and your arm, and it's cheaper per restring than buying single sets. It's the same wear-out-before-they-break story as regripping your clubs — and worth keeping the rest of your sports gear fresh too, right down to your running shoes.

    Common questions

    How often should I restring my tennis racquet?

    About every six months is a sensible default for a regular recreational player, but the honest answer depends on how much you play. The classic rule of thumb is to restring as many times per year as you play per week — so if you play twice a week, aim for roughly two restrings a year; three times a week, about three; and so on. On top of that schedule, restring immediately whenever a string breaks, notches deeply, shifts out of place, or the bed simply feels dead and mushy. Frequent players and those using natural gut or multifilament will land at the more frequent end; occasional players on durable poly can stretch the interval, but shouldn't kid themselves that "unbroken" means "still good."

    What's the "play per week = restrings per year" rule?

    It's the simplest way tennis has to turn "restring when it feels off" into an actual schedule. Count how many times in a typical week you play, and restring that many times over the course of a year. Play once a week? Once a year is a reasonable minimum. Play three or four times a week? Three or four restrings a year. The logic is that strings lose tension steadily with use (and even a little just sitting), so more court time means faster decline. It's a floor, not a ceiling: if you play competitively, use tension-losing strings, or have a sensitive arm, restring even more often. And it always yields to the obvious signs — a break or a dead feel means restring now, regardless of what the calendar says.

    Do strings really go bad even if they don't break?

    Yes — this is the part most recreational players miss. A tennis string is under high tension, and from the moment it's installed it begins to relax and lose that tension, a little every week, whether you play on it or not. Polyester strings in particular can look perfectly intact while playing completely dead, because they lose their elasticity well before they snap. A dead bed feels mushy and unpredictable, gives you less control and spin, and — importantly — cushions the ball less, sending more shock into your arm. So "it hasn't broken yet" is not the same as "it's still good." If the racquet has been strung a long time, or has sat in a bag or closet for a season, it's very likely due even with every string intact.

    Can I just replace the one string that broke?

    No — restring the whole racquet. It's tempting to swap only the string that snapped, but the rest of the bed is worn and has already lost tension, so mixing one brand-new string in with old ones gives you an uneven, inconsistent bed that plays worse than before. Uneven tension can also stress the frame. A stringer will pull the whole set out and restring the racquet to a single, even tension, which is the only way to restore the crisp, predictable response you're after. Restringing everything also isn't much more work or money than a "one-string repair" would be — and it's a real machine job, not something to hand-tie yourself.

    Can dead strings actually cause tennis elbow?

    They're a recognized contributor, not the sole cause. Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis) comes from repetitive strain on the forearm tendons, and the shock your racquet transmits on every hit is part of that load. Fresh strings — especially softer, lower-tension setups — flex and absorb more of the ball's impact, cushioning your arm. Dead, low-tension strings absorb less, so more shock travels into your wrist, elbow, and shoulder with every stroke. Over thousands of hits that added jolt can push a vulnerable arm toward injury. If you have arm pain, restring promptly and talk to a stringer or coach about a softer string and lower tension; a multifilament or natural gut at a modest tension is one of the gentler setups. Persistent or severe pain also warrants a doctor or physio.

    What string and tension should I use?

    It depends on your game and your arm, which is exactly why a good stringer or coach is worth talking to. Broadly: natural gut and multifilament strings are soft, lively, and gentle on the arm but lose tension faster and cost more; polyester is durable and gives big spin and control for hard hitters but is stiff and harsher on the elbow, especially at high tension. Tension is a trade-off too — higher tension gives more control and less power (and more shock), lower tension gives more power and comfort. If you're arm-sensitive or fighting tennis elbow, lean toward a softer string and a lower tension. Rather than guess, tell your stringer how you play and how your arm feels; they'll recommend a setup and fine-tune it over a restring or two until it's right.
