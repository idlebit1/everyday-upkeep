Wash Your Pillows

You change the pillowcase, but the pillow underneath quietly soaks up everything the case can't stop — sweat, body oils, drool, dead skin — night after night. That's what yellows the fill, breeds dust mites, and gives an old pillow that musty smell. The good news: most pillows go right in the washing machine. The catch: memory foam and latex absolutely do not. Here's how to tell which is which, and how to wash and dry yours so they come out fresh and lump-free.

🗓️ Wash every ~3 months 🧺 Two at a time balances the drum ⚠️ Never machine-wash memory foam 💸 About $0–5 in supplies

Why it matters

A pillowcase is a thin first line of defense, and it only catches so much. Everything your head gives off all night soaks straight through it into the fill — where a wash of the case alone never reaches. Here's what builds up in a pillow you've never actually washed:

Sweat, oils, drool & skin

Adults give off moisture and skin oils all night, plus drool and dead skin cells. The case absorbs the surface of it; the rest wicks down into the fill, where it sits and accumulates wash after wash you only ever gave the case.

Saturated, smelly fill

Dust mites & allergens

Mites eat the skin cells you shed and thrive in the warm, slightly damp fill of an unwashed pillow. Their droppings are a leading year-round trigger for allergies and asthma — pressed right under your nose for eight hours a night.

3 a.m. congestion & flares

Yellowing & odor a case can't stop

Sweat and oils that soak past the case oxidize into yellow staining and a stubborn musty smell. No fabric spray and no fresh pillowcase fixes a saturated core — the only reset is washing the pillow itself.

Permanent yellow & musk

A pillow that lasts longer

Washing the fill clears the oils and residue that mat fibers and clump down clusters, so a clean pillow re-lofts and supports your neck longer. Skip it and the fill saturates years early — and you're buying replacements you didn't need to.

Replaced before its time

How often should you wash them?

Every 3 months is the sensible default for a washable pillow — but allergies, how much you sweat, and whether a zippered protector is doing some of the work all move the dial. And if your fill is memory foam or latex, the answer changes entirely. Answer four quick questions:

1. What's the fill? (Check the care tag if you're not sure.)
2. Do you have allergies or asthma?
3. How do you sleep, temperature-wise?
4. Is there a zippered protector under the pillowcase?

    How to wash your pillows

    Mostly machine time — the hands-on part is about ten minutes, plus a long, patient dry. Read the techniques first, then work the checklist; your progress saves automatically, so a slow-drying pillow can span the afternoon.

    ⚠️ Read this before anything goes in the machine

    • Check the care tag first — it outranks this guide. Look for "machine wash" versus "spot clean only." Most down, feather, and synthetic pillows are machine-washable.
    • Never machine-wash memory foam or latex. The agitation and water tear the foam apart from the inside and it can stay damp for days. Spot-clean the surface, wash only the cover, and air it out instead.
    • Wash two pillows at once. A balanced pair keeps the drum from thumping off-balance on the spin cycle, and they clean more evenly.
    • Use only a small amount of mild detergent. Excess soap is what mats and stiffens fill — less is genuinely more, and an extra rinse clears what's left.
    • Dry fully — a damp pillow grows mildew. A core that's even slightly damp can mildew within days, which ends the pillow. Low heat, dryer balls, and patience.

    📐 Five techniques worth knowing

    Check the tag before you commit

    "Machine wash" means down, feather, and most synthetic pillows are good to go. "Spot clean only" almost always means memory foam or latex — those never get submerged. The tag settles it; this guide doesn't override it.

    Two at a time, warm & gentle

    Load two pillows on opposite sides to keep the drum balanced. Warm water, gentle cycle, and only a small squirt of mild detergent — too much soap is what leaves fill stiff and matted.

    Run a second rinse

    Fill traps detergent deep inside, and trapped soap is what dries stiff and attracts grime. An extra rinse cycle flushes it out so the pillow comes back soft. A splash of white vinegar in the wash helps with stubborn odor.

    Dry low with dryer balls

    Low heat plus two or three wool dryer balls (or clean tennis balls) tumbles the fill so it dries evenly and re-fluffs instead of clumping. Expect several cycles; pull the pillow out and break up clumps by hand between each.

    Confirm it's dry to the core

    A pillow can feel dry on the surface while the center is still damp — and a damp core mildews fast. Squeeze the middle: any coolness, dampness, or clumping means run another low cycle before it goes back on the bed.

    When to replace instead of wash

    • It fails the fold test. Fold the pillow in half and let go. A live pillow springs back open on its own; one that stays folded (or doesn't bounce back) has dead fill, and no wash revives it. For memory foam and latex, press your palm into the center instead — slow, partial recovery means it's done. When that's the verdict, it's time to replace the pillow, not wash it.
    • Stains or odor survive a proper wash. Light yellowing that rinses out is normal aging. Deep, permanent yellowing or a musty smell that's still there after a full wash-and-dry means the fill is saturated past saving.
    • The fill clumps and won't redistribute. If you wash, dry with dryer balls, fluff by hand, and it still comes out lumpy with bare patches, the fibers have broken down — that's structural, not dirt.
    • It got put away damp and smells of mildew. A mildewed core is very hard to rescue. Replace it, and next time dry it bone-dry before it goes anywhere near the bed.

    Common questions

    How do I clean a memory foam (or latex) pillow if it can't go in the washer?

    Spot-clean and air it out. Mix a little mild detergent with water, dab any soiled spots with a cloth (don't soak the foam), and blot with a clean damp cloth to rinse. Press out excess moisture with a towel and let it air-dry completely, flat and away from direct heat or strong sun, which degrade foam. Between cleanings, vacuum the surface with the upholstery attachment to lift dust and dander, and air the pillow near an open window monthly. Always keep the washable cover laundered on its own schedule.

    What's the fold test, and how do I know it's time to replace rather than wash?

    Fold the pillow in half, squeeze the air out, and let go. If it springs back open on its own, the fill is alive — wash it and keep going. If it stays folded or barely recovers, the fill is dead and washing won't bring it back; replace it. Memory foam and latex don't fold, so press your palm firmly into the center and watch the recovery — quick and even is healthy, slow and partial means it's done. Persistent stains or odor that survive a full wash are the other clear replacement signal.

    Can I just toss a pillow in the dryer to freshen it up between washes?

    Yes, and it's a great quick refresh for washable fills. Run it on low (or an air/no-heat cycle) for 15–20 minutes with a couple of wool dryer balls; the tumbling re-fluffs the fill, knocks loose dust, and the gentle heat helps drive off absorbed moisture. A dryer sheet or a dab of essential oil on a dryer ball adds a fresh scent. It's not a substitute for an actual wash, but it keeps pillows lofty and less musty between the real washes. (Skip the heat for memory foam and latex.)

    How do I dry pillows without ending up with lumps?

    Low heat, lots of patience, and dryer balls. Two or three wool dryer balls (or clean tennis balls, knotted inside a sock if they leave marks) keep the fill tumbling and breaking apart instead of settling into clumps. Run multiple low-heat cycles rather than one long hot one — high heat can scorch synthetic fill and damage down. Between cycles, take the pillow out and knead it by hand to redistribute the fill and find any wet spots. Don't stop until the core squeezes bone-dry.

    Should I wash a brand-new pillow before using it?

    If it's a washable fill, a wash (or at least a low-heat tumble) before first use isn't required but is nice — it fluffs the fill, lofts it up, and airs off any factory or packaging smell. Always launder a new pillowcase and protector before first use, since new fabric carries finishing chemicals that can irritate skin. For memory foam, skip washing and instead unpack it and let it air out for a day or two; that "new foam" off-gassing smell fades with airing, not water.
