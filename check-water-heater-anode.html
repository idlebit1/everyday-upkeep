Check Your Water Heater Anode Rod

There's one part inside your water heater whose entire job is to die so the tank doesn't: a long metal rod, screwed into the top, that corrodes on purpose. As long as it's there to give itself up, the rust attacks the rod instead of your steel tank — but once it's eaten down to the bare core wire, the tank itself starts rusting from the inside, and that's the day the clock starts on a $1,000–2,000 replacement. Check the rod every couple of years, swap a depleted one for $20–40, and you can genuinely double the life of the most expensive appliance in your basement. It's an hour with a socket wrench, and almost nobody does it.

🗓️ Typically every 2 years ⏱️ ~45–90 min, plus cool-down 🛡️ Can double a tank's lifespan 💸 Rod runs ~$20–40

Why it matters

A water heater is a steel can of hot water, and hot water rusts steel. The whole design rests on a clever bit of chemistry: a more-reactive metal rod that the corrosion eats first, sacrificing itself so the tank survives. It works beautifully — right up until the rod is gone and no one noticed. Here's what's at stake:

The rod corrodes so the tank doesn't

The sacrificial anode is a more-reactive metal — usually magnesium or aluminum — wired to the steel tank. Electrochemically, the rust goes for the rod first and leaves the steel alone. The catch: once the rod is consumed, that protection is simply gone, and the tank begins rusting from the inside out.

Tank rusts once the rod's gone

A $30 rod can double tank life

This is the best return on any home-maintenance dollar there is. A fresh rod swapped on time keeps an 8-year tank going for 15 or more. Neglect it and you're buying a whole new $1,000–2,000 heater years before you needed to — to save a part that costs less than a pizza.

$1,000–2,000 replacement, years early

Smelly or rusty hot water is a warning

A depleted or reacting anode often announces itself: rotten-egg-smelling hot water, or water that runs rusty and discolored from the hot tap. Most people just live with it, or blame the city. It's usually the rod telling you to take a look — an early warning worth catching.

Rotten-egg smell, rusty hot water

Soft and hard water both eat it faster

Softened water is electrically conductive and chews through anodes alarmingly fast; very hard water drives a different kind of attack. Either way, the rod is the one component that actually wears out on a schedule you can't predict from the outside — which is exactly why it earns a real inspection.

Conditions vary — so check it

How often should you check it?

Every two years is the sensible default — pull the rod, look, and replace it when it's heavily consumed. But softened water, a tank that's getting on in years, or any smell or rust in the hot water all tighten that to a yearly look and a sooner swap. Answer four quick questions:

1. What kind of water feeds the heater?
Softened water is the anode's worst enemy — the salt makes it conductive and the rod can disappear in a couple of years. Hard water is tough on it too. Normal city water is the gentlest case.
2. How old is the water heater?
On an older or unknown-age tank, the original rod may already be long gone, so checking sooner tells you whether you're still protected — or already running on borrowed time.
3. Any smell or color in the hot water?
A sulfur (rotten-egg) smell or rusty hot water are classic anode signals — bacteria reacting with the rod, or a depleted rod letting the tank rust. Either means look now, not in two years.
4. When was the rod last checked?
If the rod has never been pulled, you simply don't know what's left of it — and "never" is the most common answer there is. The first check is the one that matters most.

    How to check and replace the anode rod

    The job is short but unforgiving of shortcuts: kill the power or gas and the cold-water supply, relieve the pressure, break the hex head loose on top, pull the rod and look, and either drop it back in or replace it. Read the safety box and the techniques first — the two things that bite people are scalding water and a plug torqued down impossibly tight. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Cut the power, drop the pressure, brace the tank

    • Turn off the energy first. On an electric heater, switch off its breaker at the panel. On a gas heater, turn the gas control dial to PILOT (or OFF). Never pull an anode with the burner or element live.
    • Shut the cold-water supply and relieve pressure. Close the cold inlet valve on top of the tank, then open a hot tap somewhere in the house and drain a couple of gallons from the tank's drain valve. This drops the pressure so nothing sprays when the plug breaks free.
    • The water is scalding — respect it. The tank holds 120–140°F+ water. Let it cool for an hour or two if you can, and keep hands and face clear of the opening. A little water will weep around the threads; that's normal, but it's hot.
    • The plug can be brutally tight — use the right tools. Anode plugs are factory-torqued and often seized. Use a 1 1/16" socket (6-point) and a long breaker bar, and brace the tank so you don't rock it and damage the water connections. A helper steadying it, or a strap to the wall studs, makes this far safer.
    • Mind the headroom, and don't overtighten the new one. A standard rod is ~44" long and needs that much clearance above the tank — a tight ceiling calls for a flexible/segmented rod. Going back in, wrap the threads with PTFE tape rated for water heaters and snug it firmly, but don't gorilla it.
    • Gas units: relight the pilot per the label, and know when to stop. Restore the gas dial and relight following the instructions printed on the tank. And if the tank is already leaking, or the rod is rusted in solid and won't budge with a breaker bar, stop — that's a plumber's call, and a leaking tank usually means it's done.

    A 1 1/16" socket (6-point) A breaker bar or long ratchet A replacement anode rod (standard or flexible/segmented) PTFE / Teflon thread tape A garden hose for the drain valve A bucket & towels Work gloves A helper or tank strap (recommended)

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Cut power, water & pressure

    Switch off the breaker (electric) or set the gas to PILOT, close the cold-water inlet, then open a hot tap and drain a couple of gallons. That kills the heat and drops the tank pressure so nothing sprays scalding water when the plug lets go.

    Find the anode hex head

    Look for a hexagonal plug on top of the tank — about 1 1/16". It may hide under a snap-off plastic cap, or share the hot-water outlet (a combo anode). If you can't find a standalone one, the outlet-port anode is what your tank uses.

    Break it loose & brace

    Fit the 1 1/16" socket and lean on a long breaker bar to crack the plug counter-clockwise — it's often seized hard. Brace the tank (a helper, or a strap to the wall) so you don't rock it and crack a water connection while you heave.

    Pull it & inspect

    Lift the rod out and read it. Heavy pitting, a thick calcium crust, or steel core wire showing — about a half-inch of metal or less left — all mean replace. Still fat and coated in metal? Drop it back in and check again in a couple of years.

    Wrap, install & restore

    Tape the new rod's threads, thread it in by hand, then snug it firmly with the socket. Use a flexible/segmented rod if the ceiling's tight. Reopen the cold supply, let the tank refill (open a hot tap until water runs steady), restore power or relight the pilot, and watch for leaks.

    Signs the rod is overdue — and when to call a pro

    A protected tank is silent and clean; the trouble shows up in the water and in the noise. These are the signals it's time to look, and the handful of cases that are a plumber's job — or a sign the heater itself is finished:

    • Rotten-egg-smelling hot water. A sulfur smell that's only in the hot water (not the cold) is the classic anode tell — sulfate-reducing bacteria reacting with a magnesium rod. Switching to an aluminum/zinc rod or a powered anode usually clears it.
    • Rusty or discolored hot water. If hot water runs orange or cloudy while the cold runs clear, the rod may be gone and the tank starting to rust internally. Check the anode now — this is the warning before a leak.
    • A rumbling, aging tank. Popping and rumbling usually means sediment, but on an older heater it's a nudge to verify the anode too while you're in there — the two tank-longevity jobs go together.
    • A rod seized solid. If a 1 1/16" socket and breaker bar won't budge the plug and you're worried about twisting the tank, stop and let a plumber break it loose with the right gear rather than damage the connections.
    • No clearance above the tank. If a low ceiling or shelf means you can't lift a 44" rod straight out, a flexible/segmented rod solves it — but if you can't even fit that, a pro can advise on a combo or powered anode.
    • A tank that's already weeping or leaking. Water pooling under the heater or seeping from the tank body means the steel has already rusted through. That's not an anode fix — a leaking tank means replacement. Call a plumber.
    • Pro tip: the anode is one of the two jobs that decide how long a tank lasts. Do it alongside flushing the water heater — drain the sediment and check the rod in the same session — and pair your heating-season checks with bleeding the radiators so the whole system gets looked at once a year.

    Common questions

    What does the anode rod actually do?

    It's a sacrificial part — a long rod of a more-reactive metal (usually magnesium, sometimes aluminum or an aluminum/zinc alloy) screwed into the top of the tank and wired electrically to the steel. Because that metal is more reactive than steel, the corrosion that would otherwise attack your tank goes for the rod instead. It "sacrifices" itself, slowly dissolving over the years. As long as a meaningful amount of rod is left, your steel tank is protected. The moment the rod is consumed down to its bare steel core wire, the protection ends and the tank begins rusting from the inside — which is why checking and replacing the rod is the single biggest factor in how long a water heater lasts.

    How often should I check or replace the anode rod?

    Check it about every two years and replace it when it's heavily consumed — that's the sensible default for most homes on normal municipal water. Tighten to once a year if you have a water softener (softened water can devour an anode in as little as two to three years), very hard or well water, an older or unknown-age tank, or any rotten-egg smell or rust in the hot water. The first check is the most important one of all, because if nobody has ever pulled the rod, you genuinely don't know whether you're still protected or already running on a bare wire. Take the quiz above for a schedule tuned to your water and tank.

    How do I know when the rod needs replacing?

    Pull it out and look. A healthy rod is still fat with metal along its length. Replace it when you see any of these: it's eaten down so that the steel core wire is exposed for long stretches, there's roughly a half-inch of metal or less left around the wire, it's deeply pitted and thin, or it's caked in a thick crust of calcium or other deposits that has effectively passivated it. If more than about six inches of bare core wire is showing, or chunks have fallen off, it's overdue. When in doubt, a new rod is $20–40 — far cheaper than guessing wrong and losing the tank.

    Why does my hot water smell like rotten eggs?

    That sulfur smell is almost always a reaction between a magnesium anode rod and sulfate-reducing bacteria in the water — harmless to health, but unpleasant, and it tells you the rod is active. The usual fix is to swap the magnesium rod for an aluminum/zinc alloy rod, which doesn't feed the same reaction, or to install a powered (impressed-current) anode that protects the tank electrically without dissolving and so doesn't produce the smell at all. Shock-disinfecting the tank with a chlorine treatment and flushing can help temporarily, but if the smell returns, changing the rod type is the durable answer.

    What if there's no clearance above the tank?

    A standard anode rod is one solid piece about 44 inches long, so it needs that much headroom to lift straight out — and many tanks tucked under a low basement ceiling, a shelf, or in a closet simply don't have it. The answer is a flexible (also called segmented or "linked") rod: it comes in short jointed sections connected by a flexible cable, so you feed it in piece by piece and it bends into the tank through a much smaller opening. It costs a little more than a standard rod and protects the tank just as well. If even that's a struggle, a powered anode, which is rigid but shorter, or a plumber's advice on a combo-port unit, is the next step.

    Does softened water really shorten anode life?

    Yes — noticeably. Water softeners replace hardness minerals with sodium, which makes the water more electrically conductive, and that supercharges the sacrificial reaction. A magnesium rod that might last six or eight years on normal water can be eaten down in as little as two to three years on softened water. It's not a reason to stop softening — soft water is gentler on the rest of the plumbing and on the tank's sediment buildup — but it is a strong reason to check the rod yearly rather than every two years, and many people on softeners switch to a longer-lasting powered anode so they're not pulling a fresh rod every couple of years.
