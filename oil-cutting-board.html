Oil Your Wooden Cutting Board

A wooden board is thirsty. Every wash pulls moisture out of the grain, and as it dries the wood shrinks, lightens, roughens, and eventually splits — and those splits trap food and bacteria. Food-grade mineral oil fills the wood back up and seals the surface, so it shrugs off water, stains, and odors instead of soaking them in. A quick rub about once a month — or whenever the board looks dry — is the whole secret to a board that lasts for decades.

🗓️ Oil about monthly 🧴 A bottle costs $8–$15 ⏱️ 5 minutes of hands-on time 🪵 A cared-for board lasts decades

Why it matters

Wood is not an inert slab — it's a bundle of fibers that swells when wet and shrinks when dry, over and over, every time you wash and dry the board. Oil is what keeps that swing from tearing the board apart: it fills the grain, slows water from soaking in, and keeps the surface dense enough that food and bacteria can't burrow in. Here's what a regular oiling is quietly doing for you (and for your wooden spoons and salad bowls, which want the same treatment):

It stops the wood drying out

Every wash leaches moisture and natural oils out of the grain. A board left to dry bare keeps losing them until the fibers go brittle, lighten in color, and feel rough and fuzzy underhand. Mineral oil tops the wood back up so it stays supple instead of parched.

A dry board is a dying board

It prevents cracks & warping

When one face dries faster than the other, the board cups, twists, and finally splits along the grain. Sealing the wood evens out how fast moisture moves in and out — that's what keeps a flat board flat and a solid board un-cracked for years.

Cracks & cupping are forever

Water, stains & odors bead off

A sealed surface lets beet juice, raw-meat residue, garlic, and onion sit on top instead of sinking in. Water beads and wipes away rather than soaking into the fibers — so the board rinses clean and doesn't end up permanently stained or smelling like last night's onions.

Bare wood drinks it all in

A good board lasts decades

A well-made wooden board is a buy-it-for-life tool — but only if you feed it. Oil it on a rhythm and the same board outlasts a drawer full of plastic ones, getting a deeper color and a smoother surface with age instead of cracking apart in a season.

Heirloom, not disposable

How often should you oil it?

The rule of thumb is once a month — or whenever it looks dry. That dry look is the real signal: a board that's gone pale, dull, or feels rough is telling you it's thirsty. Heavy use and lots of washing speed that up; a brand-new board needs a burst of coats up front. Answer three questions to tune your rhythm. (This is for wood and bamboo boards — plastic ones don't need oiling.)

1. How often do you use and wash the board?
2. How does it look and feel right now?

    How to oil a wooden cutting board

    Hands-on time is about five minutes; the rest is just letting the oil soak in. The whole trick is two ideas — start with a board that's clean and bone dry, then give it more oil than you think it needs and let the wood drink. Read the techniques, then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Before you start — the right oil, a dry board, and never the dishwasher

    • Use FOOD-GRADE mineral oil (or a board cream) — never cooking oils. Olive, vegetable, canola, and other kitchen oils go rancid: they oxidize, turn sticky, and leave the board smelling sour and spoiled. Plain food-grade mineral oil never goes off. A beeswax-and-mineral-oil "board butter" is the other safe choice.
    • The board must be clean and completely dry first. Oil can't penetrate wet wood — it just sits on top and traps moisture underneath. Wash, dry, and let the board sit until it's bone dry before you start, ideally a few hours or overnight.
    • Never put a wooden board in the dishwasher. The long soak, blasting water, heat, and detergent are exactly what warps, cracks, and splits wood — one cycle can ruin a board. Hand-wash only, and never leave it soaking in the sink either.
    • Let it cure before you use it. After oiling, give the board a few hours — overnight is best — to absorb the oil and dry to the touch. Wipe off whatever didn't soak in so it isn't slick when you start chopping.
    • This is for wood and bamboo only. Plastic and composite boards don't absorb oil and don't need it — save the mineral oil for your wooden boards, spoons, and bowls.

    📐 Five things worth seeing first

    Wash & dry it completely

    Hand-wash the board with mild soap, towel it off, then stand it on edge until it's bone dry — a few hours, ideally overnight. Oil never soaks into damp wood; it just sits on top and seals moisture in.

    Sand lightly if it's rough

    Optional, but worth it on a board whose grain has raised or feels fuzzy after washing. A few passes with fine sandpaper (320+ grit), always with the grain, smooths it back so the oil goes on evenly.

    Pour on plenty & rub it in

    Be generous — pour a good puddle of mineral oil and rub it into every surface with a cloth: the top, the bottom, the sides, and especially the thirsty end grain. A dry board will drink up far more than you'd guess.

    Let it soak, then wipe off

    Walk away and let the oil sink in for a few hours or overnight. Then buff off whatever didn't absorb with a clean cloth, so the surface is satiny — not slick — and ready to use the next day.

    Finish with wax — and repeat if new

    For extra water resistance, rub on a thin coat of board cream over the cured oil and buff it to a soft shine. And a brand-new board is parched: repeat the whole oil-and-soak routine every day or two until the wood stops drinking the oil in.

    🎉 Done — the board is fed, sealed, and ready. Let it cure overnight, then the next oiling is due in about a month (or whenever it looks dry).

    When to resurface or replace it

    Oiling maintains a sound board, but sometimes the wood is past saving with a cloth and a bottle. Sand it back to fresh wood, or retire it, when you see any of these:

    • Deep cracks or splits. A hairline that closes when the board is oiled is fine; a crack you can catch a fingernail in traps food and water down where you can't clean it. Sanding may save a shallow one — a deep split means it's time to replace.
    • A warped or cupped board. A board that rocks on the counter or has visibly bowed has dried unevenly. Heavy sanding can sometimes flatten a mild cup, but a badly warped board rarely comes back and is no longer safe to cut on.
    • Black mold worked into the grain. Dark spots that won't scrub or sand out have grown roots into the fibers. Once mold is established deep in the wood, replace the board — you can't reliably clean it out.
    • Deep, crowded knife grooves. A few light score marks are normal; a surface chewed into deep gouges harbors bacteria and food. Sand it smooth (resurface), or replace it if it's too thin to take more sanding.
    • A glued board separating at the seams. When a butcher block or edge-glued board starts coming apart along its glue lines, water gets into the joints and there's no fixing it at home. Replace it.

    Common questions

    How often should I oil a cutting board?

    The common advice is about once a month, but the honest answer is "whenever it looks dry." A board in heavy daily use, or one that gets washed a lot, dries out faster and may want oiling every couple of weeks; a board that mostly serves cheese can go longer. Watch the wood, not just the calendar: when the color goes pale and dull, water stops beading, or the surface feels rough, it's thirsty. A brand-new board is the exception — it needs several coats up front (see below).

    What oil should I use — can I use olive or vegetable oil?

    Use food-grade mineral oil, and only that (or a purpose-made board cream). Do not use olive, vegetable, canola, sunflower, or other cooking oils: they're unsaturated and go rancid, oxidizing into a sticky, gummy film that makes the board smell sour and spoiled. Mineral oil is inert — it never goes off, has no taste or smell, and is sold cheaply at pharmacies and hardware stores. Walnut and other specialty "drying" oils exist, but plain food-grade mineral oil is the simple, reliable, allergy-friendly default.

    What's the difference between mineral oil and board butter or wax?

    Mineral oil penetrates: it soaks into the grain and keeps the wood moisturized from within. Board cream — often called "board butter," usually a blend of beeswax (or carnauba) and mineral oil — sits more on the surface and adds a water-repellent layer on top. The best routine uses both: oil monthly to feed the wood, then occasionally finish with a coat of board cream for extra water resistance and a soft sheen. The wax alone isn't a substitute for the oil underneath.

    How do I season a brand-new board?

    A new board is bone dry from the factory and very thirsty, so one coat isn't enough. Oil it generously, let it soak in for a few hours or overnight, wipe off the excess, and then do it again — repeat the whole cycle every day or two for the first week or two until the wood stops absorbing the oil as fast (you'll notice the surface staying glossy instead of drinking it right in). After that initial burst, drop back to the normal monthly-ish rhythm.

    Can a wooden cutting board go in the dishwasher?

    Never. The dishwasher is the single fastest way to destroy a wooden board: a long hot soak swells the wood, the drying cycle shrinks it again, and the repeated swing — plus harsh detergent — warps, cracks, and splits it, often in a single run. The same goes for leaving a board to soak in the sink. Hand-wash with mild soap and water, rinse, towel dry, and stand it on edge to finish drying so air reaches both faces.

    How do I remove stains and odors before oiling?

    Scrub the board with a paste of coarse salt and half a lemon: the salt is a gentle abrasive that lifts surface stains while the lemon's acidity cuts grease and neutralizes odors like garlic, onion, and fish. Work it in, let it sit a few minutes, then rinse and dry the board completely. Do this before you oil — never after — so you're sealing a clean, fresh surface. While you're caring for the kitchen, a sharp knife is kinder to the board too; see our guide to sharpening kitchen knives.
