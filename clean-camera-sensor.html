Clean Your Camera Sensor

You notice it on a blue-sky shot first: the same little dark blobs, frame after frame, in exactly the same spots. That's dust on your sensor — and once you've seen it, you can't unsee it in every photo you take. The good news is that most of it lifts off in minutes with the right gentle method: confirm it's the sensor and not the lens, run the in-camera shake, then a few puffs from a rocket blower with nothing ever touching the glass, and — only if a spot is truly stuck — a single, careful pass with a sensor swab. The bad news is that the wrong method (canned air, a fingertip, a dry rub) can scratch a part that costs more to replace than your camera body is worth. So this guide is about doing the safe thing, in the safe order, and knowing the one moment to stop and hand it to a shop.

Why it matters

A clean sensor is invisible — you never think about it. A dirty one quietly taxes every single frame you shoot, and the longer you ignore it the more photos it ruins. But it's also a part you can damage badly if you go in heavy-handed, which is exactly why the gentle, in-order approach below matters so much. Here's what's at stake:

Dark spots on every shot

Sensor dust prints the same dark blobs in the same place on every frame — most visible on blue skies and at small apertures like f/16 and up, where the specks come into sharp focus. Then you're cloning them out one by one in editing, photo after photo.

Hours of spot-removal in editing

It's basically unavoidable

Every time you change a lens, you open the camera and invite dust onto the sensor — and mirrorless bodies, with the sensor sitting right behind the mount, catch it especially fast. There's no preventing it entirely; the only question is how cleanly you deal with it.

New dust with every lens swap

The wrong fix is expensive

Canned air can blast propellant and freeze-spray liquid onto the sensor; a fingertip leaves oils that bond to the coating; a dry rub drags grit across the surface and scratches it. Any of those can mean a repair that costs more than the body is worth.

A scratch can total the body

The right way takes minutes

Done gently — shake, then a few puffs of a rocket blower, and a single swab only if needed — most dust is gone in well under twenty minutes, with no contact for the easy cases. You rarely need a shop, and you save yourself the spot-removal grind.

Otherwise: a shop visit each time

How often should you clean it?

Honest answer: a sensor is cleaned as needed — whenever you actually see spots — not on a rigid calendar. A periodic check, though, keeps dust from sneaking up on you before a big shoot. About once a year is plenty for careful shooters; swap lenses constantly or work in dust and wind and you'll want to look far more often. Answer four quick questions for a check interval that fits how you shoot:

1. How often do you change lenses or shoot in dust and wind?
Every lens change is an open door for dust, and dusty or windy locations load the sensor far faster. The more you expose the mount, the more often it's worth a look.
2. Do you see dark spots in your photos right now?
Visible spots mean it's already time to clean, not just to check. Test on a blank sky at a small aperture if you're not sure — that's where dust shows itself.
3. Mirrorless or DSLR?
On mirrorless bodies the sensor is exposed at every lens change, so it tends to collect dust faster. A DSLR's mirror gives a little shelter — but its sensor still gathers dust over time.
4. How do you feel about cleaning it yourself?
There's no shame in any answer. A rocket blower is risk-free and worth owning whatever you decide; wet-swabbing is safe done right but unforgiving done wrong — and a shop is cheap insurance for an expensive part.

    How to clean your camera sensor

    This is an expensive, delicate part, so the whole method is built around escalating gently and stopping early. Confirm it's actually the sensor, try the no-contact fixes first (in-camera shake, then a rocket blower), and only reach for a wet swab if a spot is genuinely stuck — one careful pass, then re-test. Read the safety box and all five techniques before you open the camera, then work the checklist. Progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Read this first — a sensor is fragile and pricey

    • Fully charge the battery before you open up. If the camera powers down mid-clean, it can drop the shutter or mirror onto your blower or swab and cause real damage. A full battery (or AC adapter) keeps the sensor-clean mode held safely open.
    • Use the camera's sensor-clean / mirror-lock-up mode — never hold a DSLR mirror up by hand. Dig into the menu for "sensor cleaning," "manual cleaning," or "mirror lock up for cleaning." It locks everything safely out of the way; forcing the mirror with a finger can bend or break it.
    • NEVER use canned or compressed air. The propellant can spray liquid onto the sensor, and the sheer force (and freezing) can damage the surface or the delicate AA filter over it. Use only a hand-squeezed rocket blower — your lungs and aerosols are both off-limits.
    • Never touch the sensor with a finger, and never dry-rub it. Skin oils bond to the coating, and dragging anything dry across the surface grinds grit into it and leaves scratches. The sensor surface gets touched only by a fresh, fluid-dampened sensor swab — nothing else.
    • Use only sensor-rated swabs and fluid, one pass per swab. Buy the correct swab width for your sensor size, use just a drop or two of sensor fluid, make one light pass across and back, and switch to a fresh swab (or fresh side) every time. Never reuse a dirty swab.
    • Work clean, draft-free, and face-down. Pick a clean, low-dust room with no fan or breeze, and hold the camera with the mount facing the floor so loosened dust falls out instead of resettling. Have everything laid out before you open the body.
    • If you're unsure at any point, stop and send it to a pro. There is no shame in it, and a shop cleaning is cheap next to a damaged sensor. Repeated swabbing that isn't working, oily smears, or any worry about a scratch all mean: close the camera and let a professional finish.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    First, confirm it's the sensor

    Shoot a plain white wall or even sky at f/16–f/22 and look across several frames. Dust that sits in the exact same spot every time is on the sensor; specks that move, change, or don't appear are on the lens or elsewhere — clean those differently.

    Try the in-camera shake first

    Most cameras have an ultrasonic sensor-cleaning function that vibrates dust loose. Run it from the menu a few times — it's completely safe, needs no tools, and clears a surprising amount of light dust before you ever open the body.

    Blower next — face down, no contact

    Lock the mirror up or enter sensor-clean mode, then hold the camera with the mount pointing straight down and puff the rocket blower toward the sensor. Never let the nozzle touch the glass; gravity carries the loosened dust out the bottom.

    Wet swab only if it's stuck

    For a spot that won't blow off, use a correct-width sensor swab with a drop or two of fluid. Drag it across the sensor with light, even pressure — one pass over and back — then stop. Use a fresh swab or fresh side every time; never go over the same swab twice.

    Re-test, and know when to stop

    Take another f/22 sky shot and check your work. If a couple of careful swabs aren't shifting a spot — or you see oily smears or worry about a scratch — that's your signal to stop. Repeated swabbing only risks damage; let a shop finish the job.

    Signs it's time — and when to call a pro

    A clean sensor announces itself only by its absence: clear blue skies, no repeating blobs, nothing to clone out. These are the signals it needs attention — and the handful that mean you should close the camera and let a professional handle it rather than risk an expensive body:

    • The same dark spots, frame after frame. If a blank-sky test at f/16–f/22 shows fixed blobs in the same spots across shots, that's sensor dust — start with the shake and blower. This is also worth a quick check before any important shoot.
    • Spots that won't blow or swab off. Dust that survives the in-camera shake, several puffs of the blower, and a careful swab or two is telling you it's stuck or it isn't simple dust. Stop swabbing and let a pro take over — more passes just add scratch risk.
    • Oily streaks or smears, not dry specks. Greasy marks often come from the shutter mechanism or lubricant migration, not stray dust. That's a service issue, not a cleaning job — a shop can diagnose and clean it properly.
    • Any worry that you've scratched it — or might. If you see a mark that looks like a scratch, or you simply feel out of your depth with the swab, stop. A scratched low-pass filter or sensor coating is a costly repair, and pushing on rarely helps.
    • You'd rather not risk an expensive body at all. There's nothing wrong with this. A camera shop's sensor cleaning is typically $50–80 and is cheap insurance against a four-figure mistake — especially for a pro body or a brand-new camera.
    • A weather-sealed or unfamiliar body you're not sure how to open safely. If you can't find the sensor-clean / mirror-lock-up mode, or you're unsure how your particular model exposes the sensor, don't improvise — check the manual or hand it to a shop.

    Common questions

    How do I tell sensor dust from lens dust?

    Shoot a test: point the camera at a plain white wall or an even patch of sky, set a small aperture like f/16 to f/22, and take several frames — slightly defocus so only the dust shows. Then look at the images side by side. Dust on the sensor appears as the same dark spots in exactly the same place in every frame, regardless of what you point the camera at; it's most visible at small apertures and on bright, even backgrounds. Dust or smudges on the lens usually don't show in the image at all (the front element is far from the focal plane), and any marks that move, change between frames, or vanish when you swap lenses are not on the sensor. If the spots stay put across frames and lenses, it's the sensor — and this guide is for you. If they move or disappear with a different lens, clean the lens instead, which is a separate, gentler job.

    Can I use canned or compressed air?

    No — never. This is the single most common way people damage a sensor. Canned air is under high pressure and often sprays liquid propellant along with the air; that propellant can leave a residue or freeze-spray right onto the sensor's surface, which is far harder to remove than the dust you started with. The force itself can also drive grit into the surface or stress the delicate low-pass filter sitting over the sensor, and a stray blast can bend the shutter blades. The same goes for blowing with your mouth (you'll spray saliva) and for any air compressor. The only safe air is a hand-squeezed rocket-style blower, held close but never touching, with the camera facing down. If a rocket blower and a careful swab don't do it, that's a sign for a shop — not a stronger air source.

    Blower or wet swab — which do I try first?

    Always escalate gently, least-invasive first. Step one is the camera's built-in ultrasonic sensor-cleaning (the "shake"), which is completely safe and clears a lot of light dust on its own. Step two is the rocket blower: enter sensor-clean or mirror-lock-up mode, hold the body with the mount facing straight down, and puff air toward the sensor without touching it, letting gravity carry the dust out. Most dust is gone by this point with zero contact, which is exactly why you do it first. Only if a specific spot survives the shake and several rounds of blowing do you move to a wet swab — and even then it's a single, light pass per swab, fresh side each time. The wet swab is the one method that actually contacts the sensor, so it's the last resort, not the opener.

    Is there a safe DIY method, and will I wreck my sensor?

    Yes, there's a safe DIY method, and no, you won't wreck it if you stay within the gentle steps. The genuinely risk-free parts — the in-camera shake and the rocket blower — handle the majority of dust and involve no contact at all, so anyone can and should do those. Wet-swabbing is also safe when you use a sensor-rated swab of the correct width, a drop or two of real sensor fluid, light even pressure, one pass, and a fresh swab every time. Where people get into trouble is by improvising: canned air, a cotton swab or tissue, a fingertip, dry rubbing, reusing a dirty swab, or scrubbing the same stuck spot over and over. Charge the battery fully so the camera can't power down mid-clean, work in a clean draft-free room, and — most important — be willing to stop. If you're nervous about the swab, do the no-contact steps and leave the rest to a shop. That's not failure; that's good judgment with an expensive part.

    How often should I clean the sensor?

    Treat it as as needed rather than a fixed schedule: the real trigger is seeing dark spots in your shots, especially on blue skies and at small apertures. That said, a periodic check keeps dust from ambushing you before a big shoot. For careful shooters who don't change lenses much and work in clean conditions, checking about once a year is plenty. If you swap lenses constantly, shoot outdoors in dust or wind, or use a mirrorless body where the sensor is exposed at every change, look far more often — every month or two, and definitely before any shoot that matters. A good habit is a quick blank-sky test whenever you suspect something or before an important job; clean only when the test actually shows spots. The blower-first approach means most "cleanings" are a thirty-second, no-contact affair anyway.

    Mirrorless vs. DSLR — and should I just pay a shop?

    The method is the same, but the details differ. On a mirrorless camera the sensor sits right behind the lens mount, so it's exposed every time you change a lens — that means it collects dust faster, but it's also easy to see and reach for cleaning. On a DSLR there's a mirror (and shutter) in front of the sensor, which offers a little shelter from dust but means you must use the camera's mirror-lock-up or sensor-clean mode to get access — never force the mirror up by hand, as you can bend or break it, and never let the camera power down with the mirror up. Either way, charge the battery, work face-down in a clean room, and escalate gently. As for paying a shop: if you'd rather not risk an expensive body, if a spot won't budge after a couple of careful swabs, if you see oily smears, or if you're simply unsure — yes, hand it over. A professional cleaning is typically $50–80, which is trivial next to repairing or replacing a sensor. Many people happily do the blower step themselves and leave wet-swabbing to a pro.
