How to Wash a Jacket

There's no single answer to "how do I wash this jacket?" — a down puffer, a waterproof rain shell, a cozy fleece, and a wool or leather coat each want to be washed a completely different way, and the little care label sewn into the seam is the boss of all of them. The good news: a few universal rules cover most of it. Wash it less than you think — over-washing and heat quietly wear out the fabric, the insulation, and the water-repellent coating. Empty the pockets, zip everything up, skip the fabric softener, and dry it the right way. Get the method right for your jacket's material and it comes out fresh, re-lofted, and still performing — instead of flat, funky, or ruined.

🗓️ Typically every 3 months 🏷️ Care label is the boss 🧴 Tech/down wash ~$10–15 🚫 No fabric softener

Why it matters

A jacket touches your neck, your wrists, and everything you lean against, so it gets dirtier than it looks — but the fix isn't "wash it more." Getting this right is really about washing the right amount in the right way for what the jacket is made of. Here's what's at stake:

Body oils & grime build up

A jacket rubs against your neck, wrists, and everything you lean on, so it collects body oils, sweat, and everyday grime faster than you'd guess — the collar and cuffs especially. Left alone it starts to look grubby and smell, even when the rest of it seems fine.

A grubby, funky-smelling jacket

The wrong wash ruins it

Machine-washing a structured wool or leather jacket, tumble-drying down on high, or using fabric softener on a rain shell can wreck a pricey piece in one cycle — felted wool, clumped insulation, a coating that no longer repels water. The method matters more than the wash.

A $100–400 jacket, wrecked

Over-washing wears it out

Washing a jacket too often — and too hot — breaks down the fabric, flattens insulation, and strips the water-repellent coating over time. Most jackets need washing far less than people assume. Spot-clean the collar and cuffs and air it out between real washes.

Thin, flat, worn-out fabric

The right method keeps it performing

Washed the way each material is meant to be, a jacket keeps doing its job: down re-lofts and traps warmth again, rain shells re-bead so water rolls off, and fleece stays fresh and un-pilled. The correct wash actually restores performance instead of eroding it.

Warmth & water-repellency lost

How often should you wash it?

For a jacket you wear regularly, roughly every few months is a sensible default — but wear and type move it a lot, and the honest headline is wash less than you think, because over-washing wears a jacket out. Just as important as how often is how: the material and the care label decide machine-wash vs. dry-clean. Answer four quick questions:

1. What's the jacket made of?
Material is the biggest fork in the road: down and shells want special washes and specific drying, fleece is easy, and wool, leather, and suede shouldn't go in the machine at all.
2. How hard do you wear it?
A jacket you sweat in or wear against skin every day loads up with oils fast; one you grab occasionally over other layers stays clean far longer and can be washed much less.
3. How is it right now?
A jacket that smells or is visibly dirty is due now; a rain shell that soaks in instead of beading needs a wash and a re-proof; and one that seems fine mostly wants airing out, not a wash.
4. What does the care label say?
The label outranks everything here. "Dry-clean only" means take it in, not risk it; and if you can't tell, treat it as delicate and check the maker's guidance before you wet it.

    How to wash a jacket, by material

    The care label decides machine-wash vs. dry-clean, and the material decides the method — so start there. The universal moves are the same for every jacket: empty the pockets, zip and fasten everything, skip the fabric softener, and dry it fully before storing. Read the techniques first (especially the label rules and drying), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ The label rules — read this before you wet anything

    • Check the care label first — it outranks this guide. The sewn-in label decides machine-wash vs. dry-clean and gives the temperature and drying limits. If it says dry-clean only, take it in; if the symbols are unclear, look up the brand's care page before you risk it.
    • Wash jackets LESS often than you think. Over-washing and heat wear out the fabric, flatten the insulation, and strip water-repellent coatings. Between real washes, spot-clean the collar and cuffs and air the jacket out — that handles most of the "it seems dirty" cases without a full cycle.
    • DOWN jackets: use a down or tech wash (never regular detergent — it strips the loft), run a gentle cycle in a front-loader, then a LONG, LOW-heat tumble with a couple of dryer balls or clean tennis balls to break up clumps and re-loft the down — exactly like a sleeping bag.
    • WATERPROOF / rain shells: use a tech wash — NEVER regular detergent or fabric softener. Softener coats the fabric and destroys the DWR (durable water-repellent) finish so water soaks in instead of beading. After washing, a low tumble or a spray/wash-in re-proofer reactivates the DWR.
    • FLEECE / synthetics: machine-wash cool, inside-out, with a mild detergent and no fabric softener (it clogs the fibers and kills the loft). Air-dry or use low heat — high heat pills and can even melt synthetic fabric.
    • WOOL, LEATHER, SUEDE, and structured/tailored jackets: don't machine-wash them. Water felts wool and stiffens or stains leather and suede, and agitation destroys the internal structure of a tailored jacket. Spot-clean small marks or take it to a dry cleaner or leather specialist.
    • Before ANY wash: empty the pockets, ZIP all zippers, and fasten Velcro and snaps. Open Velcro snags and pills everything it touches, and loose zips can tear fabric or scratch the drum. Close the jacket up and turn it inside out to protect the face fabric.
    • Dry it FULLY before storing. Damp insulation and folds grow mildew and smell. Never wring or high-heat a jacket with a membrane or insulation — heat and twisting damage coatings and clump down. Let it finish completely before it goes in the closet.

    📐 Five methods — find your jacket's material

    1 · Read the label & prep

    Find the care symbols and follow them — they outrank everything. Then empty every pocket, zip all the zippers, fasten the Velcro and snaps (open Velcro snags), and turn the jacket inside out to protect the face fabric.

    2 · Down — wash gentle, tumble to re-loft

    Down or tech wash (never regular detergent) on a gentle cycle in a front-loader. Then a long, low-heat tumble with a couple of dryer balls to break up the clumps and re-loft the down — just like a sleeping bag.

    3 · Waterproof shell — tech wash, then re-proof

    Tech wash only — no regular detergent, and never fabric softener (it destroys the DWR). After washing, reactivate the water-repellency with a low tumble or a wash-in / spray re-proofer so water beads up and rolls off again.

    4 · Fleece / synthetic — cool wash, air-dry

    Cool, gentle cycle, inside-out, mild detergent, and no fabric softener (it clogs the fibers). Air-dry on a hanger or use low heat only — high heat pills fleece and can melt synthetic fabric.

    5 · Wool / leather / structured — dry-clean

    Don't machine-wash these. Spot-clean small marks, or take the jacket to a dry cleaner or leather specialist. Then air it out and let it dry fully before it goes back in the closet.

    Signs it's due — and when to hand it to a pro

    Most jackets tell you clearly when they want a wash, and a few problems aren't a washing job at all. Here's what to watch for, and where to hand it off:

    • It smells or looks visibly dirty. A funky smell that airing out won't fix, or grime you can see on the collar, cuffs, and front — that's a jacket that's genuinely due for a wash, by the right method for its material.
    • A rain shell soaks in instead of beading. When water darkens and wets the fabric rather than rolling off in beads, the DWR has worn down. Wash it with a tech wash and re-proof the coating — the guide's shell method restores the beading.
    • Any wool, leather, suede, down, or structured jacket you're unsure how to clean. These are the pieces where a wrong move is expensive and hard to undo. If you're not confident, a dry cleaner or leather/down specialist is cheap insurance against ruining it.
    • A delaminating membrane. If a waterproof shell's inner layer is bubbling, flaking, or peeling away from the face fabric, that's not dirt — the laminate has failed. It's a repair or warranty issue, not something a wash fixes.
    • Leaking down. Feathers poking through or escaping from seams mean a torn baffle or worn fabric. Patch small holes, but persistent leaking is a repair job — washing won't stop it and can make it worse.
    • Pro tip: most "it seems dirty" jackets just need airing out and a quick spot-clean of the collar and cuffs, not a full wash. Save the real washes for when it smells or is visibly soiled — your jacket will last far longer for it.

    Common questions

    How often should I wash my jacket?

    Less often than you probably think. For a jacket you wear regularly, every few months is a reasonable rhythm — but it really comes down to wear and type. Something you sweat in daily or wear against your skin needs washing more often than a shell you throw on over other layers, which might go a whole season. Over-washing (and washing hot) breaks down fabric, flattens insulation, and strips water-repellent coatings, so the goal isn't a clean jacket at all costs — it's a jacket that stays fresh and keeps performing for years. Between real washes, air it out and spot-clean the collar and cuffs, and only run a full cycle when it actually smells or looks dirty.

    How do I wash a down jacket?

    Treat it like a sleeping bag. Zip it up, empty the pockets, and turn it inside out. Use a dedicated down wash or tech wash — never regular detergent, which strips the natural oils that let the down loft — and run a gentle cycle in a front-loading machine (top-loaders with a center agitator can tear baffles). The critical part is drying: tumble on LOW heat for a long time with a couple of dryer balls or clean tennis balls, pausing to pull apart any clumps by hand. The balls and the low, slow heat break up the wet clumps and re-loft the down so it traps warmth again. Rushing the dry, or using high heat, leaves you with a flat, lumpy jacket — so give it as long as it needs to come out completely dry and fluffy.

    How do I wash a waterproof or rain jacket?

    Use a tech wash — a cleaner made for technical fabrics — and never regular detergent or fabric softener. Detergent residue and, especially, fabric softener coat the fabric and kill the DWR (durable water-repellent) finish, which is exactly what makes water bead up and roll off. Wash on a gentle cycle following the label, then reactivate the DWR: either a low tumble-dry (the heat re-bonds the existing coating) or a wash-in or spray-on re-proofer if the beading is really gone. Counterintuitively, a rain shell often works better after a proper wash-and-reproof than a grubby one, because grime itself attracts water and defeats the coating.

    Can I machine-wash a wool or leather jacket?

    No — don't. Water and agitation felt and shrink wool, and they stiffen, stain, and warp leather and suede; a structured or tailored jacket also has internal canvas and padding that the machine destroys. For these, spot-clean small marks (a damp cloth for wool, a dedicated leather cleaner or a suede brush for those) and otherwise take the jacket to a dry cleaner, and a leather or suede specialist for those materials. It costs more per clean than a home wash, but it's a fraction of replacing a jacket you ruined — and these pieces need cleaning far less often anyway, so the cost over a year is small.

    Why does my rain jacket soak through now?

    Its DWR finish has worn down — the invisible coating on the face fabric that used to make water bead up and roll off. Body oils, dirt, abrasion, and time all degrade it, and once it's gone the fabric wets out (darkens and soaks) instead of shedding water. Note that the waterproof membrane underneath is usually still fine; it's the surface repellency that's failed. The fix is a wash-and-reproof: clean it with a tech wash to remove the grime that's defeating the coating, then reactivate or replace the DWR with a low tumble-dry or a wash-in / spray-on re-proofer. Do that and the water beads again.

    Should I use fabric softener on jackets?

    Never on a jacket — especially a technical one. Fabric softener works by leaving a waxy coating on fibers, and on a rain shell that coating destroys the DWR so water soaks straight through. On fleece and synthetics it clogs the fibers, kills the loft, and reduces breathability and wicking. On down it can weigh down and mat the insulation. There's essentially no jacket where softener helps and several where it does real, hard-to-reverse damage — so leave it out of every jacket wash. If a jacket comes out stiff, that's usually detergent residue; an extra rinse fixes it far better than softener would.
