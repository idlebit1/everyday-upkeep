Wash Your Mattress Protector

It's the quiet workhorse of the bed: night after night, the protector soaks up the sweat, skin cells, oils, and drool that would otherwise sink straight into your mattress — which is exactly why it needs washing on roughly the same schedule as your sheets. The catch is that most protectors are waterproof, with a thin TPU or polyurethane membrane on the back, and high heat is what kills them. Wash it warm — never hot — skip the fabric softener, dry it on low or hang it, and it'll keep doing its job for years. Crank the dryer to rush it, and you'll crack the waterproofing and be shopping for a new one. Here's how to do it right.

🗓️ Typically every 2 weeks 🌡️ Warm, not hot — protects the membrane 🔥 Dry on low or air-dry 💸 Saves a $25–60 protector

Why it matters

A mattress protector isn't decoration — it's a barrier, and a barrier only works if it stays clean and intact. Every night it intercepts what your body sheds, and most protectors do it with a waterproof layer that's easy to ruin if you wash it wrong. Here's why a regular, careful wash matters:

It soaks up the nightly mess

Sweat, dead skin cells, body oils, and drool come off you every night and the protector catches them first. Leave it unwashed and that buildup turns into odor and a damp little breeding ground — the exact thing the protector is meant to keep out of your bed.

Odor & a grimy bed

It's where allergens collect

All that shed skin is dust-mite food, and the protector is right where they and their droppings gather. Washing it regularly — in warm water, dried fully — clears out the allergens and is a genuine, proven help if anyone in the bed has allergies or asthma.

Worse allergies & asthma

A dirty one defeats its purpose

A protector saturated with old sweat and oils stops protecting — odor and moisture push right through to the mattress it's supposed to guard. The whole point is to keep your mattress clean and warranty-safe, and a neglected protector quietly fails at it.

A soiled, unwashable mattress

The waterproof layer is delicate

That thin TPU or polyurethane membrane is what makes it waterproof — and high heat, hot water, or fabric softener crack, peel, or coat it until it stops repelling liquid. Wash it wrong even once and you can end its waterproofing for good.

Cracked, dead waterproofing

How often should you wash it?

Run it with the sheets — about every two weeks — and you can't go far wrong; once a month is the absolute floor. But allergies, a hot or sweaty sleeper, pets and kids in the bed, or any history of spills all pull it toward weekly, while a barely-used guest-bed protector can stretch to every month or two. Answer four quick questions:

1. How warm or sweaty is the sleeper?
The more you sweat into the bed, the faster the protector loads up with moisture and oils — hot sleepers and warm rooms drive a tighter schedule than a cool, dry one.
2. Anyone with allergies or asthma in the bed?
Dust mites and their allergens collect right in the protector, so frequent washing in warm water is one of the most effective things an allergy or asthma sufferer can do.
3. Who (or what) shares the bed?
Pets bring dander, dirt, and the occasional accident; kids and co-sleepers add more bodies shedding into the same protector. More occupants means more to wash out, more often.
4. How often do spills or accidents happen?
A protector's whole job is catching spills — but a caught spill needs washing out promptly, before it sours or wicks through. Frequent accidents are the single biggest reason to wash weekly.

    The cost of skipping it

    Washing the protector is genuinely free — it goes straight into a normal wash load with your sheets, so it costs you nothing but the second it takes to toss it in. What costs money is washing it wrong: high heat that forces an early replacement, or skipping it entirely until the mattress underneath is soaked with sweat and allergens and can't be cleaned at all.

    How to wash a mattress protector

    The whole job is easy — strip it with the sheets, pre-treat anything that needs it, wash warm and gentle, dry low or hang it, and put it back on dry. The one thing that matters above all else is heat: the waterproof backing can't survive a hot wash or a high-heat dryer. Read the safety box and the techniques first (especially "no high heat"), then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Protect the waterproofing — heat is the enemy

    • Read the care tag first — it outranks everything here. Most waterproof protectors want a gentle or normal cycle in warm (not hot) water, but yours may differ. The tag is sewn in for a reason; check it before you set the dial.
    • Never use high heat to dry it. Tumble on low or air-dry. High heat is the number-one way protectors are ruined — it melts, cracks, or peels the TPU/polyurethane backing, and once that membrane fails the protector is no longer waterproof. There's no fixing it.
    • Skip fabric softener and dryer sheets. They leave a coating that clogs the membrane and kills both its breathability and its waterproofing. A protector should never feel "softened."
    • Skip bleach unless the tag specifically allows it. Bleach degrades the waterproof layer and the elastic. Use a normal amount of mild detergent instead — no need to overdose it.
    • Don't iron and don't dry-clean. An iron's heat will melt the backing instantly, and dry-cleaning solvents attack it; both are off the table for waterproof protectors.
    • Wash a brand-new protector before its first use. It clears manufacturing residue and softens the fabric so it sleeps better from night one.
    • Make sure it's fully dry before it goes back on. A protector put back even slightly damp traps moisture against the mattress and grows mildew — the opposite of the point.
    • Give thick, quilted protectors a longer low-heat dry. The padding holds water, so be patient on low rather than cranking the temperature to rush it — rushing it with heat is exactly what ends its life.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Strip it with the sheets & check the tag

    Pull the protector off along with your sheets so it washes on the same schedule. Then find the care tag and read it — whether it's a waterproof protector with a TPU backing or a simple fabric one changes the rules on heat and softener.

    Pre-treat stains & shake it out

    Dab any spots — sweat shadows, spills, accidents — with a gentle stain remover, a little dish soap, or an enzyme cleaner for urine, and let it sit a few minutes. Shake off loose hair and debris so it doesn't ride into the wash.

    Wash gentle, in warm water

    Run a gentle or normal cycle in warm water — never hot — with a normal dose of mild detergent. No fabric softener and no bleach (unless the tag explicitly allows it), since both coat or degrade the waterproof membrane.

    Dry on low — never high heat

    Tumble on low or hang it to air-dry; high heat is what melts and cracks the waterproof layer. A couple of wool or rubber dryer balls help a quilted protector fluff and dry evenly without raising the temperature.

    Dry-check, then remake the bed

    Confirm it's bone-dry — including the corners and any thick quilting — then pull it back onto the mattress smooth and snug, with no bunching, before the fitted sheet goes over the top. A flat protector is a comfortable one.

    Signs it's overdue — and when to just replace it

    A protector you forget about is one that's working: clean, quiet, and quietly keeping your mattress safe. These are the signs it's overdue for a wash — and the ones that mean washing won't help anymore and it's time for a new one:

    • The waterproof layer is cracking, peeling, or stiff. Once the backing has gone crinkly, flaky, or hard, the membrane has failed — this is what high-heat washing does. No wash brings it back; replace it.
    • Water no longer beads on it. Splash a little water on a clean, dry spot — if it soaks straight in instead of beading and rolling off, the protector has stopped being waterproof and needs replacing.
    • A smell that won't wash out. If a sour or musty odor survives a proper warm wash (try an enzyme cleaner and a vinegar soak first), the fabric is saturated past the point of cleaning. Time for a fresh one.
    • Tears, thin spots, or stretched-out elastic. Holes and worn-thin patches let everything through, and slack corner elastic that won't grip the mattress means it shifts and bunches all night. Both are replacement signals.
    • It's just old. Even cared-for, most waterproof protectors last about 1–3 years before the membrane wears out. If yours is in that range and showing any of the above, don't nurse it — a new one is $25–60 and protects a much pricier mattress.
    • Good news — there's rarely a "pro" to call. This is fully a do-it-yourself task: wash it right, and replace it when it's spent. The only related pro job is replacing the mattress underneath if a failed protector let it get soaked beyond saving.
    • It's part of a bigger routine. Wash it with the bedding, and pair it with the other mattress care — rotating the mattress and knowing when to replace it. Bulkier bedding like the duvet and pillows run on their own, longer schedules.

    Common questions

    How often should I wash my mattress protector?

    The simple rule is to wash it with your sheets — roughly every two weeks — and once a month at the very minimum. That keeps the sweat, skin cells, and dust-mite food from building up faster than you clear it. Several things push the interval shorter: if anyone in the bed has allergies or asthma, if you're a hot or sweaty sleeper, if pets sleep on the bed or you co-sleep with kids, or if spills and accidents are common, lean toward weekly. A barely-used guest-bed protector, by contrast, can stretch to every four to eight weeks. And always wash it promptly after any accident or spill — that's its whole job, but a caught spill still needs rinsing out before it sours or wicks through to the mattress.

    Can I put it in the dryer — and at what heat?

    Yes, most waterproof protectors can go in the dryer, but the setting is critical: tumble on low heat, or use the air-dry/no-heat cycle, or hang it up. The waterproof backing is plastic-based, and a hot dryer is the single most common way protectors get ruined — the heat melts, cracks, and peels that membrane. Drying on low simply takes a little longer, especially for thick quilted protectors that hold a lot of water; be patient rather than turning up the heat to speed it up. A couple of wool or rubber dryer balls help it tumble and dry evenly. Whatever you do, make sure it comes out completely dry before it goes back on the bed, or it'll trap moisture and grow mildew.

    Will hot water or high heat really ruin the waterproofing?

    Yes — this is the one mistake that genuinely kills a protector. The waterproof layer is a thin film of TPU or polyurethane bonded to the fabric, and like most plastics it can't take much heat. Hot wash water and especially a high-heat dryer cause that film to soften, crack, separate, and eventually peel away from the backing fabric, after which the protector no longer repels liquid. It often happens gradually, so people don't connect the dead waterproofing to the hot wash that caused it months earlier. Stick to warm water and low or no heat to dry, and the membrane stays flexible and intact for years. If the tag specifies something different, follow the tag — but you'll almost never see one that asks for hot water or high heat.

    Can I use fabric softener or bleach?

    Skip both unless the care tag specifically says otherwise. Fabric softener (and dryer sheets) leave behind a waxy coating designed to soften fibers — but on a protector that coating clogs the waterproof membrane, reduces its breathability, and can keep liquid from beading and rolling off the way it should. A protector should never feel "softened." Bleach is also off the table for most protectors: it breaks down both the waterproof layer and the elastic over time, and can yellow the fabric. For normal washing, a regular amount of mild liquid detergent is all you need — there's no benefit to overdosing it, and excess detergent just leaves residue. For odors, reach for an enzyme cleaner or a white-vinegar soak rather than bleach or scented softeners.

    How do I get sweat smell or stains out?

    Pre-treat before the wash. For a general sweat shadow or yellowing, dab the spot with a little mild detergent or a gentle stain remover and let it sit a few minutes. For odors and for biological stains like urine or sweat that just won't quit, an enzyme cleaner is the right tool — it breaks down the proteins that cause the smell, rather than just masking it. A soak in warm water with a cup of white vinegar (or a scoop of baking soda) before washing also lifts stubborn odor. Then wash warm and gentle, and — this is the part people miss — dry it fully on low heat. A protector put back even slightly damp will smell musty again within days, because trapped moisture is exactly what odor-causing bacteria and mildew need. If a smell survives all of that, the fabric is saturated past saving and it's time to replace it.

    How often should I replace it?

    Even with careful washing, most waterproof mattress protectors last about one to three years. The fabric side holds up fine; it's the waterproof membrane that wears out first. Replace it as soon as you notice the backing cracking, peeling, or going stiff, water no longer beading on a clean spot, a smell that won't wash out, tears or thin patches, or elastic too stretched to grip the mattress. The easy test: splash a little water on a clean, dry area — if it beads and rolls off, it's still doing its job; if it soaks in, the protection is gone. A new protector runs $25–60, which is cheap insurance for an $800–2,000 mattress, so don't try to nurse a failed one along.
