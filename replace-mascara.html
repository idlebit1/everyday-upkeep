How Often to Replace Mascara

Of everything in your makeup bag, mascara has the shortest safe life — and it's not close. The tube is warm, wet, dark, and airtight, and every single day you plunge a wand loaded with lash bacteria back inside it, so it's less a cosmetic than a tiny, near-perfect incubator. Left too long, that's where real eye infections come from: pink eye, styes, and in rare cases worse. The rule is simple and worth keeping: replace it about every three months from the day you open it — sooner if it dries, clumps, or smells off — and treat the habits around it (no pumping, no water, no sharing) as part of the deal.

🗓️ Replace every ~3 months 👁️ Shortest-life item in your bag 🦠 Avoids real eye infections 💸 Just ~$6–30 a tube

Why it matters

Most makeup ages slowly and mostly cosmetically — a lipstick that's a bit past its best is still just lipstick. Mascara is the exception, and it's a big one: it sits right against your eye, it's designed to be wet and dark and sealed, and it gets re-inoculated with bacteria every day you use it. Here's why the three-month rule earns its place:

The tube is a bacteria incubator

Warm, wet, dark, and airtight — and re-seeded with bacteria straight off your lashes every single day. That's a near-perfect breeding ground, which is exactly why mascara goes "off" faster than almost anything else you own.

A perfect place for germs to grow

Old mascara causes eye infections

Because it's applied right at the lash line, a contaminated tube can seed pink eye (conjunctivitis), styes, and irritation — and in rare cases more serious corneal problems. It's the one product where "past its prime" can mean an actual infection.

Pink eye, styes & irritation

It stops performing anyway

Even setting the germs aside, old mascara just works worse: the formula dries out, clumps, flakes onto your cheeks, and can take on a faintly odd smell. It literally looks worse the older it gets — so there's no upside to hanging on.

Dry, clumpy, flaky & smelly

Bad habits make it worse

Pumping the wand in and out shoves air and bacteria deep into the tube and dries it faster; adding water, saline, or drops to "revive" it just injects more germs; and sharing spreads infection from eye to eye. Small habits, big difference.

Pumping, watering & sharing

How often should you replace yours?

The standard rule is about three months from the day you open a tube — that's the eye-safety baseline, and it's already short. Sensitive eyes, a history of eye infections, or habits like pumping and watering pull it sooner; the moment it dries, smells, or clumps, it's a toss no matter what the calendar says. Answer four quick questions:

1. How are your eyes — sensitive or hardy?
Sensitive eyes, contact lenses, or any history of styes or pink eye all raise the stakes if a tube goes bad — worth replacing on the earlier side and being extra careful with hygiene.
2. Do you pump the wand or add water to revive it?
Pumping the wand and topping the tube up with water, saline, or eye drops both drive air and bacteria in and dry it out — habits that shorten a tube's safe life and push replacement sooner.
3. What state is your current tube in?
Any drying, clumping, flaking, or a funny smell means it's already past it — that's a toss-now signal that overrides the calendar entirely, no matter how new the tube feels.
4. Regular or waterproof formula?
Waterproof formulas tend to dry out and stiffen faster in the tube, so they often reach the end of their usable life a little sooner than a regular mascara.

    The cost of skipping it

    A tube of mascara runs about $6 to $30, so replacing it every three months is a small, predictable line item — a few dollars a month at most. Set that against the misery and expense of an actual eye infection, and the three-month rule isn't just the safe choice, it's the cheap one.     How to keep mascara safe — and know when to swap it

    There isn't much to "do" with mascara except use it well and replace it on time — but the habits matter more here than with anything else in your bag. Read the safety points first (especially the "never add water" and "don't pump" rules), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Eye-infection basics — general info, not medical advice

    • Replace mascara about every 3 months from first use. It's the shortest-life item in your makeup bag, and the reason is the eye-infection risk — not just performance. When in doubt, three months is the number to remember.
    • Write the open date on the tube. Mark the day you first used it, or note the PAO "open jar" symbol (often 6M) as an outer limit — but mascara specifically wants about three months, well inside that.
    • Never share mascara or use store testers. A shared or tester wand is one of the fastest ways to pass an eye infection from one person to the next. Keep your tube strictly your own.
    • Never add water, saline, or eye drops to revive dried mascara. It doesn't refresh the formula — it just injects more moisture and bacteria into an already-contaminated tube. Dried out means replace, not top up.
    • Don't pump the wand in and out. Pumping pushes air and bacteria deep into the tube and dries it faster. Instead, swirl the wand gently against the sides to load it.
    • Toss it at the first bad sign. If it dries, clumps, flakes, or smells odd, replace it immediately — those are the tube telling you it's done, regardless of the date.
    • After any eye infection, throw it away. Bin the mascara (and any eye makeup used alongside it) and don't reuse it, or you risk re-infecting yourself from the same tube.
    • Take extra care with contacts or sensitive eyes, and see a doctor for symptoms. Remove eye makeup before bed, and for any redness, pain, or discharge, see an eye doctor — that's not something a makeup swap fixes.

    📐 Five things worth knowing

    Date it

    Write the day you open it right on the tube (or set a phone reminder), then count roughly three months ahead. A dated tube takes all the guesswork out of "wait, how old is this?"

    Apply without wrecking it

    Swirl the wand against the inside of the tube to load it — never pump it in and out. Pumping drives air and bacteria down into the formula and dries it out fast.

    Keep it clean & solo

    Never share your mascara, never use store testers, and never add water, saline, or drops to thin it out. Each of those is a direct route for bacteria into the tube — and into your eye.

    Know the toss signs

    Dry, clumpy, flaky, an off smell, or any eye infection means replace it now — don't wait for the date. And after an infection, replace all the eye makeup you used with it, not just the mascara.

    Swap and reset

    Bin the old tube, open the fresh one, write the new open date on it, and note when the next replacement is due. That closes the loop so you're never guessing again.

    Signs to replace it now — and when to see a doctor

    The three-month clock is the default, but your tube (and your eyes) will often tell you sooner. Any one of these means replace it right away — and a couple of them mean it's time to stop reaching for makeup and call an eye doctor:

    • Dried-out, clumpy, or flaky formula. When it stops going on smoothly, clumps on the wand, or flakes down onto your cheeks, it's past its usable life — and no, you can't fix it by adding water. Replace it.
    • A strange or chemical smell. Fresh mascara barely smells like anything. A sour, chemical, or just "off" odor is a clear sign the formula has broken down and bacteria are at work — toss it.
    • Irritation, redness, or watering when you wear it. If your eyes sting, water, or go red when you apply a mascara that used to be fine, stop using that tube. Something in it is bothering your eyes, and pushing through invites worse.
    • Any eye infection — past or present. Had a stye, pink eye, or any eye infection recently? Throw the mascara out (and the eye makeup used with it) so you don't re-infect yourself from the same tube. This isn't optional.
    • It's simply been about three months. Even if it looks and smells fine, three months from opening is the sensible outer limit for eye safety. If you can't remember when you opened it, that alone is a good reason to start fresh.
    • See an eye doctor for pain, redness, or discharge. Eye pain, persistent redness, swelling, or any discharge is not a makeup problem to troubleshoot — it needs a professional. Stop wearing eye makeup and get it looked at, especially if you wear contacts.
    • Pro tip: the same makeup-hygiene logic applies to your makeup brushes, and eye hygiene ties directly to your contact lenses — treat all three as one connected routine and your eyes stay a lot happier.

    Common questions

    How often should I replace mascara?

    About every three months from the day you first open and use it — that's the widely recommended rule, and it's shorter than almost anything else in your makeup bag for a reason. The three-month window is really an outer limit for eye safety, not a guarantee; if the mascara dries out, clumps, flakes, or takes on an odd smell before then, replace it sooner. A helpful habit is to write the open date on the tube (or note the PAO "open jar" symbol on the packaging, often 6M — but treat that as a maximum, since mascara specifically wants about three months). And any time you've had an eye infection, throw the current tube out regardless of how new it is.

    Why does mascara expire so fast?

    Because the tube is close to a perfect incubator and it sits right against your eye. It's warm, wet, dark, and airtight, and every time you use it you draw the wand across your lashes — picking up skin bacteria and oil — and plunge it straight back into that sealed, moist environment. Do that daily and you're re-inoculating the formula with germs over and over. Combine that with the fact that mascara is applied at the lash line, one of the more vulnerable spots on your body, and you get the reason it has the shortest safe life of your cosmetics: an old tube isn't just stale, it can genuinely carry enough bacteria to cause an eye infection. This is general information, not medical advice — but it's why the three-month rule exists and why it's worth following.

    Can I add water or drops to revive dried-out mascara?

    No — this is one of the most important don'ts. Adding water, saline, or eye drops to a drying tube doesn't restore the formula; it just adds moisture and pushes more bacteria into a product that already lives in a germ-friendly environment, turning a merely dried-out tube into a genuinely contaminated one you're about to put next to your eye. It can also change how the mascara performs and dries. If your mascara has dried out enough that you're tempted to thin it, that's simply the tube telling you it's reached the end of its life — the right move is to replace it, not top it up. A fresh tube is only a few dollars; an eye infection is not.

    Is it bad to pump the mascara wand?

    Yes, and it's a common habit worth breaking. Pumping the wand in and out of the tube feels like it loads more product, but what it actually does is push air deep into the formula — which dries it out faster — and drive bacteria from the wand down into the tube with every plunge. Both shorten the mascara's safe life and make it clumpier sooner. Instead, to load the wand, slowly swirl it against the inside walls of the tube; that picks up plenty of product without the air-and-bacteria pumping action. If you find you have to pump hard just to get product on the brush, that's usually a sign the tube is drying out and it's time to replace it rather than work harder to squeeze more life out of it.

    Can I share mascara or use store testers?

    No — sharing mascara or using in-store testers is one of the fastest ways to pass an eye infection from one person to another. A wand that's touched someone else's lashes (or dozens of strangers', in the case of a tester) can carry bacteria straight to your own eye, and conditions like conjunctivitis and styes spread this way easily. Treat your mascara as strictly personal, the same way you would a toothbrush. If you want to try a shade in a store, use a fresh disposable wand and apply to the back of your hand, never to your lashes from a communal tube. And within your own household, don't lend your mascara out either — the infection risk doesn't care that you're related.

    What happens if I use expired mascara?

    Two things, and one of them matters a lot more than the other. The mild problem is performance: old mascara dries out, clumps, flakes onto your face, and can smell off, so it simply looks worse. The serious problem is your eyes. An old, bacteria-laden tube applied at the lash line can lead to eye infections such as conjunctivitis (pink eye) and styes, along with general irritation, redness, and watering — and in rare cases, more serious problems like a corneal infection, which is a genuine risk to your sight. You won't always get an infection from one use of a slightly old tube, but the risk climbs the longer you keep it, and it's simply not worth it when a replacement is so cheap. If you ever develop eye pain, persistent redness, swelling, or discharge, stop using eye makeup and see an eye doctor — this page is general guidance, not medical advice.
