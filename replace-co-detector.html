Replace Your CO Detector

Here's the part almost no one knows: the carbon monoxide sensor inside your detector quietly wears out in about five to seven years, and after that the unit can still power on, still light up, still beep when you press test — while detecting nothing at all. The test button only checks the horn and the battery, never the sensor. So the one thing that actually keeps an invisible, odorless gas from harming your family is replacing the whole unit by the "replace by" date stamped on its back. This isn't testing it, and it isn't changing the battery. It's the deadline you've probably never looked for.

🗓️ Replace every 5–7 years (combo units ~10) 🔘 The test button doesn't check the sensor 💸 New unit ~$20–40 ☠️ CO is invisible & odorless

Why it matters

A carbon monoxide detector is the rare device that fails in total silence. It doesn't go dark, it doesn't throw an error — it just stops being able to do the one thing you bought it for, while looking and sounding completely fine. Here's why "replace it on a date" matters more than any other CO-alarm habit:

CO is invisible and deadly

Carbon monoxide has no color, no smell, and no taste — people fall asleep and don't wake up. A dead sensor gives you zero warning while you go on assuming you're protected. The only thing standing between your family and the gas is a working detector, so "working" can't be a guess.

A silent gap in your only warning

The sensor wears out silently

A CO sensor is a chemical cell that degrades whether or not it ever sees any gas — most are rated for about 5–7 years. Past that point it simply stops detecting, even though the unit still looks new, still lights up, and still tests "fine." Nothing tells you it's gone blind.

Looks & tests fine — detects nothing

The test button can't prove it

Pressing TEST confirms the battery, the electronics, and the horn — it does not expose the sensor to carbon monoxide, so it can't tell you the sensor still works. A passing test on an expired unit is a false sense of safety. Date-based replacement is the only real safeguard.

Test ≠ proof the sensor works

Battery swaps hide the deadline

Most people dutifully change the battery for years and never realize the whole detector expired underneath their hand. A fresh battery makes a dead-sensor unit feel cared-for and current — so the house sits unprotected while everyone believes the opposite.

Cared-for battery, expired sensor

How often should you replace yours?

A standalone CO detector lasts about 5–7 years; a combination smoke/CO unit often runs closer to 10. But two things override the rule of thumb: the date printed on the unit, and an end-of-life chirp — either one means replace now, no matter the math. Answer four quick questions:

1. What kind of detector is it?
A standalone CO detector typically lasts about 5–7 years; a combination smoke/CO alarm usually about 10. If you're not sure, read the label on the front or back — and when in doubt, plan for the shorter life.
2. Can you read a date on it?
Take the unit down and look at the back or side. A manufacture date plus the rated life tells you the deadline. No readable date at all almost always means the unit is old enough to replace on principle.
3. How is it behaving — and do you remember installing it?
A unit chirping its end-of-life signal is telling you in plain language that it's done — that's not a battery, it's the whole alarm. And if you can't recall replacing it, assume it's older than you'd like.
4. Does your home burn fuel?
A furnace, water heater, gas range, fireplace, wood stove, or an attached garage all produce CO — which raises the stakes of an expired detector and makes replacing on time non-negotiable.

    How to replace your CO detector

    The whole job is short: read the date, tell the chirp apart from a low battery, buy the right unit, mount and test it, then do it across the house and write the dates down. Read the safety notes first — especially what a real alarm means and what the test button does not prove — then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Read this first — this is a life-safety device

    • If a CO alarm ever sounds for real, get everyone outside to fresh air immediately and call 911 or the fire department. Do not ignore it, silence it, and stay. Don't go back in until responders say it's safe — then have your fuel appliances inspected before you run them again.
    • The test/silence button only checks the horn and battery — not the sensor. A unit can pass the button test and be blind to CO. The only way to know the sensor still works is to replace the whole unit by the date stamped on it — typically about 5–7 years for CO, and roughly 10 for combo and smoke alarms.
    • Learn the difference between a low-battery chirp and an end-of-life chirp. A low-battery chirp means replace the battery; an end-of-life chirp means replace the whole unit. The patterns differ by brand — check the manual so you don't throw out a good alarm or keep a dead one.
    • Place CO detectors on every level and near each sleeping area — but follow the manual on distance from fuel-burning appliances and the garage. Too close and you'll get false alarms; the manufacturer's placement guidance wins.
    • Never pull a battery to stop a chirp and forget about it. A silenced alarm is no alarm at all. If it's chirping low-battery, swap the battery; if it's end-of-life, replace the unit that day.
    • Have working smoke alarms too. CO and fire are two separate threats. A combo unit covers both, or keep separate smoke alarms on their own replacement clock (~10 years) alongside your CO coverage.

    📐 Five things worth knowing

    Find the date on the back

    Take the unit down and read the manufacture or "replace by" date on the back or side. Most CO detectors are rated about 5–7 years from manufacture; combo units roughly 10. If there's no readable date at all, it's old enough — replace it.

    Know the chirp

    Match the beep pattern to the manual. A single occasional chirp usually means low battery — change the battery. A distinct, repeating end-of-life pattern means the unit itself is done. A detector chirping end-of-life is finished, battery or not.

    Buy the right replacement

    Pick a CO or combo smoke/CO detector to match each location — ideally a 10-year sealed-battery model, which never needs a battery and dies and gets replaced on one clean clock. Get the right type for each spot before you start pulling old units down.

    Install it and test the horn

    Mount the new unit per the manual — the right level and the right distance from fuel appliances and the garage. Write the install date on it, then press TEST to confirm the horn sounds. Now you have a known-good alarm and a known clock.

    Do the house & log the dates

    Replace every expired unit — on each level and near every sleeping area — not just the one that chirped. Write the install date on each, and note the next replacement year somewhere you'll actually see it, so the deadline never sneaks up again.

    Signs it's time — and what counts as an emergency

    Replacing a CO detector is a routine swap on a schedule. These are the signals it's due — and the one situation that isn't maintenance at all but an emergency, every single time:

    • An end-of-life chirp. A distinct, repeating chirp pattern (check the manual to be sure it's not just low battery) means the unit has reached the end of its service life. A new battery won't fix it — replace the whole detector that day.
    • No readable date, or older than its rated life. A standalone CO unit past about 7 years, or a combo/smoke alarm past about 10, is at or beyond the end of its sensor life. If you can't find a date at all, treat it as expired and replace it.
    • It won't pass its horn test. If pressing TEST produces a weak or silent response even after a fresh battery, the unit is failing — replace it. A detector you can't hear isn't protecting anyone.
    • A real CO alarm — the loud, repeating emergency pattern. This is not maintenance. Get everyone, including pets, outside to fresh air and call 911 or the fire department from outside. Don't re-enter until responders clear it, and have your fuel-burning appliances inspected to find the source before you run them again.
    • You're already doing a home-safety round. Replacing a CO detector pairs naturally with testing your CO detectors, checking your smoke detectors, swapping detector batteries, inspecting the fire extinguisher, and testing your GFCI outlets — bundle them and the whole house gets safer in one afternoon.

    Common questions

    How often do CO detectors need to be replaced?

    Most standalone carbon monoxide detectors are rated for about 5–7 years, because the CO sensor is a chemical cell that wears out whether or not it ever detects any gas. Combination smoke/CO units often last closer to 10 years, and standalone smoke alarms are generally a 10-year item too. The exact deadline is the "replace by" date printed on the unit — replace the whole detector by that date, even if it still powers on and passes a button test. If there's no readable date, the unit is old enough to replace on principle.

    Doesn't the test button prove it works?

    No — and this is the single most important thing to understand about CO detectors. The test button verifies the battery, the electronics, and the horn. It does not expose the sensor to carbon monoxide, so it cannot confirm the sensor still detects CO. A unit with a dead sensor will happily pass the button test and beep right on cue while detecting nothing. That's exactly why every unit carries an expiration date, and why date-based replacement — not a passing test — is what actually keeps you protected.

    What's the difference between a low-battery chirp and an end-of-life chirp?

    Both are intermittent chirps, but they mean very different things. A low-battery chirp — usually a single chirp every minute or so — means swap the battery and the alarm is fine. An end-of-life chirp means the unit itself has reached the end of its service life and must be replaced; a fresh battery won't silence it for long. The two patterns differ by manufacturer (often a different number of chirps or a different interval), so check the unit's manual to tell them apart. The simple rule: if a new battery doesn't stop the chirping, it's end of life — replace the whole detector.

    Where should CO detectors go?

    Put one on every level of the home, including a finished basement, and one near (or inside) every sleeping area so it can wake you. Carbon monoxide mixes fairly evenly with air, so many modern units can be placed at any height — but follow the manufacturer's guidance, which often suggests keeping them at least 10–15 feet from fuel-burning appliances like the furnace, water heater, or stove, and away from the garage, to avoid nuisance trips. Keep them out of dead-air corners and away from very humid or dusty spots. When in doubt, the manual's placement instructions outrank any general rule.

    CO vs combo smoke/CO detectors — which, and how often?

    A standalone CO detector does one job and typically lasts about 5–7 years. A combination smoke/CO unit covers both fire and CO on a single device and usually carries a ~10-year rating — convenient, one less puck on the ceiling, and many announce which threat they've detected. Two things to keep in mind: a combo unit retires on the earlier of its two clocks (the CO sensor often ages out first, so go by the printed replace-by date), and either way you still need enough units to cover every level and every sleeping area. Whichever you choose, replace it by the date stamped on it.

    My CO alarm is going off — what do I do?

    Treat it as real, every time. Get everyone — including pets — out of the house and into fresh air immediately, then call 911 or the fire department from outside. Don't try to "air it out" by opening windows first, don't go searching for the source, and don't silence the alarm and stay. Don't re-enter until emergency responders tell you it's safe. If anyone has a headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, or drowsiness, tell the dispatcher you suspect carbon monoxide. Before running any fuel-burning appliance again, have it inspected to find and fix what caused the alarm.
