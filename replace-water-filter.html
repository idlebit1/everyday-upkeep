Replace Your Fridge Water Filter

Every glass of water and every cube of ice your fridge makes passes through one small cartridge of activated carbon. It pulls the chlorine taste and odor out of your tap water and reduces certain contaminants — until the carbon fills up and quietly stops working. Past that point you're drinking through dead carbon, the dispenser slows to a trickle, and a forgotten filter can even grow bacteria. The fix is a five-minute swap, twice a year.

🗓️ Typically every 6 months ⏱️ ~5 minutes plus a flush 💧 $15–60 for the filter, DIY 💸 Way cheaper than bottled water

Why it matters

The filter is a one-job part: a bed of activated carbon that the water flows through on its way to your glass and ice maker. Carbon has a finite capacity — once it's full, it can't trap anything more. Here's what a spent filter actually does (typical U.S. figures):

Clean-tasting water and ice

The carbon strips the chlorine taste and odor your utility adds, so water from the door tastes like nothing — the way good water should. It also reduces certain contaminants. A fresh filter is the whole reason the dispenser beats the tap.

Better than bottled

A spent filter stops filtering

Once the carbon is saturated it doesn't keep cleaning — it just lets water pass straight through. Worse, a forgotten filter sits damp for months and can become a breeding spot for bacteria and biofilm, so old water is arguably worse than no filter.

No filtering · possible bacteria

The dispenser slows to a trickle

As sediment packs the cartridge, flow drops — the glass that used to fill in a few seconds now dribbles, and the ice maker makes smaller, slower batches. That "weak dispenser" almost everyone blames on the fridge is usually just a clogged filter.

Slow water · small ice

The 6-month / gallon clock

Manufacturers and NSF base the interval on roughly 6 months or a rated number of gallons, whichever comes first. The indicator light and gallon counter exist for exactly this reason. Past the limit, the filter is just a delay in the line — not a filter.

~6 months or by gallons

How often should you replace it?

Every 6 months is the rule of thumb — but the real driver is how much water you pull and what's in your local supply. Heavy use, big households, and sediment-heavy or well water all push the interval shorter. Answer four questions for your schedule:

    How to replace your fridge water filter

    Most filters are a five-minute, no-tools job: locate it, twist or pop the old one out, click the new one in, then flush. The flushing and resetting are the parts people skip — don't. Read the techniques first, then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Get this right and the rest is easy

    • Use a filter certified for your fridge. Look for NSF/ANSI certification and the correct part number for your exact model. An uncertified or wrong-fit filter may not seal, may not actually filter, and can leak.
    • Flush the new filter before you drink from it. Run 2–4 gallons through the dispenser to clear harmless carbon fines (the cloudy or grey first water) and trapped air. Sputtering and spitting at first is completely normal.
    • Reset the filter-status indicator afterward — usually a press-and-hold on the dispenser panel. Otherwise the light keeps nagging and the gallon counter never restarts.
    • Keep a towel handy. A little water usually escapes when the old cartridge comes out — that's expected, not a leak. A few drips, not a flood.
    • You usually don't need to shut off the water. Most in-fridge filters have a self-sealing valve that stops flow the instant the cartridge leaves the housing. If yours doesn't, or it's an inline filter, close the supply valve first.

    📐 Five things worth seeing first

    First, find it

    Common spots: a flip-down compartment inside the top-right of the fridge, the bottom front grille (kick plate), or an upper interior corner. A few are in the back. Your manual names the exact spot in a minute.

    Match the model — and buy certified

    Read the old filter's part number, or look up your fridge's model number. OEM or aftermarket both work — what matters is NSF/ANSI certification and the correct fit. The wrong cartridge won't seal.

    Remove the old one

    Depending on the design, you press a release button, give it a quarter-turn counterclockwise, or pull it straight out. A little water escaping is normal — hold a towel under it. Toss the spent cartridge in the trash.

    Lock the new one in

    Slide the new filter into the housing and push or turn it until it clicks and seats fully — most are a quarter-turn clockwise or a firm push-in. A filter that isn't fully seated is the most common cause of leaks and no flow.

    Flush, then reset the light

    Run 2–4 gallons through the dispenser to clear carbon fines and air — the first water is often cloudy and sputters; that's normal and clears quickly. Then press and hold the filter button to reset the indicator.

    Signs you're overdue — and when to call for service

    A filter rarely fails dramatically; it just quietly stops helping until the water tells on it. These are the tells that yours is spent, plus the handful of cases where it's worth calling someone:

    • The filter-status light is on. The simplest tell — the indicator tracks the months and gallons for you, so when it turns red or amber, the filter has hit its rated life. Replace it and reset the light.
    • Water and ice dispense slower than they used to. Sediment packing the cartridge restricts flow first. A glass that used to fill fast now dribbles, and ice batches shrink — the classic sign of a clogged filter.
    • An off taste or smell is creeping back. When the chlorine taste, a metallic edge, or a musty note returns, the carbon is saturated and no longer scrubbing it out. A fresh filter clears it within a flush or two.
    • Cloudy water or cloudy ice. Trapped air and carbon fines from a brand-new filter clear after flushing — but persistent cloudiness from an old one means it's past due.
    • It's simply been 6+ months. Even with light use, manufacturers and NSF cap the life at roughly six months because a damp, long-idle cartridge can harbor bacteria. The calendar alone is reason enough.
    • When to call for service: if the dispenser leaks after a change, the filter is stuck in the housing, or you get no flow at all, first reseat the filter and confirm the water shutoff is fully open. If a reseat doesn't fix the leak or restore flow, or the housing is cracked, that's an appliance-service call — not a DIY fix.

    Common questions

    How often do I really need to change it?

    About every 6 months or when the rated gallon count runs out — whichever comes first — which is exactly what the indicator light and gallon counter are tracking. Heavy households, lots of ice, and sediment-heavy or well water can push it to every 3–4 months; light use with clean municipal water comfortably reaches the full six. But don't stretch much past six months even on light use: a cartridge that sits damp and idle for a year can grow bacteria, so the calendar caps it regardless of gallons.

    OEM or aftermarket — does it matter?

    What matters is certification, not the logo. A genuine OEM filter (the fridge brand's own part) typically runs $30–60; a good certified aftermarket filter is $15–35 and does the same job — if it carries NSF/ANSI certification and is listed to fit your exact model. Look for NSF/ANSI 42 (taste and odor) and ideally 53 (specific contaminants like lead) on the package. Cheap, uncertified knock-offs are the ones to avoid: they may not seal, may restrict flow, and make claims they've never been tested against.

    Do I really need to flush a brand-new filter?

    Yes — every time. A new carbon filter sheds harmless carbon fines (fine black or grey dust) and traps air during manufacturing, so the first water out is often cloudy and the dispenser sputters and spits. Running 2–4 gallons through clears all of that and primes the cartridge. Skip the flush and your first few glasses look murky and may taste of carbon. The water is safe; it just isn't pleasant until you've flushed it.

    What does the filter actually take out?

    A standard fridge filter is activated carbon, which is excellent at the things you taste and smell: chlorine, its byproducts, and many odors — that's the NSF/ANSI 42 job. Many are also certified to NSF/ANSI 53 for specific health contaminants such as lead, mercury, or certain cysts; the exact list is printed on the box and varies by model. What it generally does not do is soften hard water or remove dissolved minerals, salts, or "everything" — for that you'd need reverse osmosis or a whole-home system. Check your filter's certifications for what yours is rated to reduce.

    My fridge has no filter, or uses an inline filter — what then?

    Plenty of refrigerators don't filter at all, especially basic models or ones without a water/ice dispenser — there's simply nothing to replace, and that's fine. Some setups instead use an inline filter: a cartridge spliced into the water supply line behind or under the fridge rather than inside it. Those follow the same roughly-6-month rhythm, but you'll usually shut off the supply valve, relieve the pressure, and twist the cartridge off its quick-connect fittings. Same idea, just behind the appliance instead of in it.

    Can I run the fridge without a filter at all?

    Usually yes. Many refrigerators ship with — or sell — a bypass plug: a dummy cartridge that lets unfiltered water flow straight to the dispenser and ice maker when no filter is installed. If you're between filters, that keeps the water and ice working (just unfiltered) and prevents leaks. A few models will reduce or stop dispenser flow without either a filter or the bypass plug installed, so check your manual. Long term, of course, an actual filter is the point — but the bypass is a fine stopgap.

    The light resets — your memory doesn't.

    You just reset the indicator, but six months from now the only reminder is a slow trickle and a faint taste creeping back.
