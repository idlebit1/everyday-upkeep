Test Your Sump Pump

It sits quietly in a pit in the floor, and for months it does absolutely nothing — which is exactly the problem. The sump pump is the one machine standing between a heavy rain and a flooded basement, and when it fails, it fails silently. You don't find out it's dead until the worst possible moment: the night of the storm, with water rising over your shoes. The fix is almost insultingly simple. Pour a five-gallon bucket of water into the pit, watch the float rise, listen for the pump to kick on, see the water pump out and the pump shut off again. Two minutes, four times a year, and you'll never be surprised by a pump that quietly quit on you.

🗓️ Typically every 3 months ⏱️ ~2–5 min to test 🌧️ Saves you from a flooded basement 💸 Costs $0 to test

Why it matters

A sump pump is insurance you can't see working — it earns its keep on maybe a handful of stormy nights a year, and the rest of the time it just waits. The trouble is that a waiting pump and a dead pump look exactly alike from across the room. You only learn which one you have when the water comes, and by then it's far too late to do anything but bail. Here's what's actually at stake:

A dead pump floods the basement

When the pump fails and the rain keeps coming, the water has nowhere to go but up — across the floor, into the drywall, and over everything you store down there. A flooded basement means thousands in cleanup, ruined belongings, and mold that takes hold within a day or two.

Thousands in damage & mold

It fails silently

There's no warning light, no beep, no smell — a sump pump gives no sign that it's stopped working. So you discover the failure the one time you can't afford to: mid-storm, water rising, with no chance to swap a pump or call anyone in time. A quick test is the only way to find out before the weather does.

You find out mid-storm

The fixes are cheap & quick

Most "dead" pumps aren't dead at all. The usual culprits are a float stuck against the pit wall, a pit clogged with gravel and silt, a discharge line that's frozen or disconnected, or a tripped GFCI that cut the power. Every one of those is a two-minute catch — if you go looking before the storm does.

Five-minute problems, missed

Storms knock out the power

The cruel irony: the same storm that floods the pit is the one most likely to cut your power — so the pump goes dark exactly when you need it most. That's why a battery backup (or a water-powered second pump) matters, and why testing it is part of the job, not an afterthought.

No power, no pump, full pit

How often should you test it?

Every three months — and always right before your wet or storm season — is the sensible default. But a finished basement full of things you can't replace, a high water table that keeps the pit working hard, an old pump, or no backup all push toward testing monthly. A dry crawlspace with a newer pump can relax a little. Answer four quick questions:

1. What's down there, and how wet does it get?
The more a flood would cost you — finished space, stored valuables — and the harder the pit works in a high water table or wet climate, the more often testing pays off.
2. How old is the pump?
Sump pumps last roughly ten years, and failure odds climb steeply at the end. An old or unknown-age pump deserves more frequent testing — and a plan to replace it before it quits.
3. How rough is your weather?
Downpours and rapid snowmelt are when the pump runs flat-out, and hard freezes can lock up a discharge line. The bigger the storms, the more it's worth checking ahead of them.
4. Do you have a backup if the pump or power fails?
Without a backup, a single failure or a power cut means a flooded basement. If the pump is your only line of defense, test it more often — and consider adding one.

    The cost of skipping it

    The test itself is free — a bucket of water and two minutes. Even fixing what you find is cheap: a replacement pump runs about $100–300 plus install, a battery backup $150–400.     How to test your sump pump

    The test is the heart of it: pour water into the pit and watch the whole cycle happen — float rises, pump starts, water pumps out, pump shuts off. Around that, you check the things that quietly fail: the float, the discharge line, the pit itself, and the backup. It's a natural pairing with flushing the water heater — the other basement-plumbing check worth doing while you're already down there. Read the techniques first (especially the safety box — this is water and electricity together), then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Water and electricity — work carefully

    • Keep your hands dry and out of the pit when the power is on. The pump is plugged into a GFCI outlet right beside standing water. Never reach into the pit while it's powered — water and electricity together is a real hazard, not a theoretical one.
    • Unplug the pump before clearing debris or touching it. Anytime you're going to put a hand in the pit — to free a stuck float, scoop out gravel, or check the intake — pull the plug first, then plug it back in to test.
    • Check the GFCI and outlet first. A tripped GFCI is one of the most common "dead pump" causes. Press reset on the outlet and confirm it has power before you assume the pump itself has failed — it's worth testing that GFCI on its own schedule too, since the pump is only as reliable as the outlet it's plugged into.
    • Lift water buckets with your legs. A five-gallon bucket of water weighs about forty pounds. Bend at the knees, keep it close, and don't twist while you pour — basements have plenty of edges to trip on.
    • Don't run the pump dry. The pump is cooled by the water it moves. Letting it run dry for more than a moment can burn out the motor, so once the pit is pumped down, let it shut off — don't force it to keep running.
    • Watch your footing on a wet floor. Test water can splash. Wipe up spills as you go and wear shoes with grip — a slick concrete floor is its own hazard.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Pour a bucket and watch the cycle

    Slowly pour five gallons of water into the pit and watch the whole sequence: the float rises, the pump kicks on, the water pumps out, and the pump shuts off by itself. That's a healthy pump. No start, no shut-off, or a strain to move water all mean something needs attention.

    Check the float moves freely

    The float is the switch — when it floats up, the pump turns on. A float jammed against the pit wall, snagged on the pump, or tangled in its own cord can't rise, so the pump never starts no matter how high the water gets. Give it a gentle lift by hand and confirm it swings clear.

    Inspect the discharge line

    Trace the line that carries water out. It should be clear and unfrozen, drain well away from the foundation (not pooling right back against it), and have a working check valve so the water it pumps out doesn't just drain back into the pit. A clogged or frozen line means the pump runs but the basement still floods.

    Clean debris out of the pit

    Gravel, silt, and grit settle in the bottom of the pit and can clog the pump's intake or jam the float. With the pump unplugged, scoop or shop-vac the muck out so the intake stays clear and the float has room to move. A clean pit is a pump that can actually do its job when it counts.

    Test the backup & the outlet

    Confirm the battery backup (or water-powered second pump) actually kicks in — unplug the main pump and run the test, or use the backup's self-test. Make sure the GFCI outlet is live and that the pump is plugged directly into it, not into a switched outlet or a daisy-chained power strip someone could accidentally cut.

    Signs your pump is in trouble — and when to call a pro

    A healthy pump kicks on, clears the pit, and shuts off — quietly, occasionally, and completely. These are the signals that something's wrong, plus the cases that are a plumber's job, not a bucket-of-water test:

    • It runs constantly or short-cycles. A pump that never seems to rest, or that clicks on and off every few seconds, usually has a stuck float, a too-small pit, a failed check valve letting water drain back in, or a pump that's wearing out. Constant running burns it out fast — get it diagnosed.
    • It won't shut off. If the pit is empty and the pump keeps running, the float is stuck in the "on" position or the switch has failed. Running dry destroys the motor, so unplug it and sort out the float or switch before it does.
    • It hums but doesn't move water. A pump that buzzes or hums without pumping may have a jammed impeller, an airlock, or a seized motor. Sometimes clearing debris frees it; often it means the pump is done.
    • It's rusty, loud, or simply old. Sump pumps last about ten years. A rusty, rattly, or decade-old pump is living on borrowed time — replace it on your schedule, not the storm's.
    • The discharge dumps right at the foundation. If the line drains water a foot from the wall, it just soaks back down to the pit and the pump keeps re-pumping the same water. A pro (or a length of pipe) can route it well away from the house.
    • Frequent flooding, a wet basement, or a pit that fills constantly. If the pump runs nonstop even in dry weather, or the basement keeps taking on water, you may have a drainage or grading problem the pump alone can't solve. A plumber or waterproofing pro should look at the whole system.
    • Pro tip: add a battery backup and a simple water alarm in the pit. The backup keeps the pump running when the storm cuts your power, and a $15 alarm screams the moment water rises past where it should — so you hear about a problem before the floor does. While you're thinking about keeping water out, pair this with cleaning the gutters so roof runoff is carried away from the foundation instead of straight down to the pit, and keep your emergency kit updated for the storm that does knock the power out.

    Common questions

    How often should I test my sump pump?

    Test it about every three months, and always right before your wet or storm season — the spring thaw, the summer storm months, or whenever your region tends to get its heavy rain. Quarterly testing catches the slow failures (a pump wearing out, a float drifting out of position) while there's still time to fix them in calm weather. If you have a finished basement, a high water table, an older pump, or no backup, tighten that up to monthly. The whole point is to never let the storm be the thing that tells you the pump stopped working.

    How do I actually test it?

    Pour water in and watch the cycle. Fill a five-gallon bucket and pour it slowly into the pit until the water rises. As it does, the float should lift, the pump should switch on, the water should pump out through the discharge line, and the pump should shut off again once the pit is low. That's the whole test, and a healthy pump runs the sequence smoothly. While you're there, lift the float by hand to confirm it moves freely, glance at the discharge line to be sure it's clear and aimed away from the house, and check that the outlet and GFCI are live. The whole thing takes about two minutes.

    Why won't my pump shut off — or why does it run constantly?

    A pump that runs nonstop is almost always one of a few things. Most common is a float stuck in the "on" position — jammed against the pit wall, snagged on the pump, or tangled in the cord — so the switch never tells it to stop. Next is a failed check valve: without it, the water the pump pushes up the pipe just drains straight back into the pit, so the pump keeps re-pumping the same water forever. It can also mean the pit is undersized, the water table is genuinely high, or the pump is wearing out. Running dry or running constantly both burn out the motor, so unplug it, free or reposition the float, check the check valve, and if it still won't behave, call a plumber.

    Do I really need a battery backup?

    If a flooded basement would be a serious problem for you, yes. The catch with sump pumps is that the storms most likely to flood your pit are also the ones most likely to knock out your power — so the pump dies right when you need it. A battery backup ($150–400) keeps it running for hours on its own power; a water-powered backup pump runs off your home's water pressure with no battery to maintain. Either one turns "the power went out and the basement flooded" into a non-event. Pair it with a cheap water alarm in the pit and you've covered both the silent-failure and the no-power scenarios.

    Where should the water discharge to?

    Well away from the house — at least ten feet from the foundation, onto ground that slopes away so the water keeps moving instead of soaking back down to the pit. If the line dumps right at the wall, the pump ends up pumping the same water over and over, working itself to death and never really clearing the basement. Make sure the outlet isn't buried, crushed, or pointed at a low spot, and in cold climates check it isn't frozen, since a frozen discharge line backs water right up into the pit. Don't run it into a sanitary sewer where that's prohibited — route it to a yard, a storm drain, or a dry well per your local code.

    The pump runs but no water moves — or it has no power at all. What's wrong?

    If it has no power, start at the outlet: a tripped GFCI is one of the most common "dead pump" calls, so press reset and confirm the outlet is live, and make sure the pump is plugged directly in rather than into a switched outlet or power strip that got bumped off. If it has power and hums but moves no water, the likely causes are a jammed or worn impeller, an airlock, a clogged intake, or a frozen/blocked discharge line — clear the pit and the line, and if it still won't pump, the motor may be done. And bear in mind the lifespan: sump pumps last about ten years, so if yours is around that age and acting up, replacing it is often smarter than chasing the fault. When in doubt, a plumber can diagnose it in one visit.
