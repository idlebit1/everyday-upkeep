Mulch Your Garden Beds

A two-to-three-inch blanket of mulch is the cheapest, highest-leverage thing you can do for a garden bed. It smothers weeds before they start, holds moisture so you water less, and keeps roots from cooking in July and freezing in January. Spread it right — at the right depth, kept off the trunks — and it quietly feeds the soil as it breaks down. Here's how much, how often, and the mistakes that turn a good idea into rot.

🗓️ Refresh once a year 📏 Keep it 2–3 in deep 💸 Bulk runs ~$30–50/yard 🌸 Late spring is the classic time

Why it matters

Bare soil isn't a neutral starting point — it's a problem in progress. It crusts over, sheds water, bakes the roots underneath, and sprouts weeds the moment light hits it. A layer of mulch reverses all of that at once. Here's what those 2–3 inches actually buy you:

Weeds can't get started

Most weed seeds need light and open ground to germinate. A 2–3 inch mulch layer blocks the light and smothers what does sprout — far less hand-pulling all season.

Hours of weeding, saved

Soil stays moist

Mulch slows evaporation dramatically, so the ground under it holds water for days longer. You water less often, and the plants ride out dry spells without wilting.

Lower summer water bills

Roots ride out temperature swings

The mulch layer insulates like a blanket — keeping roots cooler in summer heat and buffering them from the freeze-thaw cycles that heave young plants out of the ground in winter.

Fewer winter-killed plants

Organic mulch feeds the soil

Bark, wood, leaves, and straw slowly break down into the soil, building organic matter, feeding earthworms and microbes, and improving structure year after year — for free.

Healthier soil over time

The beds just look finished

Nothing makes a yard look cared-for faster than fresh, dark mulch with clean-cut edges. It's the landscaping equivalent of making the bed — instant tidiness for an afternoon's work.

Curb appeal, cheaply

How often should you re-mulch?

Once a year is the honest default — organic mulch slowly decomposes and thins out, so beds need topping up to stay at that magic 2–3 inches. But your mulch type, where you're starting from, and what you're after all nudge the answer. Three quick questions:

1. What kind of mulch is on (or going on) your beds?
Hint: if it breaks down and changes color over a season, it's organic. Stone and gravel are inorganic and don't decompose.
2. How much mulch is on the beds right now?
3. What's the main thing you want from it?

    How to mulch a garden bed

    Mulching a bed is mostly hauling and raking — an afternoon for a typical yard. The skill is all in the depth and the clearance, not the muscle. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ More is NOT better — and keep it off the trunks

    • Stay at 2–3 inches total — that's the whole game. Piled deeper, mulch suffocates roots, sheds water before it soaks in, and turns into a soggy, matted mess that grows its own weeds.
    • No "mulch volcanoes." Never heap mulch up against tree trunks or plant stems. It traps moisture against the bark, invites rot, insects, and rodents, and slowly kills the tree. Leave a few inches of bare clearance — a flat "donut," never a cone.
    • Refresh and fluff, don't endlessly bury. If the old layer is still 2 inches deep, you only need a thin top-up. Break up and rake the existing mulch first rather than burying it under load after load each year.
    • Don't mulch over weeds you didn't pull. Tough perennial weeds and runners push right through. Clear them first, or you're just hiding the problem.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Mulch (matched to your beds) Wheelbarrow Rake (a hard/bow rake works best) Shovel or pitchfork Gloves Tarp (optional, for hauling/spills) Landscape edging (optional)

    📐 Five moves that get it right

    1. Weed and edge first

    Pull weeds — roots and all — then cut a clean edge around the bed with a spade or edger. A defined border keeps mulch in and grass out, and makes the whole job look intentional.

    2. Fluff the old layer

    If there's existing mulch, rake it loose and level — break up any matted, crusted patches so air and water move through it. This often revives the bed enough that you need very little new mulch on top.

    3. Spread evenly to 2–3 in

    Tip small piles around the bed, then rake them out into one even layer. Aim for a total depth of 2–3 inches — including whatever was already there. Check with a finger or a stick; it's easy to overshoot.

    4. Pull it back from trunks

    Rake mulch a few inches away from tree trunks and plant stems — a flat ring, never a cone. The bark needs to breathe and stay dry. Mulch volcanoes are the single most common (and most damaging) mistake.

    5. Water it to settle

    A light watering settles the fresh mulch, knocks down dust, and helps fine bark knit together so it won't blow or wash around. It also gives the soil underneath a head start before the heat.

    Signs to watch for — and when to bring in help

    • Matted or moldy old mulch. If last year's layer has crusted into a water-shedding mat, rake it apart to break it up — or skim off the worst and start fresh. A whitish mold on the surface is usually harmless and breaks up on its own.
    • Weeds pushing through. A few are normal; a steady flush means the layer is too thin (top up toward 3 inches) or you're fighting aggressive perennials — in which case clear them and consider a thin layer of cardboard or paper under the mulch as a barrier.
    • Odd fungi — "artillery fungus," slime molds, mushrooms. These live on decomposing wood mulch and are mostly cosmetic. Artillery fungus can fling sticky spore specks onto siding and cars; if that's a problem, switch to a bark or large-nugget mulch away from the house and rake the surface occasionally.
    • A sour, ammonia, or rotten-egg smell. "Sour mulch" was stored anaerobically and can harm plants. Spread it out to air for a day or two before using it near anything living.
    • Big jobs, slopes, or steady annual volume. Mulching a whole property is real labor. A bulk delivery saves money on the material; a landscaper saves your back — and both pencil out once you're moving several cubic yards.

    Common questions

    How much mulch do I actually need?

    Multiply depth by area. The rule of thumb: one cubic yard covers about 100 square feet at 3 inches deep (or ~160 sq ft at 2 inches). So a quick estimate is square footage ÷ 100 ≈ cubic yards for a 3-inch layer. In bags, one cubic yard is roughly 13–14 of the standard 2-cubic-foot bags. Measure your beds (length × width, and add up the patches) before you buy — it's the difference between one trip and three.

    How often do I have to re-mulch?

    Plan on once a year for organic mulch — it slowly decomposes, so beds need a top-up to stay at 2–3 inches. In hot climates, or if you're chasing that crisp dark look, twice a year (a spring refresh and a lighter fall one) makes sense. Inorganic rock or gravel doesn't break down, so it rarely needs adding — just the occasional weeding and rinse.

    What's the best type of mulch?

    For most beds, shredded hardwood or bark is the all-rounder: it stays put, looks good, and feeds the soil as it breaks down. Wood chips and bark nuggets last longer but break down slower. Straw and shredded leaves are great (and cheap or free) for vegetable beds. Rock and gravel suit dry/xeric plantings and high-traffic spots but offer no soil benefit and can bake plants in heat. Skip dyed mulch near edibles if the dye source is unknown, and avoid landscape fabric under organic mulch — it clogs and fights you later.

    Should I remove the old mulch before adding new?

    Usually no. Organic mulch is meant to break down into the soil, so removing it throws away the benefit. Instead, rake and fluff the existing layer to break up any matting, then add just enough new mulch to bring the total back to 2–3 inches. Only strip it out if it's deeply matted, moldy, or you've been over-piling for years and the total is already too deep.

    Why can't I pile mulch against tree trunks?

    Because bark isn't built to stay wet. A "mulch volcano" heaped against a trunk traps constant moisture against the bark, which invites rot, fungal disease, boring insects, and gnawing rodents — and it encourages roots to grow up into the mulch instead of down. Over a few years it can quietly kill an established tree. Keep a few inches of clearance and lay mulch in a flat ring, like a donut, not a cone.

    Does mulch really stop weeds?

    Mostly, at the right depth. A solid 2–3 inch layer blocks the light most weed seeds need to germinate and smothers small seedlings — that's the bulk of your weed pressure gone. It won't stop tough perennials or runners that are already established (pull those first), and a too-thin layer lets light through. For stubborn beds, a layer of plain cardboard or newspaper under the mulch adds a temporary barrier that breaks down over the season.
