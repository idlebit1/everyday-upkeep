Replace Your Headphone Earpads

You probably noticed the little black flakes on your ears before you noticed anything else — that's the leatherette on your earpads disintegrating, and by the time it's flaking, the foam underneath has long since flattened. Worn pads don't just look grim; they break the seal against your head, so the bass thins out, the world leaks back in, and you nudge the volume up to compensate. Here's the good news: on most over-ear and on-ear headphones the cushions simply pull, twist, or clip off, and a fresh pair costs a fraction of new headphones. Swap them and a tired old pair feels — and sounds — brand new.

🗓️ Typically every ~2 years ⏱️ ~10–20 min for the pair 🎧 Restores bass & isolation 💸 Costs ~$10–30

Why it matters

Earpads are the one part of a good pair of headphones that's designed to be consumable — they sit against your skin all day, soaking up oils, sweat, and friction — yet most people never think to swap them. Instead the sound and comfort erode so gradually that you just adapt, when a few dollars of fresh foam would bring the whole pair back to life. Here's what worn pads actually cost you:

The foam flattens & the cover flakes

Over months the plush foam compresses into a hard, thin disc, and the leatherette cover starts to crack and peel — that black "flaking" you find on your ears and shoulders is the coating literally disintegrating. It's uncomfortable and it makes a mess.

Hard, flat pads & black flakes everywhere

A broken seal kills the bass

The pad forms an acoustic seal against your head. Once it's flattened or cracked, that seal leaks — so the low end thins out, passive noise isolation drops, and outside sound bleeds in. You end up turning the volume up to make up for foam that gave out.

Weak bass & sound leaking in and out

They soak up sweat & skin oils

Pads press against your skin for hours, so they absorb sweat, oils, and whatever's in the air. Over time they get grimy, discolored, and frankly unhygienic — a smell and a sheen no wipe-down really fixes once the material has broken down.

Grimy, smelly, unhygienic cushions

Fresh pads revive the whole pair

For a few dollars, new pads restore the plush comfort, the seal, the bass, and the looks — all at once. It's the single cheapest upgrade you can make to a set of headphones, and it saves a $150 pair from being retired over a $15 problem.

Living with degraded $100–400 headphones

How often should you replace them?

About every two years is the sensible default for daily-driver headphones, but it swings a lot. Long daily sessions, hot gym or commute use, and cheap leatherette pads all wear out in a year or less, while velour pads on a pair you use occasionally can go three years or more. Answer four quick questions:

1. How hard do you use them?
Every hour against your head compresses the foam and abrades the cover. All-day wear or sweaty gym and commute sessions age pads far faster than the odd hour on the couch.
2. What are the pads made of?
The smooth "leather" covers are a coating over foam, and that coating cracks and flakes within a couple of years. Velour and fabric last longer but flatten and hold odor instead.
3. What kind of shape are they in now?
This is the tie-breaker. No matter the schedule, the moment the foam flakes, flattens, or the seal goes soft, the pads are done — replace them now, then reset the clock.
4. How warm and sweaty is your typical use?
Heat and sweat break down foam and coatings and grime up the surface fastest. Cool, dry desk use is the gentlest life a pad can have.

    How to replace your headphone earpads

    The whole job hinges on two things: getting the right pads for your exact model, and knowing how they attach. Most pull straight off and press, twist, or bayonet-clip back on; some sit in a plastic ring you transfer; a few are glued. Read the techniques first (especially "identify the model" and "note the attachment"), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Get the right pads, and work gently

    • Buy pads that fit your exact model. Earpads are model-specific in size and attachment — order the brand's OEM pads or a reputable compatible set made for your headphones by name. A "close enough" pad won't seat, won't seal, and won't sound right.
    • Know your attachment type before you pull. Most pads pull straight off and press/twist/bayonet-clip back on; some sit in a plastic ring you transfer from the old pad; a handful are glued. Check a teardown or the maker's guide so you don't force the wrong motion.
    • Don't disturb the mesh or foam under the pad. Under the cushion is a thin layer of foam and mesh that protects the driver — leave it intact. Work gently so you don't tear it or crack the plastic cup while prying.
    • Keep moisture away from the driver. If you're cleaning the cup ring, use a barely-damp cloth and let it dry — never let water run into the exposed driver behind the mesh.
    • Glued or sealed? Consider the maker's service. If your pads are glued in, or the headphones are high-end and sealed, prying can damage them. Warm the adhesive gently and pry carefully — or, for premium models, use the manufacturer's pad-replacement service instead of forcing it.
    • Wipe the ring clean, and recycle the old pads. Before fitting the new pad, wipe the cup's mounting ring so the fresh pad seats cleanly. Then bin or recycle the old cushions rather than leaving them to keep shedding flakes.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Identify the model & buy matching pads

    Find your exact model — it's usually printed inside the headband or on the box. Then order the brand's OEM pads or a reputable compatible set made for that model by name. Right size, right attachment, right sound.

    Note how they attach

    Most pads pull straight off and press, twist, or bayonet-clip back on. Some sit in a plastic ring you transfer from the old pad to the new one. A few are glued — those need heat and care, or the maker's service.

    Remove the old pad gently

    Work a finger under the edge and peel or unclip the old pad off all the way around — no yanking. Leave the thin foam and mesh over the driver exactly where it is; that layer protects the driver and stays put.

    Seat the new pad evenly

    Line the new pad up and work it into the groove — or clip it onto the ring — pressing or twisting your way all the way around so it sits flush with no lifted edges. An unevenly seated pad pops off and won't seal.

    Check the seal & clean up

    Put them on and press gently around each cup — the bass should snap back and outside sound should drop. While you're at it, wipe down the headband cushion (the other spot that gets grimy) and bin the old pads.

    Signs your earpads are overdue — and when to call the maker

    Good pads are plush, quiet, and invisible — you just hear the music and forget they're there. These are the signals it's time for a swap, and the handful of cases where the manufacturer's service is the smarter move:

    • Black flakes on your ears, neck, or shoulders. That's the leatherette coating disintegrating. Once it starts flaking it only gets worse, and it never comes back — replace the pads.
    • Cracked or peeling covers. Visible cracks, splits, or lifting patches in the surface mean the cover has failed. It'll shed and feel rough against your skin.
    • Flattened, hard foam. If the cushion is thin and firm instead of plush and springy, the foam is spent — comfort and seal both suffer.
    • A poor seal — weak bass, less isolation. If the low end sounds thin, outside noise leaks in, or you keep nudging the volume up, the pads have stopped sealing. Fresh ones bring the bass and quiet right back.
    • Grimy, discolored, or smelly pads. Once the material has soaked up enough sweat and oils that it looks grungy or smells, cleaning won't recover it — swap them for hygiene as much as comfort.
    • Glued-in or sealed premium models. If the pads are adhered rather than clipped, or the headphones are high-end and sealed, prying risks damage. Many makers sell a pad-replacement service or replacement kit — use it rather than forcing the job.
    • Pro tip: when you buy new pads, consider whether you want the same material or a switch — velour is cooler and more durable, leatherette seals bass better. Either way, fresh pads are the cheapest way to make old headphones feel new.

    Common questions

    How often should I replace my earpads?

    About every two years is a good default for a daily-driver pair, but it depends heavily on how you use them. Long daily sessions, warm or sweaty use (the gym, a hot commute), and smooth leatherette pads all wear out faster — often within a year — while velour or fabric pads on headphones you use occasionally can easily last three years or more. Honestly, the calendar is just a reminder; the real trigger is the pads themselves. The moment the foam flakes, flattens, or the seal goes soft, they're done — replace them then and reset the clock, regardless of how long it's been.

    Why are my earpads flaking and peeling?

    Because the smooth "leather" surface on most earpads isn't leather at all — it's a coating (usually polyurethane, sometimes called protein leather or leatherette) bonded over foam. That coating breaks down over time from skin oils, sweat, sunlight, and friction, and once it starts to degrade it cracks, peels, and sheds those little black flakes onto your ears and shoulders. It's completely normal and it happens to almost every leatherette pad eventually; nothing you do will restore a coating that's already flaking. The fix is simply new pads — and if you're tired of the shedding, velour or fabric pads don't have a coating to flake at all.

    Do worn earpads actually affect the sound?

    Yes, more than most people realize. The pad forms an acoustic seal between the earcup and your head, and that seal is a big part of how closed and over-ear headphones produce bass and block outside noise. When the foam flattens or the cover cracks, the seal leaks: the low end thins out and loses punch, passive noise isolation drops so more of the room bleeds in, and you tend to crank the volume to compensate. Because it happens gradually you adapt without noticing — which is exactly why a fresh pair of pads can sound like an upgrade. The bass snaps back and the outside world goes quiet again.

    How do I know which replacement pads fit?

    Start with your exact model name and number — it's usually printed inside the headband, on the earcup, or on the original box. Then buy either the manufacturer's official (OEM) pads for that model, or a well-reviewed compatible set that explicitly lists your model by name. Earpads are model-specific in both size and attachment method, so a pad that's a few millimeters off, or built for a different clip, won't seat, won't seal, and won't sound right. When in doubt, OEM pads are the safe choice; compatible sets are cheaper and often just as good, but read reviews from people with your exact headphones before you order.

    Are earpads hard to replace myself?

    On most over-ear and on-ear headphones, no — it's a ten-to-twenty-minute job with no tools. The majority of pads simply pull straight off and then press, twist, or bayonet-clip back on; work a finger around the edge to remove the old one, then seat the new one evenly all the way around. Some pads sit in a plastic ring that you transfer from the old pad to the new one. The exceptions are pads that are glued in place or headphones that are sealed shut — those need gentle heat and careful prying, and on premium models it's often wiser to use the maker's pad-replacement service than to risk damage. The one rule throughout: don't disturb the thin mesh and foam protecting the driver underneath, and never force anything.

    Leatherette or velour pads — which should I get?

    It's a trade-off, not a clear winner. Leatherette (protein leather) seals tightly against your head, so it gives you the strongest bass and best passive isolation, and it wipes clean — but the coating cracks and flakes within a couple of years, and it gets warm and sweaty on your ears. Velour or fabric pads are cooler, more breathable, more comfortable for long sessions, and far more durable since there's no coating to degrade — but they seal a little less, so bass can be slightly softer, and they absorb odor over time. If bass and isolation matter most, go leatherette; if all-day comfort and longevity matter most, go velour. Many popular headphones offer both as replacement options, so you can swap the character of your headphones just by changing the pad material.
