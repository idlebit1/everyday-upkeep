Replace Your Door Weatherstripping

You can usually see daylight at the edge of a tired exterior door — a thin bright line where the cracked, flattened weatherstripping used to seal. That gap is heating or cooling the outdoors and inviting in drafts, water, dust, pollen, noise, and even mice. The fix is one of the cheapest, highest-payback chores in the house: do the draft test, peel off the brittle old strip, measure the gap and pick the right type — V-strip for the jamb, foam or EPDM where it compresses, a sweep at the bottom — then press it on and shut the door to make sure it still seals and latches. A few dollars of strip and an hour, and the draft you'd learned to live with simply goes away.

🗓️ Replace every ~2 years ⏱️ ~1 hour per door 🌬️ Stops drafts & energy loss 💸 Costs ~$5–20

Why it matters

A door seal is one of those things that works perfectly until, one cold morning, it simply doesn't — and because it fails so slowly, you stop noticing the draft and just nudge the thermostat instead. But that thin gap around a closed exterior door is doing real work against you, every hour of every day. Here's what a worn-out seal actually costs:

A bad seal is a constant draft & energy drain

A worn or cracked seal leaves a gap that air pours through — so you're paying to heat or cool air that leaks straight back out around the door. It's a slow, invisible leak in your energy bill that runs around the clock, every season.

Heating & cooling the outdoors

Gaps let in water, dust, pollen, noise & pests

The same opening that bleeds heat lets the outside in: wind-driven rain, dust, pollen, street noise, and bugs. And a gap under a door is a mouse's front entrance — they only need about a quarter-inch to squeeze through.

Water, bugs & a mouse's doorway

Weatherstripping is consumable

Sun, cold, and a door slammed thousands of times all flatten, harden, and crack the strip over a few years. A seal that worked beautifully five years ago may compress to nothing today and do absolutely nothing — even though it still looks like it's there.

A seal that's quietly stopped sealing

It's one of the cheapest, highest-payback fixes

Few home repairs return as much for as little. A few dollars of strip and an hour of your time can noticeably cut drafts and trim the heating and cooling bill — there's almost nothing else you can buy at the hardware store that pays back this fast.

Skipping easy savings

How often should you replace it?

There's no calendar rule that beats the draft test, but as a planning default: inspect the seal once a year and expect to replace it about every two years — sooner with cheap foam tape, longer with quality silicone or a metal V-strip. Harsh climates and a heavily used main door wear it out faster. Answer four quick questions for your interval:

1. What's your climate like on this door?
UV from strong sun bakes and cracks the strip, deep freezes make it brittle, and humidity rots and compresses it. A protected door under a porch in a mild climate lasts far longer than one baking on a south wall.
2. How much does this door get used?
Every close compresses the seal, and a door that gets slammed thousands of times a year flattens its weatherstripping much faster than a side door you open once a week.
3. What's the seal doing right now?
The current state is the real trigger. If you can see daylight or feel a draft around the closed door, it doesn't matter what the calendar says — it's due now.
4. What kind of weatherstripping is on it?
Cheap open-cell foam tape flattens and falls off within a year or two; quality silicone, EPDM rubber, and spring-metal V-strip can hold their seal for many years. What it's made of largely sets how often you'll redo it.

    How to replace your door weatherstripping

    The order is simple: find the leaks with a draft test, peel and clean off the old strip, measure the gap and pick the right type, then apply it and shut the door to confirm it still seals and latches. Read the techniques first — especially picking the right type and thickness so the door still closes — then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Work smart — the right type, a clean surface, and a door that still latches

    • Cut away from yourself. Trimming strip with a utility knife or scissors is the one real hazard here — keep your free hand clear, cut on a board not your lap, and use a fresh blade so it slices instead of skips.
    • Pick the right type for each gap. Spring-metal or vinyl V-strip (tension seal) goes on the jamb sides and top, compressible foam or EPDM rubber suits some gaps, a door sweep handles the bottom of the door, and a threshold or door shoe seals the floor gap. The wrong choice either won't seal or won't let the door close.
    • Get the thickness right. Too thin and it won't bridge the gap; too thick and the door fights the latch — and stripping that's always being crushed gets torn off within a week. When in doubt, slightly thinner, and test before you commit the whole length.
    • Clean and dry the surface first. Self-adhesive strip will not stick to dust, grime, old adhesive, or cold metal. Scrape it clean, wipe with a little rubbing alcohol, let it dry, and apply in moderate temperatures — adhesives don't bond well in the cold.
    • Confirm the door closes, latches, and locks without forcing. After installing, shut the door normally. If you have to shove it or the deadbolt won't throw, the stripping is too thick — switch to a thinner profile rather than living with a door that binds.
    • Mind storm doors and metal frames. On a storm door or metal jamb, follow the product's surface notes for adhesion and fasteners. And for a full seal, pair new weatherstripping with caulking the exterior trim gaps around the frame.

    🧰 What you'll need

    New weatherstripping (V-strip, foam/EPDM, or silicone) A door sweep for the bottom A tape measure A utility knife or sharp scissors A putty knife or scraper Rubbing alcohol & a rag A screwdriver or small drill (for a sweep) A candle, incense, or a flashlight (for the draft test)

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Find the leaks — do the draft test

    Feel for moving air along the edges of the closed door, look for daylight around it, and on a windy day pass a candle, a stick of incense, or a lit phone flashlight (with a helper outside at night) along the seam — a flickering flame or a visible beam marks every leak.

    Remove the old strip & clean the surface

    Peel the old weatherstripping off and scrape away the leftover adhesive, dust, and grime with a putty knife. Wipe the bare jamb with a little rubbing alcohol and let it dry — new self-adhesive strip only bonds to a clean, dry surface, never to dirt or old glue.

    Measure & choose the right type

    Measure the gap and the length of each side, then match it: V-strip for the jamb sides and top, foam or EPDM where it needs to compress, a sweep for the bottom. Cut each piece a touch long and trim to a clean fit at the corners.

    Apply it

    Peel the backing and press self-adhesive strip firmly onto the clean jamb as you go, no stretching. For the bottom, screw or nail on a door sweep so it just kisses the threshold — touching enough to seal, but not dragging so hard it scuffs the floor or sticks.

    Test the closure

    Shut the door and check that it seals all the way around — then make sure it still latches and locks easily. If it binds or fights the deadbolt, the strip is too thick: switch to a thinner profile. Finally, re-do the draft test to confirm the leaks are gone.

    Signs it's overdue — and when to call a pro

    A good door seal is invisible: no draft, no daylight, no water, and a door that latches with a soft, solid click. These are the signs the weatherstripping is overdue — plus the handful of problems that aren't a stripping job at all and need a carpenter or door pro:

    • Visible daylight or a felt draft around the closed door. The clearest tell of all — if you can see a bright line at the edge or feel cold air on your hand, the seal has failed and it's due now, no calendar required.
    • Water seeping in along the bottom or sides. Wind-driven rain getting past the door means the sweep, threshold, or jamb seal isn't doing its job — and standing water is the start of swollen trim, a damaged floor, and rot.
    • Bugs or mice getting under or around the door. If insects appear at the threshold or you find evidence of mice, the gap is wide enough to be a doorway. New weatherstripping and a tight sweep close it off.
    • A whistling or rattling door in the wind. Air forced through a thin gap whistles, and a loose, unsealed door rattles in a gust — both mean the seal has worn down to nothing.
    • Cracked, flattened, hardened, or missing strip. Look at the weatherstripping itself: if it's brittle and cracked, crushed flat, peeling off, or simply gone in places, it's no longer sealing even where it looks intact. Replace it.
    • A warped door or rotted frame — call a pro. If the door itself is warped so it won't sit flush, or the jamb and frame are soft and rotted, no amount of weatherstripping will seal it. That's a carpentry or door-replacement job.
    • A damaged or out-of-level threshold — call a pro. If the threshold is cracked, badly worn, or no longer level, a sweep alone won't seal the floor gap. Replacing an adjustable threshold or door shoe correctly is fussier work and often worth handing to a pro.

    Common questions

    How do I know my weatherstripping needs replacing?

    Do the draft test. On a windy or cold day, run your hand slowly around the edges of the closed door and feel for moving air; look for a line of daylight anywhere around the door, especially at the corners and along the bottom. For a more sensitive check, hold a lit candle or a stick of incense near the seam and watch the flame or smoke — it'll lean or flicker wherever air is leaking — or have a helper shine a bright flashlight along the closed door from outside at night while you watch for the beam from inside. Then inspect the strip itself: if it's cracked, brittle, flattened, peeling, or missing in spots, it's no longer sealing even where it looks present. Any of these is your signal to replace it; the draft test, not the calendar, is the real trigger.

    How often should I replace door weatherstripping?

    Inspect it about once a year and plan to replace it roughly every two years as a default — but go by the draft test, not the clock. The real lifespan depends on what it's made of and how hard it lives: cheap adhesive foam tape often flattens and falls off within a year or two, while quality silicone, EPDM rubber, and spring-metal V-strip can hold their seal for many years. A door baking in strong sun, enduring hard freezes, or getting slammed dozens of times a day wears out far faster than a sheltered side door you rarely touch. The practical rule is simple: check it each fall when you're thinking about heating, and the day you can feel a draft or see daylight, it's time — regardless of how old it is.

    What type of weatherstripping should I use?

    Match the type to the gap. For the jamb sides and the top of the door, V-strip (tension seal) — spring-metal or folded vinyl — is durable and nearly invisible, and it flexes to bridge an uneven gap. Foam or EPDM rubber strip is great where the door compresses it on closing, like along the stop molding; foam is cheapest but shortest-lived, EPDM lasts much longer. For the gap at the bottom of the door, use a door sweep that mounts to the bottom edge, or seal the floor with an adjustable threshold or door shoe. Many doors use a combination — V-strip or rubber on the jamb plus a sweep at the bottom. When in doubt, take a small piece of your old strip and a photo to the hardware store and match the profile, and always confirm the new thickness lets the door still close and latch.

    Why won't my door close after adding weatherstripping?

    Almost always because the new stripping is too thick. Weatherstripping is meant to compress just slightly when the door closes; if you've added a profile that's taller or denser than the gap, the door now has to crush it to shut, and it'll fight the latch or the deadbolt. Don't muscle it closed — stripping that's permanently being crushed loses its spring fast and gets torn off within a week, and forcing the door strains the hinges and latch. Instead, switch to a thinner profile, or use a more compressible foam, so the door closes with normal effort and the deadbolt throws easily. The target is a seal that the door just kisses and gently squishes, not one it has to battle. If only one spot binds, the door or jamb may be slightly out of square there, and a thinner piece in that section solves it.

    How do I seal the gap at the bottom of the door?

    The bottom gap takes a different product than the sides — it's where a door sweep or threshold comes in. A door sweep mounts to the bottom edge or the inside face of the door, with a flexible vinyl, rubber, or bristle blade that drags lightly across the threshold to seal the gap; you cut it to the door's width and screw it on so the blade just kisses the floor — sealing without dragging so hard it scuffs or sticks. For a bigger or uneven floor gap, an adjustable threshold (with a screw-up insert) or a door shoe (a U-shaped channel with a gasket that wraps the bottom of the door) gives a tighter, more permanent seal. Often the easiest win is a simple sweep on the inside; for a draftier door, a sweep plus an adjusted threshold seals the bottom completely. A temporary door snake or under-door draft blocker helps in a pinch, but a sweep is the real fix.

    Does weatherstripping really save on energy bills?

    Yes — air leaks around doors and windows are one of the biggest sources of wasted heating and cooling in a typical home, and sealing them is among the cheapest efficiency upgrades you can make. A worn door seal lets conditioned air leak straight out and outdoor air pour in, so your furnace or AC runs longer to hold the temperature. You won't see a single dramatic line on your bill, but a few dollars of strip that closes a visible gap meaningfully cuts the draft your system has to fight, and the comfort difference — no cold floor by the door, no hot blast in summer — is immediate. It's the same logic as winterizing the rest of the house: small, cheap seals add up. Pair this with bleeding the radiators so your heat actually reaches the rooms, and while you've got the door open, give it a smooth, quiet swing by lubricating the hinges.
