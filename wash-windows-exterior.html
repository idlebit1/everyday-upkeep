Wash Your Exterior Windows

The outside of the glass is a different animal from the inside. Out here the window fights weather: a season of pollen and road dust, rain spots that dry into chalky rings, hard-water freckles from the sprinklers, cobwebs strung across the corners, and the smear of a bug that didn't make it. You can't just spray and wipe — grind that grit around with a dry cloth and you scratch the glass. So the order flips: rinse first to flush the loose stuff off, then wash with suds, squeegee top-down, and dry the edges. Do it that way once a year — most of it from the ground with a pole, no ladder heroics — and the whole house looks washed, not just the windows.

🗓️ Typically once a year ⏱️ ~8–12 min per window 🌳 Fights pollen, rain spots & cobwebs 💸 Kit costs ~$20–60

Why it matters

The outside of the glass takes everything the weather throws at it, and unlike interior windows it never gets the casual mid-week wipe — so it quietly accumulates a full season of grime before anyone notices. Here's what builds up out there, and why a couple of it isn't just cosmetic:

Pollen & dust dull the glass

Exteriors get filthy far faster than interiors — pollen, road dust, and a season of weather settle into a haze that scatters daylight before it reaches the room. From outside the whole house reads grimy; from inside it just looks dim, and you stop noticing how much light you've lost.

A dimmer, grubbier-looking house

Sprinkler & hard-water spots etch

Sprinkler overspray and rain leave mineral droplets that dry into chalky rings. Bake those on in the sun, season after season, and the minerals etch into the glass surface — a milky, permanent spotting no cleaner will ever wash off.

Permanent etching, $100s to fix

Screens & sills trap debris

Screens, frames, and sills catch cobwebs, pollen, and bug debris that streak the glass and clog the little weep holes that let rainwater drain out of the frame. Blocked weeps mean water pools in the sash — the start of rot and staining.

Clogged weeps, water pooling

It's your once-a-year glass check

Washing is the once-a-year excuse to look closely at every window: cracked glazing putty, peeling paint, rotting wood sills, and the tell-tale fog of moisture between the panes that means a failed seal. Catch these early and they're cheap fixes, not replacements.

Missed rot, seals & glazing

How often should you wash them?

Once a year is the sensible default for exterior glass — you're not fighting indoor cooking film and fingerprints out here, so it doesn't need the twice-a-year cadence the inside does. But a yard full of trees dropping pollen, sprinklers throwing hard water onto the panes, a coastal or rainy climate, or a wall of glass that catches every storm all push toward twice a year. Answer four quick questions:

1. What's around the house?
Overhanging trees and heavy pollen seasons coat the glass fast, and an unpaved or busy road throws a constant film of dust onto everything facing it.
2. Do sprinklers or hard water hit the glass?
Sprinkler overspray is the silent etcher: mineral-heavy water dries on hot glass day after day until the spots bake in permanently. Catching them often, before they set, is the whole game.
3. What's your climate like?
Frequent rain means rain spots and pollen-streaked runoff; coastal salt air leaves a haze that bonds to the glass. Both reward a second wash a year — a dry climate is far more forgiving.
4. How many windows — and how high?
This doesn't change how dirty they get, but it changes the plan: lots of high glass is where a water-fed pole earns its keep, or where it's worth pricing out a pro who'll do the whole house at once.

    How to wash your exterior windows

    The order is the whole secret, and it starts differently than the inside: rinse first to flush off the loose pollen and grit so you don't grind it into the glass, then wash with suds top to bottom, squeegee in overlapping passes, dry the edges, and finish the screens, frames, and weep holes. Read the safety box first — out here, the ladder is the real risk — then work the techniques and the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Work smart — the ladder is the #1 risk out here

    • Never overreach from a ladder. Falls from ladders are the single biggest danger in this whole job. Keep your belt buckle between the rails — if you have to lean to reach, climb down and move the ladder instead. It takes ten extra seconds and it's the difference between a chore and an ER visit.
    • Reach height from the ground with a pole. A water-fed or extension pole lets you wash upper glass standing safely on the ground — no ladder at all. It's the single best upgrade for a two-story house, and it's far safer than balancing a squeegee at the top of a ladder.
    • Set ladders up right, with a spotter. Firm, level ground; the right 4-to-1 angle (one foot out for every four feet up); feet planted, not on grass or soft soil; and someone footing the base. Never stand on the top two rungs.
    • Watch for overhead power lines. A wet pole or aluminum ladder near a service line is lethal. Look up before you raise anything, and keep all your gear well clear of the wires running to the house.
    • Do NOT pressure-wash the windows. A pressure washer can blow out the glazing seal, force water past the glass into the wall, and crack older panes outright. Use a regular garden hose for the rinse — gentle pressure only.
    • Never wash glass in direct sun or on hot glass. Out here the sun bakes the cleaner on before you can squeegee it, and it bakes hard-water minerals into the surface. Follow the shade around the house and do whichever side is shaded right now.
    • Leave the dangerous stuff to a pro. Anything above the first floor that a pole can't reach, anything over a roofline, a deck edge, or a drop — hire it out. A window-cleaning service has the gear and does it safely; it isn't worth a fall to save a hundred dollars.

    🧰 What you'll need

    A garden hose A squeegee An extension or water-fed pole A bucket of warm, sudsy water A washer/sponge mop or soft brush Glass cleaner, or DIY vinegar + dish soap A few microfiber cloths A soft brush for screens & weep holes A stable ladder + spotter (for the reachable upper glass)

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Rinse first with the hose

    Out here, a dry wipe is a recipe for scratches — pollen and grit act like sandpaper. Hose the whole window down first (gentle pressure, never a pressure washer) to flush the loose stuff off, so when you wash you're cleaning, not grinding dirt into the glass.

    Scrub with suds, top to bottom

    Now wash with a sudsy mop or sponge — warm water with a drop of dish soap, or a vinegar-and-water mix. Work from the top down so the dirty runoff lands on glass you haven't done yet, and loosen any clinging bug splatter or sap with the suds.

    Squeegee in a reverse-S

    Pull the squeegee down and across in an overlapping S, wiping the blade dry on a cloth after every pass. Each stroke clears the glass and the runoff together so it dries clear instead of in streaks — exactly the same finishing move as the inside.

    Use a pole for upper glass

    For second-story and other high panes, reach them with a water-fed or extension pole from the ground — no ladder, no leaning. It's the safest way to do height, and on a two-story house it's the upgrade that turns a scary job into a routine one.

    Brush screens & clear weeps

    Pull or brush the screens, sweep the frames clear of cobwebs and pollen, and poke the little weep holes in the bottom rail open so rainwater can drain. Then run a dry microfiber around the glass edges and bottom rail — that's where streaks form as it dries.

    Signs your windows are overdue — and when to call a pro

    From the curb, clean exterior glass disappears and the whole house looks cared-for. These are the signals it's overdue — and the handful of problems that aren't a cleaning job at all and are worth a professional's call:

    • A dull, hazy look from the street. If the glass reads grey or filmy from the curb, a season of pollen and dust has built up. It's quietly cutting the daylight inside, too — you just notice it less head-on.
    • Rain spots, sprinkler freckles, or visible cobwebs. Chalky rings where droplets dried, white sprinkler spotting near the bottom of the glass, and cobwebs strung in the corners all mean it's well past time — and the spotting is on its way to etching if you wait.
    • Debris packed in the screens, sills, or weep holes. Clogged weep holes are an early warning: blocked, they let water pool in the frame, which is how sills start to rot. A grimy screen or sill usually means the whole window is ready for attention.
    • Fog or moisture between the panes. Condensation trapped inside a double- or triple-pane unit means the insulated-glass seal has failed — and no cleaning reaches it, because the surfaces are sealed inside the glass. That's an IGU replacement or glazing repair, not a wash. Call a window or glazing pro.
    • Cloudy, permanent hard-water etching. If spots stay after a thorough wash and feel rough or look milky, sprinkler minerals have etched into the surface. Mild cases sometimes respond to a polishing compound or specialty hard-water remover; deep etching needs glass restoration or replacement — a pro's call.
    • High or second-story glass you'd need a tall ladder for. Tall stairwell windows, big fixed picture windows, second-story panes a pole can't reach, anything over a roofline or a drop — that's a job for a professional window cleaner with the right gear, not worth a fall.
    • Pro tip: exterior glass about once a year pairs with interior glass roughly twice a year — see wash your interior windows for the inside method. Hiring a pro for the outside is often the moment to have them do both inside and out in one visit. While you're working outside, it's also a natural time to check the gutters and to plan any pressure washing of the siding and walks — just never aim a pressure washer at the glass itself.

    Common questions

    How do I get exterior glass streak-free?

    Same finishing principles as the inside, with one big addition at the front: rinse first. Hose the loose pollen and grit off so a dry wipe can't grind it into the glass and scratch it. Then never work in direct sun or on hot glass — the cleaner and any hard-water minerals bake on before you can wipe them; follow the shade around the house. Wash with suds top to bottom, then pull a squeegee down in overlapping reverse-S passes, wiping the blade after each one, or buff dry with a clean microfiber. Finish by drying the edges, corners, and bottom rail with a dry cloth — that leftover water is exactly where streaks and water marks form as it dries. Rinse, wash, squeegee, dry the edges: do those in order and the glass dries clear.

    Can I use a pressure washer on the windows?

    No — keep the pressure washer well away from the glass. The force can blow out the glazing seal, drive water past the glass and into the wall (where it rots framing and grows mold), and crack older or already-stressed panes outright. It can also strip paint and gouge soft wood frames. A regular garden hose at normal pressure is all the rinsing the glass needs, and it's far safer. If you're pressure-washing siding, a deck, or walkways nearby — see our pressure washing guide — keep the wand pointed away from every window and door, and rinse any overspray off the glass with the hose before it dries into spots.

    How do I clean and handle the screens?

    Take the screens out first, before you wash the glass behind them — note which window each one came from, since they're often slightly different sizes. Lay them flat or lean them against a wall, brush off the dust and cobwebs with a soft brush, then rinse with the hose and, if they're greasy or pollen-caked, scrub gently with a sudsy sponge and rinse again. Let them dry fully before they go back in, so they don't trap moisture against the frame. While the screens are out, it's the perfect chance to brush the frames clear and poke open the weep holes in the bottom rail. Handle aluminum frames gently — they bend easily — and if a screen is torn, this is the moment to re-screen it or have it done.

    How do I deal with hard-water and sprinkler spots?

    The best fix is prevention: aim sprinkler heads away from the house so they stop hitting the glass, and wash spotty windows more often so minerals never get the chance to bake on. For spots that are already there but haven't etched, a 50/50 white-vinegar-and-water solution dissolves most fresh mineral deposits — soak the glass, let it sit a few minutes, scrub gently, and rinse. Stubborn buildup may need a dedicated hard-water stain remover (follow the label and test a corner first). But if the spots feel rough or look milky and stay after a thorough wash, the minerals have etched into the glass — that's a job for a polishing compound or, in deep cases, glass restoration or replacement by a pro, not something a household cleaner will remove.

    How often should I do outside vs. inside?

    Exterior glass typically needs washing about once a year, while interior glass does better on roughly a twice-a-year rhythm. It seems backwards — the outside takes all the weather — but the inside is fighting cooking film, fingerprints, and pet nose-prints that build up faster and show more in everyday use. Outside, you're up against pollen, rain spots, and dust that mostly arrive in seasonal waves. Push the outside to twice a year if you have heavy tree pollen, sprinklers hitting the glass, or a rainy or coastal climate. Many people do the outside themselves once a year on a cool, overcast day and keep up with the inside more often — see wash your interior windows for that method.

    What about high windows I can't safely reach?

    First choice: a water-fed or extension pole, which lets you wash second-story and other high glass standing safely on the ground — no ladder at all. For panes a pole can't reach, set a ladder up properly (firm level ground, the right 4-to-1 angle, a spotter footing the base, your belt buckle always between the rails) and move it rather than leaning — never overreach. But be honest about the limits: anything over a roofline, a deck edge, a drop, or that needs an extension ladder fully extended is genuinely dangerous, and a fall costs far more than the job. That's exactly what a professional window cleaner is for — they have the gear to do it safely, and they'll often clean inside and out in the same visit. There's no shame in leaving the scary glass to someone insured to be up there.
