Service Your Snow Blower

A snow blower works one season and sits for three — which is exactly how carburetors gum up, oil goes stale, and shear pins vanish from the shelf. One unhurried fall service is what separates "fires on the second pull" from a shovel at 6 a.m. Here's what to do, when, and exactly how.

🗓️ Once a year, before winter ⏱️ 1–2 hours in the fall 🧰 $20–40 in DIY parts 💸 Skipping it risks $80–200+ carb work

Why it matters

A snow blower fails in exactly one circumstance: the morning you need it. Almost every winter breakdown traces back to skipping the fall service. Here's what neglect actually costs (typical U.S. estimates):

No-start, first storm

Machines that sat all summer with untreated fuel routinely refuse to start when the first storm hits — exactly when every repair shop has a three-week backlog.

Snowed in + peak-season wait

Gummed carburetor

Ethanol gas left in the tank turns to varnish in a few months and plugs the carburetor's tiny jets. Fuel stabilizer costs about $5; carb work doesn't.

$80 – $200 to clean/replace

Sheared pins mid-storm

Shear pins snap by design when the auger hits ice or a newspaper. No spares on the shelf means a dead auger and a hardware-store run mid-blizzard — if it's open.

$5 – $15 in pins — if you have them

Worn belts slip

A glazed or cracked belt grips fine on fluff, then slips and squeals the moment you push into heavy, wet snow. Augers slow down, snow dribbles instead of flying.

$20 – $50 DIY, $60 – $120 at a shop

Paying for plowing

The fallback when yours won't run: hiring it out at $50–100 per storm. A snowy winter of that costs more than a decade of fall services — while your machine sits dead.

$50 – $100 per storm

Old oil wears the engine

Most snow blowers hold barely 20 oz of oil and have no filter — it gets dirty fast and works hard in the cold. A $6 quart a year versus a worn-out engine is easy math.

$600 – $1,500 to replace the machine

    How to service your snow blower

    Set aside 1–2 unhurried hours on a fall weekend. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can pick it back up if you take a break.

    ⚠️ Safety first — the auger doesn't care that the engine is off

    • Pull the spark plug wire before ANY work near the auger or impeller (on electric machines: remove the battery and safety key). It's the only guarantee the engine can't fire with your hands inside.
    • NEVER clear a clog with your hands — always use the clearing tool, even with the engine off. A jammed auger stores tension in the belts and shafts; the instant the clog frees, it can snap around hard enough to break fingers. Hand-in-the-chute injuries are the classic snow blower ER visit.
    • Fuel valve off and engine fully cool before draining oil or working near the muffler.
    • Handle fuel outdoors or with the garage door open — and wipe up spills before any test start.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Fresh small-engine oil (usually 5W-30) New spark plug + gap gauge Fuel stabilizer Spare shear pins (your exact model) Clearing tool Grease gun Socket set + plug wrench Drain pan & funnel

    📐 Three techniques worth knowing

    Plug wire off first

    Pull the boot and tuck it away from the plug before touching the auger, impeller, or oil. Reconnect it as the very last step.

    Know your shear pins

    They pass through the auger and shaft, and snap on impact so the gearbox survives. Check them each fall and keep 2–4 exact-fit spares taped to the handlebar.

    Stabilize, then circulate

    Stabilizer in the tank does nothing for the fuel already in the carb. Run the engine about 5 minutes so treated fuel works through the whole system.

    When it's a shop job

    • Carburetor rebuild or replacement. If a can of carb cleaner and fresh fuel don't bring it back, the jets are varnished shut — a shop soak or a new carb ($80–200) beats endless tinkering.
    • No spark after a fresh plug. That points at the ignition coil or wiring — cheap parts, fiddly diagnosis. Worth a shop's hour.
    • Auger gearbox trouble: grinding, leaking gear oil, or augers that wobble on the shaft. Gearboxes are sealed units; opening one is real surgery.
    • Impeller damage — bent blades or a clanging housing after eating ice or a rock. Spinning metal that's out of balance is not a DIY guess.
    • Typical shop tune-up runs $80–$150 — book it in September or October. By the first storm, every shop in the snow belt has a multi-week backlog.

    Common questions

    Is ethanol-free fuel really worth it?

    Yes — it's the single best thing you can put in a small engine that sits for months. Ethanol absorbs water and breaks down into the varnish that gums carburetors. Ethanol-free pump gas or canned fuel costs more per gallon, but a snow blower burns so little that the premium is a few dollars a season — far less than one carb cleaning.

    End of season: run it dry or stabilize the fuel?

    Both camps are right; pick one and actually do it. Running the carb dry (fuel valve off, run until it dies) leaves nothing to varnish, but can dry out carb gaskets. Storing with stabilized, ethanol-free fuel keeps seals happy and the machine ready. The only wrong answer is the common one: parking it in March with half a tank of untreated ethanol gas.

    How many shear pins should I keep on hand?

    At least one full set for your machine — usually 2 for a two-auger machine — and 4 is the comfortable number in heavy-snow country. Use your model's exact pin: a regular hardware-store bolt is stronger than a shear pin, so instead of snapping it transfers the impact to the auger gearbox, which is the expensive part the pin exists to protect.

    Does a single-stage machine need different care than a two-stage?

    Mostly less of it. Single-stage machines typically have no shear pins and no auger gearbox to grease — but their rubber paddles and scraper bar are wear items that do double duty (the paddles touching pavement is what pulls the machine forward). Check paddle wear marks each fall. The engine side — oil, plug, fuel care — is identical.

    It's mid-winter and it won't start. What do I try, in order?

    1) Fresh fuel — if the tank's been sitting since last year, drain it and refill. 2) Check the obvious: fuel valve on, choke on, safety key in, primer pressed 2–3 times. 3) New spark plug — five dollars, fixes a remarkable share of no-starts. 4) A short blast of carb cleaner into the carb throat; if it fires then dies, the carb is gummed. 5) Still nothing? It's a coil or carb job — see "When it's a shop job."
