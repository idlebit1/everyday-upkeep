Flea & Tick Treatment

One small dose a month — topical, chew, or collar — keeps fleas and ticks from ever moving in. Miss a month and the math turns against you shockingly fast. Here's the schedule, the why, and exactly how to do it safely for dogs and cats.

🗓️ Every month, same date ⏱️ ~2 minutes per pet 💊 $15–35/mo per pet (typical) 💸 An infestation costs $500+ to clear

Why it matters

Prevention isn't really about the pet — it's about your house. By the time you see a flea, the invisible 95% of the problem is already in your carpet. These are the bills one missed month can invite (typical U.S. costs):

One missed month = infestation

A single female flea lays 40–50 eggs a day. Skip one dose during flea season and a couple of hitchhikers become thousands of eggs in your home before the next dose is due.

Exterminator: $200 – $500+

95% lives in the carpet

For every adult flea on the pet, the eggs, larvae, and pupae — about 95% of the infestation — live in carpet, bedding, and couch cushions. Treating the pet alone doesn't end it.

Months of vacuuming & washing

The cleanup bill stacks up

Clearing an infestation means a pro treatment or weeks of sprays, treating every pet in the house, hot-washing all fabric, and daily vacuuming until the last pupae hatch out.

$500 – $900 all-in

Tapeworms ride along

Pets get tapeworms by swallowing infected fleas while grooming — which they do constantly when itchy. Then you're paying for a deworming visit on top of the flea problem.

$100 – $300 vet visit

Tick-borne disease

One embedded tick can transmit Lyme, ehrlichiosis, or anaplasmosis. Diagnosis and treatment mean blood panels, weeks of antibiotics, and sometimes lasting joint or kidney trouble.

Lyme treatment: $300 – $800+

Flea allergy dermatitis

Many pets are allergic to flea saliva — a single bite means weeks of frantic scratching, hair loss, raw hot spots, and skin infections. It's the #1 skin disease vets see.

$150 – $400 per flare-up

    How to treat your pets

    The dose itself takes two minutes. The skill is doing it right — right product, right pet, right spot — and doing it again on the same date next month. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Safety first — the species line is a hard line

    • NEVER use a dog product on a cat. Permethrin-based dog treatments are toxic — often fatal — to cats. Check the species on the box every single time, even if the boxes look alike.
    • Dose by current weight, not last year's weight. Weight bands matter, especially for growing puppies and kittens.
    • Ask your vet which product fits your region's parasites. Tick species, Lyme risk, and flea resistance vary a lot by area — this page is general education, not veterinary advice.
    • Keep treated pets separated until topicals are fully dry. Pets groom each other — a cat licking a dog's fresh topical is exactly the emergency above.
    • Wash your hands after applying, and keep kids' hands off the application spot until it's dry.

    📐 Three techniques worth knowing

    Topicals go on skin, not fur

    Part the fur down to bare skin at the base of the skull (or between the shoulder blades on bigger dogs) and squeeze the whole tube there — the one spot they can't lick.

    The tick-check body map

    After grass, woods, or the dog park, run your hands over these warm hiding spots: ears (inside too), armpits, groin, between the toes, and the tail base.

    The flea-dirt test

    Comb the rump and tail base, tap the debris onto a wet paper towel. Plain dirt stays black; flea dirt dissolves into rust-red smears. That's your early-warning system.

    Call the vet if…

    Most months, nothing happens — that's the whole point. But these four situations are worth a phone call, and the first one is an emergency:

    • You used a dog product on a cat — this is an emergency. Call your vet or a pet poison hotline immediately, even before symptoms appear. Minutes matter with permethrin toxicity.
    • Lethargy, drooling, tremors, or wobbliness after application. Mild skin irritation at the spot can be normal; whole-body symptoms are not.
    • You find an embedded tick and aren't sure — how long it's been attached, whether you got the head out, or if a bullseye rash, fever, or limping shows up in the weeks after.
    • Fleas persist after two consecutive treated months. That points to a product mismatch, a resistance issue, or an untreated reservoir (another pet, the yard, the carpet) — your vet can switch product classes and make a plan.

    Common questions

    Topical, oral, or collar — which is best?

    All three work when used correctly. Topicals are widely available and some repel ticks before they bite, but they can rub or wash off and need drying time. Oral chews can't wash off and leave nothing on the fur — handy with kids or pets that swim — but only kill parasites after a bite. Collars last months (some up to 8) but must fit snugly to work. The right pick depends on your pet, your region's parasites, and your budget — ask your vet.

    Does my indoor cat really need it?

    Yes. Fleas hitchhike in on shoes, pant legs, the dog, visiting pets, and even rodents — and once inside, a heated home is perfect flea habitat year-round. Indoor cats are also the ones most likely to go unprotected, which makes them the household's weak link. (And remember: cat products only, never dog products.)

    Can I just use natural remedies instead?

    Most popular ones — garlic, brewer's yeast, apple cider vinegar — simply don't work, and some "natural" options are actively dangerous: tea tree, pennyroyal, and citrus essential oils are toxic to pets, especially cats. Garlic itself is toxic to dogs and cats in quantity. Stick with vet-recommended preventives; they're tested for both effectiveness and safety.

    What if I miss a month?

    Dose as soon as you remember — don't double up to "catch up." Then run the flea-dirt test weekly for the next few weeks, since any eggs laid during the gap will hatch on a delay. If you keep drifting off schedule, anchor the dose to a date you can't miss (the 1st, rent day) and set a recurring reminder.

    How do I remove a tick properly?

    Use a tick remover tool or fine-tipped tweezers. Grasp the tick as close to the skin as possible and pull straight up with slow, steady pressure — no twisting, no burning, no petroleum jelly (those old tricks make the tick regurgitate into the bite). Clean the spot, wash your hands, and note the date; a photo of the tick helps your vet identify it if symptoms show up later.
