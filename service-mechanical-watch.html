Service Your Mechanical Watch

A mechanical watch is a tiny machine — dozens of gears, pivots, and springs turning on droplets of oil you'd need a microscope to see. That oil doesn't last forever: over the years it thickens, dries, and creeps away, and once a bearing runs dry, metal grinds on metal and quietly wears itself out. That's why every few years a watchmaker takes the whole movement apart, cleans it, re-oils it, replaces the seals, and regulates it back to good time. It's decidedly not a DIY job — and if yours is a quartz watch, it doesn't need this at all, just a fresh battery.

🗓️ Service every 3–7 years ⚙️ Standard automatic ~$150–400 💎 High-end / complicated $500–1,000+ 🔧 A watchmaker's job — not DIY

Why it matters

A mechanical watch — hand-wound or automatic — has no electronics and no battery; it's a self-contained machine of gears, jewels, and springs running on almost nothing but a few microscopic drops of oil. Everything about keeping it healthy comes down to what that oil is doing. Here's why a periodic service is the difference between a watch that lasts a lifetime and one that grinds itself out:

The lubricants dry out

A movement runs on tiny amounts of oil that thicken and degrade over the years. Once a pivot or gear runs dry, there's nothing between the parts — metal grinds on metal, and the wear that follows is exactly what a service exists to prevent.

Dry oil = metal grinding on metal

Small service now beats big repair later

A routine clean-and-lube is far cheaper than a repair. Let a watch run dry and the worn gears, pivots, or a fatigued mainspring have to be replaced instead of simply oiled — a much bigger bill for the same neglected watch.

Worn gears cost far more than oil

It restores accuracy and reliability

A serviced watch is cleaned, re-oiled, and regulated so it keeps good time again and winds smoothly instead of feeling gritty. If a once-reliable watch has drifted off its rate, a service usually brings it right back.

Drifting rate & a gritty wind

It protects water resistance

Service reseals the case with fresh gaskets. Rubber seals dry, harden, and shrink over time — a big reason older watches let in moisture — so a reseal and pressure test at service is what keeps the movement dry.

Old gaskets let moisture in

How often should yours be serviced?

Every 3–7 years is the working range for a mechanical watch, and the honest answer depends on what you own, how long it's been, how it's behaving, and how hard you wear it. A daily-worn diver that's overdue wants attention now; a gently-worn dress watch serviced last year can wait. Answer four quick questions:

1. What kind of watch is it?
A sweeping second hand, a see-through case back full of gears, or a watch you wind (or that self-winds on the wrist) is mechanical. A once-a-second tick and a battery inside is quartz — and quartz doesn't get this service at all.
2. How long since its last full service?
The oil degrades on a timeline whether the watch is worn or not, so age matters as much as use. A watch that's long overdue — or whose service history is unknown — is the classic candidate for a check-up now.
3. How is it running?
A watch that's suddenly gaining or losing minutes, stopping before its power reserve should end, feeling gritty to wind, or fogging under the glass is telling you it needs a watchmaker — those aren't things to wait out.
4. How hard is it worn?
Dust, sweat, shocks, and water exposure all speed up wear and stress the seals, so a hard-worn watch leans toward the shorter end of the range, while a pampered piece kept in a case can stretch toward the longer end.

    What it costs — and the cost of skipping it

    A full service isn't cheap, but it's small next to what it prevents. The whole point is to spend a few hundred dollars every several years so you never spend far more replacing a movement that ground itself out on dried oil, or drying out a watch that let moisture in through a failed gasket.     How to get your mechanical watch serviced

    This is one of the few upkeep jobs where the right move is to not do it yourself. Opening a mechanical watch invites dust, fingerprints, and damage, and breaks the water seal — so your job is to know when it's due, spot the signs, and hand it to the right watchmaker. Read the safety notes and techniques first, then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ This is a watchmaker's job — read before you do anything

    • Do not open the watch or try to service it yourself. The movement is delicate, the parts are tiny and easily bent or lost, and dust and skin oils contaminate the works. Opening the case also breaks the water-resistance seal, which has to be pressure-tested to restore — leave all of it to a qualified watchmaker.
    • Know your movement first. This applies to mechanical / automatic watches — the ones with a sweeping second hand or a see-through case back full of gears. A quartz watch (ticking second hand, battery-powered) does not need this service; it just needs a battery.
    • Keep it away from strong magnets. Phones, speakers, laptops, tablet covers, and magnetic clasps can magnetize the movement and make it run wildly fast. It's not damage — a watchmaker can demagnetize it in seconds — but it's the most common reason a watch suddenly gains minutes.
    • Avoid hard shocks and drops. A sharp knock can damage the balance staff or knock the watch off its rate. Take it off for anything jarring — sports, DIY, moving furniture.
    • Never operate the crown or pushers, or expose it to water, when the crown isn't fully in. On a screw-down crown, screw it home; on a push crown, push it fully in. An unsealed crown is an open door for water.
    • Have the water resistance tested if you swim with it. Gaskets age even between services, so a periodic pressure test is cheap insurance before any real submersion.
    • Wind a hand-wound watch gently and stop at resistance. Don't force it past the point where the mainspring feels fully wound — over-winding stresses the movement.
    • Store it safely between wears. Keep it in a case away from dust, heat, and humidity — or on a winder if it's an automatic you want kept running — never loose in a drawer with keys and coins.
    • Use an authorized or reputable watchmaker. Especially for valuable or in-warranty pieces, the wrong service can void the warranty or the value. When in doubt, go to the brand's service center or a well-reviewed independent.

    📐 Five things worth knowing

    First, know if it even needs it

    Only mechanical and automatic watches get this service. A once-a-second tick means quartz — that just needs a battery, not a strip-down. Check yours before you spend a cent, and if it's quartz, you're on the wrong guide (in a good way).

    Watch for the signs

    Gaining or losing lots of time, stopping before its power reserve runs out, a mainspring that's suddenly hard to wind, or moisture and fog under the crystal are all the watch asking for a watchmaker. Don't wait these out.

    Choose the right watchmaker

    Pick a qualified, reputable, or brand-authorized watchmaker — read reviews and ask about their training. For anything valuable or still under warranty, use the maker's service center so the work doesn't void the warranty or the value.

    What the service includes

    A full service means complete disassembly, ultrasonic cleaning of every part, fresh lubrication, new gaskets and a reseal, regulation back to accuracy, and — on water-resistant watches — a pressure test. That's why it takes skill and time.

    Care between services

    Keep it clear of magnets, avoid hard shocks, and never expose it to water with the crown unsealed. Store it in a case (or a winder for an automatic), and log the service date so the next one lands on schedule. If it wears a leather strap, keep it supple by conditioning leather.

    Signs it's overdue — and when to see a watchmaker

    A healthy mechanical watch keeps steady time, winds smoothly, and stays dry inside — you barely think about it. These are the signals that the oil has dried, the seals have aged, or the movement needs attention, and it's time to hand it over:

    • Gaining or losing more than a few seconds a day. Every movement has a rated accuracy; drifting well beyond it — suddenly running minutes off — usually means dried oil, a fault, or (very commonly) magnetism. A watchmaker can diagnose which on a timing machine.
    • Stopping before its power reserve should end. If a fully-wound watch dies early, or an automatic stops on the wrist during normal wear, the movement isn't holding or delivering power the way it should — that's a service.
    • A crown that's hard, gritty, or crunchy to wind. Winding should feel smooth. Resistance, grit, or a crunch means friction where there should be oil — keep winding it that way and you accelerate the wear.
    • Moisture or fog under the crystal. Condensation inside the glass means the seals have failed and water is getting in. Get it to a watchmaker quickly — trapped moisture rusts steel parts and can ruin a movement.
    • It's simply many years overdue. Even a watch that seems fine is running on aging oil after 5–7+ years. Servicing before it starts wearing is far cheaper than waiting for a symptom — the damage from dried oil is often invisible until it's expensive.
    • Pro note — if it's a quartz watch, it just needs a battery. A ticking, battery-powered watch doesn't get this service. If yours is quartz and has stopped or slowed, you just need a fresh cell — replace the watch battery instead.

    Common questions

    How often should I service a mechanical watch?

    The working range is every 3–7 years, and where you land within it depends on the watch and how it's worn. A daily-worn watch, a diver, or anything used around water, sweat, and knocks leans toward the shorter end; a gently-worn dress watch kept in a case can stretch toward the longer end. Age matters as much as use, because the oil degrades over time whether or not the watch is running — so a watch that's sat unworn for years still ages its lubricants. The honest plan is to service it every several years or sooner if it starts running off, stopping, or feeling hard to wind. And if you don't know when it was last done, treat it as due and have a watchmaker check it.

    Do quartz watches need servicing?

    No — not this kind of service. A quartz watch is battery-powered and has very few moving parts, so it doesn't need the full disassemble-clean-and-re-oil treatment a mechanical movement does. What a quartz watch needs is a fresh battery every 2–3 years or so, and — if it's water-resistant — a reseal and pressure test when the battery's changed. If your watch ticks once a second and runs on a battery, it's quartz: skip the service and just replace the watch battery when it stops or slows. This service guide is only for mechanical and automatic watches.

    Can I service my watch myself?

    No — this is genuinely a watchmaker's job, and one of the few upkeep tasks where doing it yourself does more harm than good. A movement runs on lubricants applied in microscopic, precise amounts to specific points; getting that right takes training, special oils, and tools. Opening the case invites dust and skin oils that contaminate the works, the parts are tiny and easily bent, dropped, or lost, and cracking the case breaks the water-resistance seal — which then has to be re-gasketed and pressure-tested to restore. On a valuable or in-warranty watch, an amateur attempt can also void the warranty or the value outright. Your role is to spot when it's due and hand it to a qualified watchmaker; leave the case closed.

    What does a watch service include, and what does it cost?

    A full service means the watchmaker completely disassembles the movement, ultrasonically cleans every part, inspects for wear (replacing anything worn, like a tired mainspring), re-lubricates each point with the correct oils, reassembles it, fits new case gaskets and reseals it, regulates it back to accuracy, and — on a water-resistant watch — pressure-tests the seal. In the U.S., a routine service for a standard mechanical or automatic watch typically runs about $150–400. High-end, vintage, or complicated watches — chronographs, perpetual calendars, and the like — cost more, often $500–1,000 or well beyond, because there are more parts and more skilled labor involved. Set against a movement worn out by running on dried oil, or moisture damage from a failed gasket, it's the cheaper path by far.

    How do I know my watch needs service?

    The clearest tells are in how it runs. If it's suddenly gaining or losing lots of time (well beyond its rated accuracy), stopping before its power reserve should run out, or an automatic won't stay running on the wrist, the movement needs attention. A crown that's become hard, gritty, or crunchy to wind is friction where there should be oil. And moisture or fog under the crystal means the seals have failed and water's getting in — that one's urgent. Beyond symptoms, simply being many years past the last service (or having an unknown history) is reason enough, because the oil dries out on its own timeline and worn-dry parts are far more expensive to fix than freshly-oiled ones. When in doubt, a watchmaker can put it on a timing machine and tell you where it stands.

    Why does my watch keep running fast?

    Very often, the culprit is magnetism, not a fault. Modern life is full of magnets — phone speakers, laptop lids, tablet covers, handbag clasps, magnetic phone mounts — and a mechanical movement that gets magnetized can start running dramatically fast, gaining minutes a day. The good news is that it's usually not damage: a watchmaker can demagnetize the watch in seconds, often for little or nothing, and it's back to normal. So if yours has suddenly started gaining a lot of time, keep it away from magnets and have it checked before assuming the worst. If demagnetizing doesn't fix it, or it's also stopping or hard to wind, then it's likely due for a full service — dried oil and worn parts can throw off the rate too.
