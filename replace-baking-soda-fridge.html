Replace the Baking Soda in Your Fridge

There's a good chance the box in the back of your fridge has been there for a year, quietly doing nothing. An open box of baking soda really does pull the smells out of the air — the onion, the leftovers, the sharp cheese — so they don't creep into your milk and butter. But it isn't magic and it isn't permanent: after a couple of months it saturates, fills up, and just sits there taking up shelf space while your fridge slowly starts to smell like everything in it again. Swapping it for a fresh, fully-opened box every three months keeps the whole fridge smelling like nothing at all — and it takes about ten seconds. The old box isn't wasted, either: it's perfect for scrubbing the sink or freshening a drain.

Why it matters

Baking soda in the fridge is one of those household habits everyone half-remembers and almost no one keeps up with — the box goes in once and then becomes furniture. Here's what a fresh box actually does, why the old one stopped doing it, and the honest limit of what baking soda can and can't fix:

It neutralizes fridge odors

An open box of baking soda absorbs the smells that build up in a fridge — the onions, the last-night leftovers, the sharp cheese — before they can drift over and settle into your milk and butter. That's why a fresh box makes the whole fridge read as smelling like nothing at all.

Milk that tastes like leftovers

It saturates and quits

Baking soda doesn't work forever. After a couple of months it's absorbed all it can hold and stops pulling odors from the air, so the "fresh fridge" box that's been in there a year is just a crusty brick taking up space and doing exactly nothing.

A year-old box doing nothing

It's nearly free and effortless

A box of baking soda runs about a dollar, and swapping it is a ten-second job — pull the old one, drop in a fresh open box. For a couple of dollars a year you keep the whole fridge (and a small box in the freezer) smelling neutral. Nothing else in your kitchen is this cheap for this much payoff.

A whole fridge that smells "off"

It only works if the fridge is clean

Here's the honest caveat: baking soda is a supplement to cleaning, not a substitute for it. It can't neutralize a spill, a container of spoiled leftovers, or a genuinely dirty fridge. Wipe up messes and toss old food first — then a fresh box keeps a clean fridge smelling clean.

Baking soda can't fix a mess

How often should you swap it?

Every three months is the sensible default — that's about how long an open box keeps absorbing before it saturates. But a fridge packed with strong-smelling foods, one that's opened a hundred times a day, or one that already smells all push toward monthly. Answer four quick questions:

1. What's usually in your fridge?
Onions, garlic, seafood, aged cheese, and open leftovers throw off a lot of odor, and they'll saturate a box faster than mild groceries ever will.
2. How full and busy is it?
A crammed fridge holds more odor sources, and every time the door opens it swaps in fresh warm air for the box to work through — a busy kitchen fridge loads a box faster.
3. How's it smelling right now?
If the fridge already smells, that's the signal to clean it first, not just add more baking soda — a saturated old box is doing nothing and needs to go regardless.
4. What are you using — open box or sealed pack?
The more surface area exposed, the more the baking soda absorbs — a box cracked open a little does far less than one fully opened, and a sealed product works differently (and usually lasts longer).

    How to replace the baking soda in your fridge

    The whole job is: clean up any messes first, open a fresh box all the way, set it toward the back where air moves, write the date on it, and repurpose the old one instead of tossing it. Read the safety notes (especially "supplement, not substitute" and "don't bake with it"), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    • Baking soda can't fix a dirty fridge — clean first. It's a supplement, not a substitute for cleaning. It won't neutralize a spill, a leaking container, or spoiled food.     • Place it toward the back where air circulates. Air moving over the powder is what carries odors to it. Set it on a rear shelf or corner where it won't be blocked in by groceries — not crammed at the very front of a packed shelf.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Clean up any messes first

    Wipe up spills and toss anything expired before you touch the baking soda. It supplements cleaning, it doesn't replace it — a fresh box can't do a thing about a leaking container or spoiled leftovers, so clear those out first.

    Open a fresh box all the way

    Baking soda only absorbs what's exposed to air, so fold the top fully open to expose the most surface — or use a vented fridge-pack designed for it. A box cracked open just a corner does a fraction of the work.

    Place it toward the back

    Set the box on a rear shelf or in a corner where air circulates over it — that airflow is what carries odors to the powder. Don't bury it behind the groceries at the front. A small box in the freezer helps deodorize there as well.

    Write the date on the box

    The one thing that turns "I should swap that" into an actual swap: write the date, or a "swap by" three months out, right on the box in marker. Now the box tells you when it's spent instead of quietly sitting there for a year.

    Swap & repurpose the old box

    Replace it about every three months. Don't bake with the spent box — it's soaked up fridge smells — but don't toss it either: pour it down a drain, scrub the sink, or freshen the disposal with it. Then log the swap so the next one lands on time.

    Signs it's time to swap — and when it's not a baking-soda problem

    A working box is invisible: you open the fridge and it just smells clean. These are the signals the box is spent and due for a swap — plus the handful of times a lingering smell means something baking soda can't fix:

    • The fridge smells despite a box being in there. That's the clearest sign the box has saturated and quit. Clean the fridge, toss anything questionable, then put in a fresh, fully-opened box — a spent box can't keep up with new odors.
    • The box has been in for months, or it's crusty and hard. If you can't remember when you put it in, or the powder has gone hard and clumped on top, it's long done absorbing. Swap it — it's just taking up shelf space now.
    • The swap date has passed. If you dated the box (and you should), a date that's come and gone is your cue. Every three months is the target; there's no benefit to stretching a saturated box.
    • Odors persist after cleaning and a fresh box. If you've wiped it down, cleared old food, and put in a new box and it still smells, the source isn't the air — go looking for hidden spoiled food, a forgotten container in the back, or something rotting in a drawer.
    • A smell that keeps coming back from underneath. A funky odor that returns no matter what you do can be the drip pan under the fridge, where condensation and spills collect and grow mildew. That pan needs cleaning — a job for behind or beneath the unit, not the shelves.
    • Warm spots, sweating, or a fridge that's not holding temperature. If food is spoiling faster than it should and creating smells, that's a cooling issue, not an odor one. That's when it's worth checking the appliance itself — no absorber substitutes for a fridge that isn't keeping cold.
    • Pro tip: baking soda is the finishing touch on a clean fridge, not a rescue for a dirty one. Keep the fridge itself in shape — check the temperature, clean the coils, and clean the ice maker — and fold it into the monthly kitchen deep clean, and a fresh box does the rest.

    Common questions

    How often should I replace the baking soda in my fridge?

    About every three months is the sweet spot. An open box keeps absorbing odors for roughly two to three months before it saturates — it's taken up all the smell molecules it can hold — and after that it just sits there doing nothing. Quarterly keeps a fresh, active box working at all times without wasting boxes. Tighten to monthly if your fridge is packed with strong-smelling foods like onions, seafood, and aged cheese, if it's opened constantly, or if it already smells. Stretch a little only if it's a light, mild-food fridge that rarely gets opened. The real trick isn't the exact interval — it's writing the date on the box so you actually notice when it's due, instead of leaving the same one in for a year.

    Does baking soda really absorb fridge odors?

    Yes — genuinely, though it's not magic. Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) reacts with and neutralizes many of the acidic and basic odor compounds that food gives off, and its powder has a lot of surface area to trap them, so an open box does pull down the general funk of onions, leftovers, and cheese so those smells don't transfer into your milk and butter. Two honest caveats: it works best on the diffuse, everyday background odor rather than a strong point-source like a rotting onion, and it only keeps working while there's fresh, un-saturated surface exposed to the air. A fully-opened box placed where air circulates absorbs far more than a box cracked open a corner and shoved behind the ketchup — and once it's saturated, it stops, which is the whole reason to swap it.

    Does baking soda replace cleaning the fridge?

    No — this is the most important thing to understand about it. Baking soda is a supplement to cleaning, not a substitute. It can slowly absorb ambient odors from the air, but it can't neutralize a spill, a leaking container, or a package of food that's actually gone bad — the source keeps producing smell far faster than a box can absorb it. So if your fridge smells, the answer is never "add more baking soda." Wipe up messes, toss expired food, and give the shelves a wipe-down with warm soapy water first; then a fresh open box keeps the now-clean fridge smelling neutral between deep-cleans. Think of it as maintenance that holds a clean state, not a cleaner that creates one.

    Open box or a sealed fridge-pack — which works better?

    It comes down to exposed surface area and airflow. A plain box with the top folded fully open exposes the most powder to the air and, for the money, absorbs the most — it's the cheapest effective option, as long as you place it where air circulates and not crammed at the front of a shelf. Purpose-made "fridge-pack" boxes have vented panels on the sides so air reaches the powder without spilling it, which is tidier and harder to knock over, though the powder inside works exactly the same way. A fully sealed box does almost nothing, because no air can reach the baking soda — if you buy a sealed box, you have to open it up. Whichever you choose, the two rules are the same: maximize the open surface, and replace it about every three months.

    Can I bake with the old box from the fridge?

    No — don't. The whole point of that box was to sit in your fridge absorbing odors and flavors, which means it's picked up traces of onion, leftovers, and whatever else has been in there. Bake with it and you risk those off-flavors and smells ending up in your cookies. Keep your baking baking soda as a separate box in the pantry, and treat the fridge box as a one-way trip: once it's spent, repurpose it for cleaning. The good news is it's far from useless — pour it down a drain to freshen it, sprinkle it in the garbage disposal, use it as a mild scrub for the sink or tub, or work it into carpet before vacuuming. It just shouldn't go anywhere near food you're going to eat.

    What else can I use to deodorize a fridge?

    The main longer-lasting alternative is an activated-charcoal absorber, sold as fridge deodorizer packs or refillable containers. Activated charcoal has an enormous internal surface area and traps a broad range of odor molecules, and it typically lasts longer between swaps than baking soda — a good pick if you'd rather not think about it every quarter. Some people also use a small dish of coffee grounds, a cut lemon, or oats, though these are milder and more of a stopgap. Whatever you choose, the same limit applies to all of them: an absorber manages ambient odor, but nothing overcomes an actual mess or spoiled food. Clean the fridge, keep it cold, and toss what's past its date first — then baking soda, charcoal, or whatever you prefer keeps the clean fridge smelling like nothing at all.
