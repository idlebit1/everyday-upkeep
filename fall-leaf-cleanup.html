Clean Up the Fall Leaves

A blanket of fallen leaves looks cozy for about a week. Then it rains, the pile turns to a wet mat, and the grass underneath spends the winter in the dark — smothered, starved of air, and primed for mold. The fix isn't one heroic Saturday with a rake. It's a few easy passes through the fall, finished before the snow falls, so nothing sits wet on the lawn all winter. Done right, those leaves even feed the yard for free.

🗓️ Once a year, in stages through fall 🍂 Start at ~half the leaves down ⏱️ 30–90 min per pass 💸 DIY runs about $0

Why it matters

Leaves themselves are harmless — it's the wet mat they become that does the damage, and it does most of it slowly, over a winter you can't see under the snow. Here's what clearing them prevents (typical U.S. cost ranges):

A wet mat smothers the grass

Pile up and rain down, leaves pack into a light- and air-blocking blanket. Grass under it yellows, weakens, and can simply die over winter — leaving bare patches to reseed in spring.

$50 – $150 per reseeding

Mold and snow mold set in

A damp, packed layer trapped under snow is exactly what gray and pink snow mold need. Come the thaw, you find matted, crusty patches the lawn has to recover from.

Spring rake-out & overseed

Clogged gutters and drains

The same leaves jam gutters, downspouts, and yard drains. Water then backs up, overflows, and runs where it shouldn't — toward the foundation, the basement, or an ice dam.

$150 – $500+ water damage

Slick, hidden hazards

Wet leaves on a walk, step, or driveway are as slippery as ice — and they hide the curb edge, the garden hose, and the kid's toy you're about to trip over.

A fall you don't want

But shredded leaves feed the yard

Flip the problem over: leaves chopped fine by a mulching mower break down into free fertilizer and soil, feeding the lawn and beds. The trouble is only ever a thick, whole, wet layer.

Free mulch & nitrogen

A free compost ingredient, wasted

Bagging every leaf for the curb throws away the best "browns" a compost pile can get. Shredded and composted, this fall's leaves become next year's garden soil for nothing.

$$ of bagged soil saved

What's your leaf plan?

It's a once-a-year job, but "once" really means a handful of passes spread through the fall — and how many, and whether you rake or mulch-mow, depends on your trees and what you want to do with the leaves. Three quick questions:

1. How many leaf-dropping trees shed onto your yard?
Count trees that actually drop onto your lawn — including ones next door that blow your way. Evergreens don't count; they keep their needles.
2. Where do most of the leaves land?
Open lawn is the easy case — you can often just mulch-mow it. Beds, fences, and corners need raking or a blower to round leaves up first.
3. How do you want to deal with them?

    How to clean up the leaves

    A single pass is 30–90 minutes depending on the yard, and most of it is just walking the lawn. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can pick it up next weekend's pass.

    ⚠️ It's the gentlest yard chore — but it still bites

    • Wear eye and ear protection with a blower. They're loud enough to damage hearing, and they fling grit, twigs, and the odd hidden pebble straight back at you. Glasses and earmuffs, every time.
    • Wet leaves are slippery — on the ground (mind steps, slopes, and the driveway) and especially up high. Leaf-clogged roofs and high gutters are a fall risk; leave the high work to a pro rather than balancing on a wet ladder.
    • Watch for hidden objects and uneven ground. A leaf layer hides hoses, roots, holes, rocks, and toys — anything a mower can throw or you can roll an ankle on. Walk it before you mow it.
    • Don't overfill the bags. Wet leaves get shockingly heavy — a packed bag can hurt your back or split. Fill loosely and lift with your legs.
    • Don't burn the pile without checking the rules. Leaf burning is banned or restricted in many towns (smoke, fire risk) — confirm local law before you light anything.
    • Pace yourself. Raking and hauling is real exercise — it's a common source of sore backs and worse every fall. Stretch, take breaks, and split a big yard across several days.

    📐 Five techniques worth knowing

    Clear it before it mats

    A thin scatter of leaves does no harm. A thick, whole, wet layer packs down into a light- and air-blocking mat that smothers the grass. The whole game is breaking it up before it can form — which is why you go in stages, not once at the end.

    Thin layers: mulch-mow them in

    If you can still see grass through the leaves, just mow over them with a mulching mower (no bag). The blades chop them into confetti that settles between the grass and breaks down into free fertilizer. Make a couple of passes; do it when the leaves are dry.

    Heavy layers: rake or blow into piles

    Too thick to mulch — or piled in beds and corners — and you round them up instead. Rake or blow inward from the edges into a few piles, working downwind. Pile onto a tarp and you can drag the whole load to the curb or bin in one trip instead of bending for a dozen.

    Dispose: bag or compost

    Bag whole leaves for municipal yard-waste pickup, or — better — feed them to a compost pile or use them as bed mulch. Either way, shred them first (run the mower over them): chopped leaves pack smaller in the bag and break down in months instead of years in the bin.

    Clear the gutters & finish before snow

    Leaves don't only land on grass — clear them out of gutters, downspouts, yard drains, and beds too, so winter water has somewhere to go. Then do one final pass once almost everything is down, so nothing's left to mat under the snow.

    Signs something's off — and when to call a pro

    • A very large wooded lot. When the leaf load is measured in truckloads, doing it by hand across the fall stops being realistic. A lawn service with a vacuum truck (about $150–500 a visit) can clear in an hour what would cost you whole weekends.
    • Leaves on a steep slope or bank. Raking or mowing across a wet, leaf-covered slope is a slip-and-fall (and tip-over) risk. If you can't keep your footing, it's a pro job.
    • Leaves and debris on the roof or in high gutters. Wet leaves plus a ladder is exactly the combination behind serious falls every year. Hand the roof and second-story gutters to a pro — it's the cheapest part of this whole task to outsource.
    • Matted, crusty patches in spring. Gray or pink "snow mold" shows up as flattened, webby circles when the snow melts — a sign leaves (or grass) sat wet under snow. Gently rake the patches open to dry them out; if the grass under them is dead, plan to overseed the bare spots.
    • A back that's had enough. If raking and hauling leaves you sore for days, that's a real reason to hire it (or at least buy a backpack blower). No lawn is worth an injury.

    Common questions

    When should I start raking leaves?

    When about half the leaves are down — not when the last one finally falls. Waiting for "done" is how the whole yard ends up under one thick, wet mat that you then have to clear in a single brutal session, often after it's already started smothering the grass. Start mid-drop, clear in stages every week or two, and finish with one pass before the first heavy snow. Most yards need two or three passes; a wooded lot needs more.

    Do I have to rake, or can I just mulch them with the mower?

    It depends on how thick they are. If you can still see grass through the leaves, skip the rake entirely — mow over them with a mulching mower and chop them into the lawn, where they decompose and feed the turf. Once the layer gets thick enough that you can't see grass (or it's wet and packed), mulching just leaves clumps; then you rake or blow it up. Realistically most people do both: mulch-mow the open lawn, rake the beds and the heavy spots.

    Are fallen leaves good or bad for the lawn and garden?

    Both — it's entirely about the layer. Shredded or mulched leaves are genuinely good: they return nutrients and organic matter, feed soil life, and make excellent compost and bed mulch (a loose layer over a garden bed protects it all winter). A thick, whole, wet mat sitting on the lawn is the bad version — it blocks light and air, breeds mold, and can kill the grass. Same leaves, opposite outcomes; the work is just turning the first into the second.

    Rake, leaf blower, or mulching mower — which is best?

    Use all three for what each does well. A mulching mower is fastest and free-est for thin layers on open lawn. A leaf blower shines at rounding leaves out of beds, off the driveway, and out of corners a rake can't reach. A rake is quiet, needs no power, and is unbeatable for tight spots and final tidying. A common combo: blow or rake everything into the open, then mulch-mow or bag it from there.

    Can I just leave the leaves on the grass over winter?

    No — not a thick layer of whole leaves. Left on the lawn, they pack into a wet mat under the snow that blocks light and air for months, breeds snow mold, and can kill the grass outright, leaving bare patches to reseed in spring. (A thin, shredded scatter mulched into the turf is fine — that's different.) The lawn needs to breathe over winter; clear the whole leaves before the snow settles in.

    How do I compost leaves?

    Leaves are "browns" — high-carbon material. Shred them first (run the mower over a pile, or use a leaf shredder) so they break down in months instead of years, then add to your compost bin. For a balanced pile, layer them with "greens" like grass clippings or kitchen scraps, keep it damp, and turn it occasionally. Don't want to manage a pile? Stockpile shredded leaves in a bin or wire ring on their own and you'll have crumbly "leaf mold" — fantastic free soil conditioner — in a year or two.
