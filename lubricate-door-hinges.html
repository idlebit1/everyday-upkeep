Lubricate Your Door Hinges

A squeaky door is one of the most satisfying five-second fixes in the house — once you reach for the right lubricant. That EEEK every time the door swings is just a dry, gritty hinge pin grinding against metal. A spray or a dab of grease silences it instantly and keeps the door gliding. Here's how often, and exactly how.

🗓️ Typically every 6 months (or when it squeaks) ⏱️ Under 5 minutes a door 🧰 DIY-friendly — no tools to remove the door 💸 A can of lube is $5–$10 and lasts years

Why it matters

A squeak is the hinge asking for help. Every door swing rides on a steel pin turning inside a steel knuckle, and once the film of lubricant between them dries out or fills with grit, metal grinds on metal. Catch it early and it's a five-second fix — ignore it and the same friction quietly wears the hinge out:

A squeak means the pin is dry

That EEEK is the hinge pin grinding inside the knuckle with nothing between them — the lubricant has dried out, washed away, or packed solid with dust and grit. The noise is the first and only warning you get that the metal is now wearing itself down.

The friction is wearing it out

Ignored, it only gets worse

Grinding metal sheds tiny filings that mix with the grit and grind faster still. The pin and knuckle wear loose and oval, the door starts to sag and rub the frame, and the cheerful squeak deepens into a groan you feel through the handle.

$15 – $40 to replace the hinge

The right lube silences it for good

A few drops of the right lubricant flows into the joint, coats the pin, and turns grinding back into gliding. Squeak gone, instantly. A proper dry or grease lubricant keeps it quiet for months instead of the days a quick squirt of penetrating oil buys you.

Silent, smooth doors

It frees sticky doors and gates too

The same trick rescues a stiff door you have to shoulder open, a storm door that drags, a gate that won't swing free, and a cabinet hinge that creaks. Anywhere two pieces of metal pivot, a little lubricant turns a fight back into a fingertip push.

Less strain, fewer slams

How often should you lubricate?

The honest answer for an indoor door is "whenever it squeaks" — and a proper lubricant keeps it quiet long enough that twice a year is plenty. But an exterior hinge fighting rain and a rusty old gate are a different story. Answer three quick questions for your cadence:

1. What's squeaking?
2. How bad is it right now?
3. What's the environment like?

    How to lubricate a squeaky door hinge

    Two ways to do this: the quick spray (under a minute, fixes most squeaks) and the thorough method (pull the pin and grease it, for a stubborn or rusty hinge). Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can pick it back up if you take a break.

    ⚠️ A few small cautions

    • Protect the floor and walls. Lay an old rag or cardboard under the hinge — overspray and drips stain wood, carpet, and clothing, and they're slippery underfoot.
    • Most sprays are flammable. Silicone and aerosol lubricants give off vapors: work in a ventilated spot, away from open flames, pilot lights, and cigarettes.
    • Aim the straw, not a cloud. Use the thin straw applicator so the lube goes into the hinge, not all over the door, the wall, and the air you're breathing.
    • If you tap the pin out, mind your fingers and the door. Support the door so it can't swing or drop, and tap gently — you're nudging a pin, not driving a nail.
    • Keep lubricant off the latch face and floor. A greasy threshold is a slip hazard; wipe every drip before you walk away.

    🧰 What you'll need

    A proper lubricant — silicone spray, white lithium grease, or 3-in-1 oil A clean rag The thin straw applicator (if your can has one) Cardboard or newspaper (drip catcher) Hammer + a nail or screwdriver (thorough method, optional) Cotton swabs (for tight cabinet hinges)

    📐 Five steps worth knowing

    Find the squeaky hinge

    Open and close the door slowly and listen — on a door with two or three hinges, usually just one is squeaking. Pinpoint it so you treat the right joint instead of spraying all of them blindly.

    The quick method

    Aim the straw right at the top of the hinge pin and give it a short burst (or a few drops of oil). Then swing the door open and closed a dozen times — that pumps the lube down the pin through every knuckle.

    The thorough method

    For a hinge that won't quiet down, set a nail under the pin head and tap it up and out with a hammer. Wipe the pin clean of old gunk, smear it with white lithium grease, and tap it back home — one door at a time.

    Wipe away the excess

    A little goes a long way. Run the rag down the hinge and the door beneath it to catch every drip before it stains the floor, the wall, or whoever leans on the door next. Excess lube just collects dust anyway.

    Swing it & spread the lube

    Open and close the door several full swings to work the lubricant evenly through the joint, then listen for silence. If the squeak ever creeps back — most likely on an exterior hinge — just give it another quick shot.

    🎉 All done! Silent, smooth, and wiped clean — that door will swing like new. Note the date and you're set for six months.

    When lubricant won't fix it — repair or replace

    • A hinge rusted solid. If the pin won't budge and the door barely moves even after soaking it with penetrating oil, the hinge is seized. Replace it — a new interior hinge is $5–$15 and swaps in with a screwdriver.
    • A bent or cracked hinge. A hinge that's been forced, kicked, or twisted won't sit flat or swing true no matter how slick the pin is. Lubricant can't straighten metal — replace the leaf.
    • Screws that won't bite (stripped holes). If the hinge is pulling away from the frame and the screws just spin, the wood is stripped. Pack the holes with glued wood toothpicks or golf tees, or step up to longer screws into the stud.
    • A door that sags and rubs even after lubricating. If the latch no longer lines up and the door drags on the jamb or threshold, the problem is alignment, not friction — the door needs rehanging or shimming.
    • When it's worth a pro. Tightening a top hinge or adding longer screws often lifts a sagging door yourself. But a door that's racked in a settling frame, or an exterior door that won't seal, is a $100–$250 handyman job — and worth it before the gap costs you in drafts.

    Common questions

    What's the best lubricant for door hinges?

    A dry silicone spray or white lithium grease is the gold standard — both cling to the pin, resist washing out, and don't attract the dust and grit that turn a lubricant into grinding paste. White lithium grease lasts longest and is great for exterior and gate hinges; silicone spray is cleaner and won't stain. Plain 3-in-1 or machine oil works well for indoor hinges too. The key is using a real lubricant rather than a water-displacing solvent.

    Why isn't WD-40 a long-term fix?

    WD-40 is primarily a solvent and water-displacer, not a lasting lubricant. It floods into a hinge and silences the squeak instantly — which is why it feels like magic — but it's thin and evaporates, so the joint is often squeaking again within days or weeks. It also leaves a residue that attracts dust. Use it in a pinch to free a stuck or rusty hinge, then follow up with silicone spray, white lithium grease, or a few drops of oil so the fix actually holds. (WD-40 makes dedicated silicone and white-lithium products for exactly this reason.)

    How do I stop a squeak without removing the pin?

    You usually don't need to remove anything. Aim the straw at the top of the hinge pin, give it a short spray or a few drops, then swing the door back and forth a dozen times. Gravity and the motion carry the lubricant down the pin through every knuckle. Wipe the drips, and you're done — the no-disassembly method fixes the large majority of squeaks. Save pulling the pin for hinges that stay noisy or are gummed up with old grease.

    Do I have to take the door off?

    No — and you shouldn't need to. For the quick method you don't even touch the pin; you spray the top and let the door's own swinging work it in. For a stubborn hinge, you can tap a single pin up and out (and back) with the door still hanging, doing one hinge at a time so the door never comes off its other hinges. Removing the whole door is for refinishing or rehanging, not for a squeak.

    How is a squeaky car door different from a house door?

    Same idea, slightly different parts. A car door pivots on hinges and rides on a separate door check (the strap that holds it at stops), and either can squeak. Wipe the hinge pins and the check strap clean, then use white lithium grease or a spray made for automotive hinges — it stands up to heat and weather better than light oil. Don't get grease on the rubber weatherstripping; for squeaky rubber seals, use a silicone or rubber-specific dressing instead.

    How do I free a stiff or sticking door?

    First rule out friction: lubricate the hinges and see if it swings freely. If it still sticks, the door is rubbing the frame — look for a shiny worn spot on the edge that shows where it's binding. Tightening the screws on the top hinge (or replacing one with a longer screw into the stud) often pulls a sagging door back up off the jamb. If it's still tight in humid weather, the wood may have swollen — that's a candle-wax rub or a light sanding of the binding edge, not a lubrication job.
