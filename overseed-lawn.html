Overseed Your Lawn

Every lawn thins over time — summer heat, foot traffic, and disease quietly open gaps that weeds are happy to fill. Overseeding spreads fresh seed over the grass you already have, thickening the turf and patching thin spots before they spread. Do it once a year, in the right season for your grass, and pair it with aeration and a fall feeding: it's the classic lawn renovation that turns a tired yard around.

🗓️ Typically once a year ⏱️ 1–2 hours for an average yard 💸 Seed runs $30–80 🍂 For northern lawns, fall is best

How often should you overseed?

Once a year is the safe default, but the right cadence depends on how thin and hard-used the lawn is — and the right season depends entirely on your grass type. Three quick questions:

1. What kind of grass do you have?
Hint: northern U.S. lawns are usually cool-season; southern lawns are usually warm-season. This sets your best seeding season more than anything else.
2. How does the lawn look right now?
Be honest: if you can see soil between the blades across much of the lawn, it's thin.
3. Will you aerate first?
Aerating right before you seed gives the seed real soil contact — it's the single biggest thing you can do for germination.

    How to overseed your lawn

    On an average yard it's a one-to-two-hour job, plus a couple of weeks of light watering while the seed comes up. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Germination is everything — and easy to wreck

    • Keep the seedbed consistently moist. Water lightly once or twice a day so the top inch never dries out — a seed that sprouts and then dries is a dead seed. This is the step that makes or breaks the whole job.
    • Do NOT apply a weed-preventer (pre-emergent) before or right after seeding. Crabgrass preventer doesn't know your grass seed from a weed seed — it stops both from sprouting. Skip it for the season unless the label specifically says it's seeding-safe.
    • Mow high and hold off on the first cut. Let new grass reach mowing height (usually 3–4 inches) before you cut, then mow high with a sharp blade so you don't tear out tender seedlings.
    • Skip weed-and-feed and broadleaf herbicides too. Most can't be used around new seed for several weeks — read the label, and when in doubt, wait.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Quality grass seed (matched to your lawn & sun level) Broadcast or drop spreader A rake (dethatching / raking in) Starter fertilizer A hose or sprinkler Core aerator (optional, rented) Compost for topdressing (optional)

    📐 The technique that makes seed take

    Mow low & expose the soil

    Seed needs to touch dirt, not sit on a spongy mat of thatch and old clippings. Mow shorter than usual and rake hard (or dethatch) so seed can reach bare soil — no soil contact, no sprouting.

    Aerate for seed-to-soil contact

    If you can, core-aerate right before seeding. The fresh holes are ideal little seed pockets — protected, in contact with soil, and far better at germinating than seed scattered on hard, closed ground.

    Spread evenly at the overseeding rate

    Use the bag's "overseeding" setting — about half the rate for a brand-new lawn. Do a perimeter pass, then steady, slightly overlapping stripes so coverage is even and you don't dump it all in one place.

    Rake in, topdress, then feed

    Lightly rake so seed settles into the soil, or topdress with a thin (¼-inch) layer of compost to hold moisture. Then spread starter fertilizer — it's formulated to power new seedlings right out of the gate.

    Water light & often, then deep

    Until it germinates, water lightly once or twice a day to keep the surface moist — never let it dry out. Once the new grass is up and rooted, gradually shift to less frequent, deeper watering to drive roots down.

    Signs of trouble — and when to call a pro

    • The seed just won't germinate. Usually one of four things: the seedbed dried out, the soil was still too cold (or too hot), the seed was old and dead, or a weed-preventer was applied that's blocking sprouting. Rule each out before reseeding.
    • Bare spots keep coming back in the same place. If a patch refuses to fill year after year, the cause is underneath — compaction, a drainage problem, a fungus, deep shade, or bad soil. A soil test, or laying sod, may be the real fix.
    • More than half the lawn is bare or weeds. At that point overseeding can't catch up; it's a full renovation — kill-off and reseed or sod. A pro can do it in a week, where DIY mistakes cost a whole season.
    • Disease keeps returning. Recurring brown patch, dollar spot, or rust points to a turf-health problem that new seed alone won't solve — worth a diagnosis before you keep spending on seed.
    • Large lawns. For an acre or more, hydroseeding or a lawn service with a slit-seeder gets faster, more even results than walking a hand spreader back and forth all day.

    Common questions

    When is the best time to overseed?

    For cool-season grass (fescue, bluegrass, rye), early fall is the sweet spot: the soil is still warm enough for fast germination, the air has cooled, and there are far fewer weeds competing for the open ground. Early spring is a decent backup. For warm-season grass (bermuda, zoysia, St. Augustine), seed in late spring into early summer, once the lawn is fully greened up and growing hard. Either way, the rule is the same — seed while the grass is actively growing so seedlings can establish before the next stress season.

    Should I aerate before or after seeding?

    Aerate first, then seed. The fresh core holes are perfect little seed pockets — they give seed protection and real soil contact, which is exactly what germination needs. So the order is: aerate, spread seed, lightly rake or topdress, add starter fertilizer, then keep it moist. Seeding before aerating just buries seed under the plugs you pull. See our full aeration guide for the details.

    How long does grass seed take to germinate?

    It depends on the type, and you'll see a range even within one bag. Perennial ryegrass is the sprinter — often 5–10 days. Tall fescue takes about 7–14 days, Kentucky bluegrass is the slowpoke at 14–28 days, and bermuda runs roughly 7–21 days. Cooler soil slows everything down. As long as you keep the seedbed consistently moist, be patient — most blends are "mostly up" in about two to three weeks.

    How often do I water new seed?

    Little and often. Until the seed germinates, water lightly once or twice a day (a third time on hot, windy days) just to keep the top inch of soil moist — never soggy, never dried out. A seed that sprouts and then dries is dead. Once the new grass is up and has been mowed once or twice, start cutting back to less frequent, deeper watering to push the roots down.

    Can I overseed and apply weed killer at the same time?

    No — they work against each other. Pre-emergent (crabgrass preventer) stops all seed from sprouting, including your grass seed, so it can't go down before or right after seeding. Broadleaf weed killers and weed-and-feed products usually can't be used around new seedlings for several weeks either. Plan to skip the herbicides for the season when you overseed; if you must spot-treat weeds, do it well before seeding and check the label's reseeding interval.

    Do I have to aerate first?

    No — it's not required, but it dramatically improves your results. The biggest factor in germination is seed-to-soil contact, and aeration is the easiest way to get it on an established lawn. If you skip it, at least mow low and rake or dethatch hard first so the seed can reach bare soil instead of sitting on top of thatch. If you can rent a core aerator, though, it's the single best thing you can do for a thin lawn.
