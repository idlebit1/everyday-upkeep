Replace Cordless Tool Batteries

Lithium-ion packs don't just wear out — they age out, losing capacity by the calendar even when the tool barely leaves the shelf. Most packs give a useful 3–6 years, and how you store them matters as much as how you use them. An hour-long audit tells you which packs are done, which are fine, and which "broken" tools just need fresh power.

🗓️ Audit packs every 4 years (typical) ⏱️ ~1 hour to test the whole fleet 🔋 $50–150 per genuine pack ♻️ Recycling is free — never the trash

Why it matters

A cordless tool is only as good as the pack hanging off the bottom of it. Li-ion cells fade gradually — by charge cycles and by plain calendar age — so the failure sneaks up on you. Here's what an aging fleet of packs actually costs:

Dead tool, mid-project

An old pack reads "charged" on the gauge, then quits ten screws into the job — or won't take a charge at all the morning you need it. Battery failure always announces itself at the worst possible moment.

Stalled project + store run

"Broken" tools that aren't

A weak pack makes a perfectly good drill bog down and stall, so the whole kit gets replaced. The motor was fine — the $80 battery died and took a $300 purchase decision down with it.

$200–500 kit for a $50–150 fix

Fire risk from bad packs

Damaged, swollen, or cheap knockoff packs are the real fire hazard in a Li-ion garage — knockoffs often skimp on the protection circuits that keep cells from overcharging and overheating.

Garage fire is not a hypothetical

Storage abuse halves lifespan

A pack baking in a hot shed all summer or sitting at full charge on the charger for months ages far faster than one stored half-charged in the house. Same purchase date, half the life.

3 years of life from a 6-year pack

How often should you replace them?

"Every 4 years" is a sensible planning default, but real pack life runs anywhere from 2 to 6+ years depending on how hard the packs work, where they live, and how old they already are. Answer four questions:

1. How hard do your cordless tools work?
2. Where do the packs live between jobs?
3. How old are most of your packs?
4. Any of these symptoms yet? (Shorter runtime, won't hold a charge overnight, charger error lights, pack gets hot, tool bogs under loads it used to handle)

    How to audit and replace your packs

    Set aside about an hour (plus charging time the night before). Skim the techniques, then work the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can spread the audit over a weekend.

    ⚠️ Safety first — lithium-ion deserves respect

    • Never put a Li-ion pack in the trash or curbside recycling. Crushed packs start fires in garbage trucks and transfer stations — it's a real and growing problem, and in many places tossing them is illegal. Drop-off recycling is free.
    • Stop using any pack that's swollen, cracked, leaking, or gets hot at rest or on the charger. Don't recharge it — set it somewhere non-flammable (a concrete floor, away from the house) until you can recycle it.
    • Tape over the terminals of every retired pack with electrical or packing tape before it goes in a bag or box. Loose packs shorting against each other (or keys, or steel wool) is how transport fires start.
    • Skip the knockoffs. Cheap no-name packs often lack proper battery-management circuits — the electronics that prevent overcharge and overheating. The savings aren't worth what they protect against.
    • Charge packs on a hard, non-flammable surface, not on a pile of rags or sawdust — and don't charge a frozen pack; let it come up to room temperature first.

    📐 Four techniques worth knowing

    The runtime test

    Charge each pack full, run the same steady load (a work light or fan is perfect), and time it. Compare against your newest pack. A pack delivering well under about 60% of healthy runtime has done its service.

    Store at ~half charge, indoors

    For packs sitting more than a month, aim for roughly 40–60% charge in a cool, dry spot indoors. Full-and-hot is the fastest way to age Li-ion; stone dead for months can kill it entirely.

    Label every pack with its year

    Packs age by calendar, but nothing on the outside shows it. A marker-on-tape purchase year turns "which one is the old one?" into a two-second glance — and makes the next audit trivial.

    Match volts; pick Ah for runtime

    A replacement must match your tool's brand/platform and voltage (e.g. 18V or 20V Max — same cells, different label math). Amp-hours are just the gas tank: 2Ah is light, 5Ah runs longer. Mowers and outdoor tools usually want the big packs.

    Make the next packs last

    Replacement is inevitable; early replacement isn't. The same pack can give three years or six depending on how it's treated — and none of this takes effort once it's habit:

    • Keep packs out of temperature extremes. Heat is the #1 ager — a hot truck bed, a shed in July, a sunny windowsill. Store packs indoors where you'd be comfortable; use them in the cold if you must, but let them warm up before charging.
    • Take packs off the charger once they're full. Most modern chargers stop on their own, but months parked at 100% still ages the cells. Charge, then shelve.
    • Going on the shelf for a month or more? Leave it around half charge. Roughly 40–60% is the low-stress zone for Li-ion. Top it off the day you need it.
    • Avoid running packs flat as a habit. Swap to a fresh pack when the tool starts to fade instead of grinding to zero — shallow cycles are gentler than full-depth discharges.
    • Consolidate onto one battery platform. Every pack works in every tool, dead packs matter less, and you buy fewer of them. Pick the brand whose batteries you already own the most of.
    • Buy packs during holiday combo deals. Black Friday and Father's Day routinely bundle a free tool or second pack with a battery purchase — the genuine pack at its real price.
    • Label the year on day one. Future-you will know exactly which packs are due for the side-eye.

    Common questions

    OEM packs or third-party — which should I buy?

    Genuine (OEM) packs are the safe default: correct protection circuitry, honest capacity ratings, and no warranty arguments. There are also a few reputable third-party brands with real reviews and honest specs that cost meaningfully less. What to avoid is the $20–30 no-name marketplace pack with a suspiciously huge Ah number — those routinely test at a fraction of their claimed capacity, die young, and often lack proper battery-management electronics, which is a fire risk on your charger. If a price looks too good for the chemistry inside, it is.

    Can a dead pack be revived?

    Sometimes a pack that's merely over-discharged can be coaxed back — some chargers refuse a deeply drained pack, and a slow "boost" from a compatible charger occasionally wakes it. But a pack that won't hold charge, trips the charger's error light repeatedly, or has a dead cell group is done; the internet tricks (jump-starting from another battery, freezer myths) range from useless to genuinely dangerous. Re-celling — having the cells replaced inside the housing — exists, but for common platforms a new pack is usually cheaper and safer.

    Why does my battery die so fast in winter?

    Cold slows the chemistry. Below freezing, a healthy pack can temporarily deliver dramatically less runtime and may feel weak under load — that's physics, not failure, and capacity comes back as it warms. Two rules: store packs indoors so they start the job warm, and never charge a pack that's below freezing — charging frozen Li-ion can permanently damage the cells (many smart chargers will simply refuse until it warms up).

    How do I actually recycle old packs?

    Tape the terminals, then drop them in a Call2Recycle bin — free, and most big-box hardware and home-improvement stores in the U.S. and Canada have one near the entrance or service desk (call2recycle.org lists locations). Many municipal household-hazardous-waste sites take them too. What you must not do is trash them: crushed lithium packs ignite in garbage trucks and sorting facilities, and many states and localities ban them from landfills outright.

    Should I leave batteries on the charger all the time?

    No. Modern chargers won't dangerously overcharge a pack, but living at 100% charge — especially in a warm garage — is one of the faster ways to age Li-ion cells. Charge it, pull it, shelve it. The exception: if your platform has a charger with a documented "storage" or maintenance mode, follow the manual. For everything else, the shelf beats the charger.

    My old NiCad tools still run — worth re-celling the packs?

    Usually not. NiCad packs can be rebuilt (and tolerate it better than Li-ion), but rebuild costs often approach the price of a modern Li-ion kit that's lighter, stronger, and self-discharges far less. If the tool is something special — a beloved old driver or a niche tool with no modern equivalent — re-celling by a reputable battery shop is legitimate. Otherwise, recycle the packs (NiCad contains cadmium and absolutely must not be trashed) and let the tool retire with dignity.

    What do the symptoms actually mean — which packs are truly done?

    Noticeably shorter runtime is normal aging; replace when it's bad enough to annoy you. A pack that's charged at bedtime and weak by morning has high self-discharge — usually a failing cell group, so plan its replacement. Repeated charger error lights mean the charger is rejecting a cell problem; trust it. A pack that gets hot at rest or on the charger, or is swollen or cracked, is past "done" — stop using it immediately and recycle it. And a tool that bogs on one pack but runs strong on another just told you everything you need to know.
