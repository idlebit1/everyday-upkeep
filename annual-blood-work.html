Keep up your annual blood work

A routine blood draw — usually done right alongside your annual physical — is one of the quietest, most powerful bits of upkeep there is for your body. A small vial and a few days' wait can flag high cholesterol, blood sugar creeping toward diabetes, or a thyroid or kidney issue years before you'd ever feel it, back when it's easiest and cheapest to deal with. The whole job here is simply not letting it lapse: keep the appointment on the calendar, prepare properly, and follow up on the results with your doctor. What tests you actually need, and how often, is your doctor's call — this guide just helps you not skip it.

🗓️ Commonly once a year ⏱️ ~5–10 min for the draw 🩸 Catches issues years early 💸 Often $0 with insurance

Why it matters

Blood work is the closest thing you get to an early-warning light for your own body. The things it looks for are, by design, the things you can't feel — and a small, cheap, routine draw is how they get caught while they're still easy to handle. Here's what keeping up with it actually buys you:

It catches the silent stuff early

High cholesterol, blood sugar creeping toward diabetes, thyroid or kidney trouble, anemia — most of these cause no symptoms at all until they're well advanced. A routine panel flags them years before you'd feel a thing, which is exactly when they're easiest to address.

No symptoms until it's late

A baseline over time is powerful

A single result is a snapshot; a run of yearly results is a story. Comparing your numbers year to year lets your doctor spot a slow drift — a cholesterol or blood-sugar line creeping up — long before any one reading looks alarming. Trends beat snapshots.

One reading tells you little

It's easy to let slide

Nothing about a blood draw feels urgent, so it's the appointment people quietly skip — one year, then three, then "it's been a while." Then a routine panel finally happens and turns up something that was building the whole time. Not lapsing is most of the job.

"It's been a while…"

Acting early is easier and cheaper

Catch a number early and the fix is often a lifestyle tweak or a single low-cost medication. Catch the same thing years later and you may be treating an established condition. Early beats late on every axis that matters — how you feel, how hard it is, and what it costs.

Late diagnosis is harder

How often should you get it done?

For a lot of healthy adults, routine blood work rides along with the annual physical — so "once a year" is a common starting point. But this is genuinely your doctor's call: your age, health, medications, and family history all move it. The quiz below gives a general starting point only — it does not prescribe. Answer four quick questions:

1. How would you describe your age and overall health?
Age and overall health are two of the biggest levers your doctor weighs. Younger and healthy often means less frequent testing; older, or living with a condition, usually means more — but only your doctor sets the actual interval.
2. Do you have a condition or take meds that need monitoring?
Conditions like diabetes, thyroid disease, or kidney issues — and many common medications — need labs checked on a schedule to confirm things are still on track. Your doctor decides how often.
3. Strong family history of heart disease, diabetes, and the like?
A parent or sibling with heart disease, diabetes, or certain cancers can move your risk — and your doctor may want labs checked earlier or more often as a result.
4. When did you last have blood work done?
If it's been a while, the useful thing isn't a perfect interval — it's simply getting back on the calendar. Bring the gap up with your doctor and they'll tell you what's due.

    What it costs — and the cost of skipping it

    Routine preventive blood work is one of the cheaper things in medicine, and often free when it rides along with a covered annual wellness visit. What it stands against is the cost — in money, treatment, and health — of a condition caught years late instead of years early.     How to keep up your annual blood work

    You can't run the labs yourself, but you can do the parts that make them count: keep the appointment, prepare properly, and — most important of all — actually follow up on the results with your doctor. Read the safety note first (especially "this isn't medical advice" and how to prepare), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ This isn't medical advice — your doctor decides. How to prepare.

    • This guide can't tell you which tests you need or how often — that's a decision for YOUR doctor. It depends on your age, sex, health, medications, and family history, and screening guidelines vary and change over time. Use this page only to help you not let routine care lapse.
    • Ask whether to fast, and about your morning meds. Some tests — like a fasting glucose or a lipid panel — may require fasting, often 8–12 hours (water is usually fine). Confirm with your doctor or lab whether to fast, and whether to take your usual medications that morning.
    • Stay hydrated and speak up at the draw. Drinking water (when allowed) makes the draw easier. Tell the phlebotomist if you feel faint or lightheaded, or if you have a preferred arm — they do this all day and would much rather know.
    • Bring your info. Your ID, your insurance, and a written list of your medications and supplements. It saves time and helps the results get read in the right context.
    • Take it easy right after. Keep gentle pressure on the site for a minute, and sit for a moment if you're lightheaded. If you fasted, have a snack ready for right after the draw.
    • The most important part is the follow-up. Actually review the results with your doctor — don't just file the portal notification. Ask what any out-of-range value means and what to do, and get a copy for your records.
    • Don't panic or self-diagnose from the numbers. Abnormal results are common and usually manageable. Don't make medication changes on your own, and don't read a diagnosis into a single flagged value. This is general information, not medical advice — talk to your doctor.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you go

    Keep the appointment

    The whole "upkeep" job is not letting it lapse. Book the draw alongside your annual physical, put it on the calendar, and treat it like any other recurring maintenance. The single most common failure here is simply forgetting to go back.

    Confirm the prep with your doctor

    Ask whether any test needs fasting (often 8–12 hours, water usually fine) and whether to take your usual medications that morning. Then hydrate as allowed — a well-hydrated arm makes the draw quicker and easier.

    Bring your info

    Carry your ID, your insurance, and a written list of every medication and supplement you take, with doses. It keeps check-in smooth and helps whoever reads the results interpret them in the context of what you're already on.

    Go to the draw prepared

    The draw itself takes a few minutes. Keep the arm relaxed, mention it if you tend to feel faint, and keep light pressure on the site afterward. If you fasted, eat something right after — that's usually why people feel woozy.

    Follow up — and schedule the next one

    This is the step that actually matters. Review the results with your doctor, ask what any flagged value means and what to do, and keep a copy for your records. Then put the next draw on the calendar so the cycle doesn't quietly lapse.

    When to book it — and always review results with your doctor

    Routine blood work is maintenance, not a substitute for care when something's off. Here are the everyday cues that it's time to get it on the calendar — and the standing rule that any results, especially abnormal ones, are a conversation with your doctor, not a self-diagnosis:

    • It's simply been over a year — or past your doctor's interval. If you can't remember your last draw, that by itself is the signal. Book it (usually with your annual physical) and let your doctor confirm what's due.
    • You're managing a condition, or on meds that need monitoring. Diabetes, thyroid disease, kidney issues, and many medications need labs checked on a schedule your doctor sets — don't let those intervals drift.
    • You've noticed new symptoms. Unusual fatigue, unexplained weight change, excessive thirst, and the like aren't for a routine annual to sort out on their own — mention them and let your doctor decide what to test and when.
    • A family-history concern has come up. A new diagnosis of heart disease, diabetes, or cancer in a parent or sibling can change what your doctor wants to check, and when. Bring it up at your next visit.
    • Always review results — and any abnormal value — with your doctor. Don't interpret the numbers yourself, and don't panic over a single flagged result. Abnormal values are common and usually manageable; your doctor puts them in context and tells you what, if anything, to do.
    • Never change or stop a medication on your own based on a lab. If a result worries you, that's a reason to call the office — not to adjust doses yourself. Get the plan from the person who ordered the test.

    Common questions

    How often should I get blood work?

    There's no single answer — and that's the honest one. For many healthy adults, routine blood work is done about once a year, often alongside the annual physical, but the real interval depends on your age, sex, health, medications, and family history, and the guidelines themselves vary and change over time. Someone younger and healthy may need it less often; someone older, or managing a condition, may need it more. The right move is to ask your doctor what's appropriate for you and put that on the calendar. This guide is here to help you not let it lapse, not to set your schedule — that's your doctor's call.

    What does routine blood work actually check for?

    It varies by what your doctor orders, but common routine panels look at things like your cholesterol and other lipids, blood sugar (fasting glucose or A1c), kidney and liver markers, thyroid function, and a complete blood count that can flag anemia and other issues. The point of these is that most of what they measure has no symptoms until it's advanced — so the numbers are an early-warning system. Exactly which tests make sense for you is a decision for your doctor based on your health and history, and this guide can't tell you which ones you need.

    Do I need to fast before my blood work?

    Sometimes — it depends on the tests. Some, like a fasting glucose or certain lipid panels, may ask you to fast, commonly for 8–12 hours, with water usually allowed. Others don't require fasting at all, and many cholesterol panels are now accepted non-fasting. Because it varies, the reliable move is to confirm with your doctor or the lab when you book: ask whether to fast, for how long, and whether to still take your usual medications that morning. If you do fast, book a morning slot and bring a snack for right after the draw.

    Does insurance cover it, and what does it cost?

    With insurance, routine preventive labs are often covered — especially when they're part of a covered annual wellness visit — so many people pay little or nothing. Coverage varies by plan, though, so it's worth checking yours; if you also discuss a new problem at the same visit, part of it can sometimes be billed differently. Without insurance, common panels typically run around $30–150 (more for extensive testing), and there are low-cost and direct-to-consumer lab options that can be cheaper. Whatever the sticker, it's small next to the cost of catching a condition years late.

    What do I do if a result comes back abnormal?

    First, don't panic — abnormal results are common and are usually manageable, and a single flagged value rarely means what a quick internet search suggests. Second, don't self-diagnose or make any medication changes on your own. The right step is to talk to your doctor: they'll interpret the result in the context of your health, your history, and your other numbers, tell you whether it needs anything, and set the plan — which is sometimes just a repeat test in a few months. Reviewing results with your doctor, rather than just reading the portal note, is the whole point of following up.

    Is blood work the same as my annual physical?

    Not quite — but they're usually done together. The annual physical is the visit with your doctor: the history, the hands-on exam, the conversation. Blood work is a set of lab tests, and it often rides along with that visit, either drawn the same day or a week or so before so your doctor can discuss real results with you. You can also have blood work done without a full physical, or a physical without labs that year. If you want the fuller picture of the visit itself, see our guide to the annual physical exam.
