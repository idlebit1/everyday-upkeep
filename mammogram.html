Keep Up Your Mammograms

A mammogram is a low-dose X-ray of the breast that can spot cancer years before anyone could feel a lump — when it's smallest, most treatable, and needs the least aggressive treatment. That head start is the whole point, and it's one of the highest-impact preventive habits there is. The part that trips people up: the recommended start age and how often to screen genuinely vary between expert guidelines, and shift with your personal risk — so this isn't one-size-fits-all. Your doctor sets your start age and interval. The upkeep job is having that conversation once, then simply not letting the screening lapse.

🩺 Screening every 1–2 years 🔎 Finds cancer years early 👩‍⚕️ Start age your doctor decides 💸 Often fully covered

Why it matters

Breast cancer is common, and the single biggest lever on how it turns out is when it's caught. A mammogram gives you a head start measured in years — but only if the screening actually happens, on a schedule you and your doctor have set. Here's what's really at stake:

It finds cancer early

A mammogram can detect breast cancer before a lump can be felt — often years earlier — which is exactly when treatment is most effective and least invasive. Screening is looking for trouble while it's still small and quiet, not waiting for it to announce itself.

Found late = fewer options

Early detection saves lives

Caught early, breast cancer is far more survivable — and often treatable with less aggressive treatment. The earlier it's found, the more choices you and your care team have, and the gentler the path through it tends to be.

More treatment, harder odds

The schedule varies

Expert guidelines genuinely differ on the right start age and interval, so a lot of people aren't sure when to begin — and "not sure" quietly turns into "not yet," then "never." The confusion, not the test, is what makes screening slip.

Falling through the cracks

Risk changes the plan

Family history, a known gene like BRCA, and dense breasts can all mean starting earlier and screening more often — sometimes adding an MRI. Your history isn't a footnote; it's the thing that tailors the whole plan to you.

A generic plan can miss it

How often should you be screened?

There's no single right answer — that's the honest truth of it. For average risk, screening commonly starts somewhere around age 40–50 and repeats every 1–2 years; higher risk means starting earlier and screening more often. The four questions below sketch the general shape of a plan, but they can't replace the conversation: your doctor sets your start age and interval. And if you've noticed a lump or breast change, that's not a "wait for screening" situation — see a doctor now.

1. Which age band are you in?
Most average-risk screening begins somewhere in the 40–50 range and continues through the 70s — but exactly when to start, and when it's reasonable to stop, is a call your doctor makes with you.
2. Do you have anything that raises your risk?
Family history of breast or ovarian cancer, a known gene like BRCA, dense breasts, or prior findings can all mean starting earlier, screening more often, or adding MRI. Your doctor decides what your risk calls for.
3. Where are you with screening right now?
If it's been longer than your interval — or you've never started — the most useful next step is simply booking the conversation and the screening. Lapsing is the most common way the benefit gets lost.
4. Have you noticed a lump or any breast change?
A new lump, skin dimpling, nipple change or discharge, or any new breast change means see a doctor now — don't wait for routine screening. A symptom usually needs a diagnostic workup, not a screening mammogram.

    What it costs — and the cost of skipping it

    Here's the reassuring part: a screening mammogram is widely covered as preventive care, and for many people that means little or no out-of-pocket cost — check your plan. If you're uninsured, free and low-cost programs exist (in the US, the CDC's NBCCEDP is a starting point). A diagnostic mammogram — done for a symptom or to follow up on a finding — may cost more. Whatever the number, set it against what a late-caught cancer costs in treatment, options, and outcome. (Coverage varies.)

    How to keep up with your mammograms

    The "how-to" here isn't a technique you perform — it's a habit you protect: have the start-age conversation with your doctor, book the screening, prepare so the day goes smoothly, know when a symptom means going in now instead, and follow up on the results and the next date. Read the safety notes first (especially "guidelines vary — your doctor decides" and "a symptom is not a screening"), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Not medical advice — guidelines vary, your doctor decides (and how to prepare)

    • The right start age and interval for YOU depend on your risk and which guideline your doctor follows — they genuinely vary. For average risk, screening commonly starts somewhere around 40–50 and repeats every 1–2 years; higher risk often means earlier and more often. Have the conversation with your doctor rather than guessing from headlines.
    • A screening mammogram is for people WITHOUT symptoms. If you notice a lump, skin dimpling or puckering, nipple changes or discharge, or any new breast change, don't wait for routine screening — see a doctor promptly. You likely need a diagnostic workup, not a screening.
    • Prepare so the day goes smoothly. Schedule when your breasts are least tender — often the week after your period. On the day, don't wear deodorant, antiperspirant, powder, or lotion on your chest or underarms; they can show up as artifacts on the images. Wear a two-piece outfit so you only undress from the waist up.
    • Bring or transfer your prior images. Comparing this year's scan to last year's is how radiologists catch subtle changes, so make sure they have your priors. Tell them about implants, any symptoms, and whether you're breastfeeding.
    • Expect brief compression — and don't panic at a callback. The compression is uncomfortable, not usually painful, and lasts only seconds per view. Being called back for more views is common and usually not cancer — it just means the radiologist wants a clearer look.
    • Ask about breast density. Dense breasts can hide cancer on a mammogram and may warrant additional screening. It's a fair, important question to ask about your own results.
    • Keep a copy of your results and the next-due date, and practice breast self-awareness. Know what's normal for you so you'd notice a change between screenings. This is general information, not medical advice.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you go

    Talk to your doctor about start age & interval

    Don't try to reverse-engineer your plan from the headlines. Ask your doctor when you should start and how often, based on your risk — that conversation, not a guideline you read, is what sets your schedule.

    Book it — and transfer prior images

    Once you've set the plan, get the appointment on the calendar so it can't drift. If you've screened elsewhere, transfer your prior images so the radiologist can compare year to year and catch subtle changes.

    Prepare for the day

    Schedule when your breasts are least tender — often the week after your period. On the day, skip deodorant, antiperspirant, powder, and lotion on your chest and underarms (they cause artifacts), and wear a two-piece outfit.

    Know screening vs. diagnostic

    A screening mammogram is for people with no symptoms. A new lump, skin dimpling, nipple change or discharge, or any new breast change means going in now for a diagnostic workup — not waiting for the next routine screening.

    Follow up on results & schedule the next

    Read your results, ask about your breast density, and keep a copy. Then put the next screening on the calendar for your interval — following up and re-booking is what turns one mammogram into an actual habit.

    When to see a doctor now — don't wait for screening

    Screening is for when nothing feels wrong. But some things aren't a "wait for the next mammogram" situation at all — they're a "call your doctor promptly" situation, because they need a diagnostic workup, not a routine screen. Watch for these:

    • A new lump or thickening. Any new lump, hardness, or thickened area in the breast or underarm that wasn't there before — see a doctor promptly. Most turn out to be benign, but it's checked, not assumed.
    • Skin dimpling or puckering. If the skin of the breast starts to pull in, pit like an orange peel, or look dimpled, don't wait for routine screening — get it looked at.
    • Nipple retraction or discharge. A nipple that newly turns inward, or any spontaneous discharge (especially clear or bloody) from one breast, is worth a prompt appointment.
    • Any new breast change at all. Persistent pain in one spot, redness or warmth, a change in size or shape, or anything that just seems different from your normal — trust that and get it evaluated. A symptom usually needs a diagnostic mammogram, not a screening one.
    • Approaching or past the typical start age, or higher-risk, without a plan. If you're nearing or past the age screening usually starts, or you have family history, a known gene like BRCA, or dense breasts and haven't had the conversation — that's your cue to book it and ask your doctor about your start age and interval.
    • Reminder: a screening mammogram looks for cancer before symptoms appear; it's not the tool for evaluating a symptom you've already noticed. If something feels off, go in for a diagnostic workup rather than waiting for your next scheduled screen.

    Common questions

    How often should I get a mammogram, and when do I start?

    Honestly — it varies, and that's not a dodge, it's the state of the evidence. For people at average risk, screening commonly starts somewhere around age 40 to 50 and repeats every one to two years, continuing into the seventies; higher risk often means starting earlier and screening more often. But different expert guidelines land on different start ages and intervals, and your personal risk shifts the answer further. So there isn't a single number that's right for everyone. The right move is to ask your doctor when you should start and how often, based on your history and risk — they set your start age and interval, not a headline or a chart. This is general information, not medical advice.

    Does it hurt? What is a mammogram actually like?

    It's quick and, for most people, more awkward than painful. You'll undress from the waist up (a two-piece outfit makes this easier), and a technologist positions one breast at a time on the machine's plate. A second plate then presses down to flatten the tissue for a few seconds while the image is taken — that brief compression is uncomfortable and can pinch, but it's over in seconds per view, and there are usually two views per breast. The compression is what makes the image clear and keeps the radiation dose low, so it's doing real work. If you tend to be tender, scheduling the week after your period helps, and you can tell the technologist if anything is too much — they do this all day and can adjust.

    Why do the guidelines disagree on when to start?

    Because reasonable experts weigh the same evidence differently. Screening earlier and more often catches more cancers early — but it also leads to more callbacks, more follow-up imaging and biopsies that turn out benign, and some anxiety along the way. Different organizations put different weight on those benefits and harms, especially in the 40s where the trade-offs are closest, so they arrive at different recommended start ages and intervals. None of them is "wrong"; they're just balancing the same facts with different priorities. That's exactly why it comes down to a conversation with your doctor, who can factor in your specific risk, your preferences, and how you feel about the trade-offs — rather than one number applied to everyone.

    What if I get called back for more images?

    First: don't panic. Being called back after a screening mammogram is common, and the large majority of callbacks turn out to be nothing — usually the radiologist just wants a clearer or closer look at an area that was hard to read, often because of overlapping tissue or the way it was positioned. A callback typically means additional mammogram views, sometimes an ultrasound, and only occasionally a biopsy. It is not a diagnosis, and it is far more often reassurance than bad news. It can feel unnerving to get that call, especially if it's your first time screening, but treat it as a routine second look rather than a verdict, and go to the follow-up promptly so you get your answer sooner.

    What is breast density, and does it matter?

    Breast density describes how much of the breast is dense glandular and connective tissue versus fatty tissue, as it appears on the mammogram — it's not about how the breast feels. It matters for two reasons. First, dense tissue shows up white on a mammogram, and so does cancer, so dense breasts can hide cancer and make it harder to spot. Second, having dense breasts is itself a modest risk factor. Many places now report your density to you, and for some people with dense breasts, doctors recommend additional screening such as ultrasound or MRI alongside the mammogram. It's a fair and important thing to ask about when you get your results: what's my density, and does it change what my screening should look like?

    What's the difference between a screening and a diagnostic mammogram?

    A screening mammogram is a routine check for people with no symptoms — it's the once-every-one-to-two-years scan meant to catch cancer early, and it's the one that's widely covered as preventive care. A diagnostic mammogram is done for a reason: to evaluate a symptom like a lump or nipple change, or to take a closer look after something showed up on a screening or a callback. It usually involves more images and more angles, is read by the radiologist while you're still there, and may be paired with an ultrasound. Because it's investigating something specific rather than screening a healthy person, it may cost more than a screening. The key thing to remember: if you've noticed a change, you need the diagnostic path, not the routine screen — so tell your doctor about the symptom rather than just booking a screening.
