Do Your Annual Home Inventory

After a fire, theft, flood, or storm, your insurer asks one deceptively hard question: what did you own? Under stress, most people can only list a fraction of it from memory. An hour with your phone camera, once a year, builds the photos-and-list record that turns a slow, partial claim into a fast, complete one.

🗓️ Refresh once a year ⏱️ 1–2 hours the first time, less after 📷 Your phone + a free app 💸 Costs about $0

Why it matters

A home inventory is one of those tasks that does nothing for you right up until the one day it does everything. Here's what an up-to-date list earns you when a loss actually happens:

Memory fails under stress

Stand in a burned or burgled room and try to list everything you owned — most people recall only a fraction, and the forgotten items are the ones you never get reimbursed for. A list made calmly, in advance, remembers for you.

You forget what you can't claim

Proof speeds the payout

Insurers reimburse faster when you can show what you owned and what it was worth. Photos, receipts, model numbers, and rough values turn a back-and-forth negotiation into a documented claim — and documented claims close sooner.

Faster, fuller reimbursement

It tests your coverage

Adding it all up is how most people discover they're under- or over-insured. If the total dwarfs your contents limit, that's a coverage gap worth catching now — pair this with your annual insurance review.

Find gaps before a loss

Belongings keep changing

A new TV, a replaced laptop, a sold couch, a year of small purchases — your stuff drifts constantly while a one-time inventory quietly goes stale. A yearly pass keeps the record matching the rooms.

Stale by next year

Off-site copies survive the loss

An inventory only on the laptop that burned or was stolen is no inventory at all. A copy in the cloud or somewhere off-site is the version that's still there when you need it — see our backup guide.

A copy that outlives the house

How often should you update it?

Once a year is the baseline for almost everyone — but where you're starting from and what you own can nudge that. Answer three questions to size your plan:

1. Right now, your home inventory is…
2. How much is in your home?
3. Do you own high-value items? (jewelry, art, collectibles, pricey electronics)

    📌 Make it stick: anchor the yearly refresh to a date you won't forget — your insurance renewal month, tax season (the paperwork's already out), or a New Year's weekend. And update sooner any time you make a big purchase or finish a renovation, instead of waiting for the annual slot.

    How to build your inventory

    Go room by room with your phone, capture first and organize later, and work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can do the whole house in one evening or one room at a time.

    ⚠️ A few things to get right before you start

    • Store a copy off-site or in the cloud — the inventory has to survive the same fire, flood, or theft your belongings might not. A file that lives only on a laptop in the house is no protection. (See the backup guide for an easy routine.)
    • Keep receipts, serial numbers, and appraisals with it. Proof of ownership and value is what speeds a claim — store it alongside the photos, not in a separate drawer you'll never find again.
    • Don't publicly post your inventory or show off your valuables. It's a map of what's worth stealing and where it lives. Keep it private; share it only with your insurer or adjuster when there's an actual claim.
    • For high-value items, ask your insurer about "scheduled" coverage and appraisals. Standard policies often cap categories like jewelry — scheduling them and keeping appraisals on file is how they're fully covered. This is general guidance; confirm the details with your own insurer.

    Your phone (photos + video) A free home-inventory app or a spreadsheet/template Cloud or off-site backup Receipts you've kept Serial & model numbers Any appraisals

    📐 The five moves, in order

    1 · Capture every room

    Photograph or video each room, then open the closets, drawers, cabinets, and the garage and capture those too. The stuff that's out of sight is exactly the stuff you'll forget.

    2 · Detail big-ticket items

    For electronics, appliances, and anything pricey, capture the brand, model, and serial number (usually on a label on the back or base) and snap or scan the receipt while you're there.

    3 · List it out

    In an app or spreadsheet, jot each item with a rough purchase date and value. Don't agonize over precision — a quick estimate on everything beats a perfect figure on three things and nothing on the rest.

    4 · Flag the high-value items

    Mark jewelry, art, collectibles, and pricey electronics. Standard policies often cap these categories — raise them with your insurer about scheduled coverage and keep any appraisals on file.

    5 · Back it up & set a reminder

    Save copies to the cloud and/or somewhere off-site so they outlive a loss (the backup guide has an easy routine), then set a yearly reminder to refresh the whole thing.

    When to get help

    • High-value collections, jewelry, or art. Get a professional appraisal and talk to your insurer about scheduling these items — standard contents limits and category caps often fall well short of what they're actually worth.
    • After a renovation or a major purchase. Don't wait for the annual slot — a finished basement, a new kitchen, or a big electronics buy changes both your inventory and possibly the coverage you need. Update right away, and revisit your insurance review.
    • In an actual total-loss claim. Your inventory is the starting document, and your insurer or adjuster will guide you through their process from there — having the photos and list ready is what makes that conversation fast instead of agonizing.
    • If something reads like a foreign language. "Scheduled personal property," "actual cash value," sub-limits — a licensed agent will explain how your contents coverage actually works. This page is general guidance, not insurance advice; confirm specifics with your own insurer.

    Common questions

    What's the easiest way to actually make a home inventory?

    Walk through with your phone and capture first, organize later. Open every door — closets, cabinets, drawers, the garage — and photograph or film what's inside. Then sit down once and turn the photos into a simple list with a rough value beside each item. A free home-inventory app or a basic spreadsheet works fine; the tool matters far less than just getting it done and backed up.

    Photos, video, or an app — which is best?

    Each has a job. A video walkthrough is the fastest capture: pan slowly, narrate ("47-inch TV, bought 2023"), and open doors as you go. Photos are better for specifics — serial-number labels, receipts, the details of a valuable piece. An app or spreadsheet is where it becomes a usable list with dates and values. The strongest inventory uses all three: a video for coverage, photos for proof, and a list for the claim itself.

    What should I include?

    More than you'd think. The obvious big items — furniture, electronics, appliances — plus the easily forgotten ones that add up fast: clothing, kitchenware, tools, sporting and hobby gear, linens, and what's in the garage and storage. For big-ticket items, capture the brand, model, and serial number and keep the receipt. You don't need every fork; you do need a fair record of each category and every item worth real money.

    Where should I store it?

    Somewhere that survives the loss. A copy that lives only on a laptop or a drawer in the house can burn, flood, or be stolen along with everything else. Save it to the cloud, or keep a copy off-site — with a relative, in a safe-deposit box, on a drive you store elsewhere. Cloud storage is the simplest: it's automatically off-site and reachable from anywhere, even a hotel after an evacuation. Our backup guide walks through a routine that keeps it current.

    How often should I update it?

    Once a year is the right rhythm for most people — frequent enough to stay accurate, rare enough to keep up with. Anchor it to a date you'll remember, like your insurance renewal or tax season. Beyond the annual pass, update sooner after anything big: a major purchase, a renovation, an inherited item, or a move. If you don't have an inventory at all yet, the best update schedule is "start now."

    Does a home inventory actually help with an insurance claim?

    Yes — a lot. After a major loss, the burden of proving what you owned and what it was worth largely falls on you, and memory under stress is unreliable. A documented inventory with photos, model numbers, and values lets your insurer process the claim faster and more completely, and it's the difference between getting reimbursed for what you actually had versus only what you could remember in the moment. It also pairs naturally with your annual insurance review, since it shows whether your contents coverage is enough. That said, this is general guidance — your policy's terms, limits, and claim process are set by your own insurer, so confirm the details with them.
