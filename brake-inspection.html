Inspect Your Brakes

Brakes wear so slowly that you adapt to them without noticing — the pedal sinks a little further, the stops get a little longer, and one day a faint squeal becomes a grind that means the pads are gone and the rotors are next. A real inspection catches it early, and most of it you can do yourself in a driveway. Listen for the warning signs, peek at the pad thickness through the wheel spokes, feel the pedal, glance at the rotor and the brake fluid — and learn the handful of symptoms that mean stop driving and get to a shop now. Done once a year, or about every 25,000 miles, it turns a fifty-dollar pad job into the worst that ever happens.

🗓️ Typically once a year 📏 Or about every 25k miles ⏱️ ~15–30 min to look & listen 💸 Costs $0 to inspect

Why it matters

Of every system on the car, the brakes are the one you can least afford to ignore — and the one whose decline is easiest to miss, because it happens a little at a time and you adjust without realizing. A regular look keeps a small, cheap problem from becoming a big, dangerous one. Here's what's at stake:

They're the #1 safety system

Nothing on the car matters more than the ability to stop. Worn brakes mean longer stopping distances when you can least spare the feet — and, if you ignore them long enough, outright failure. Keeping them healthy is the single highest-value bit of upkeep you do.

Longer stops & eventual failure

Catching pads early saves the rotors

A pad is a wear part — cheap and expected. But let it wear down to the metal backing and it chews grooves into the rotor underneath. A ~$50 pad job you caught in time becomes a ~$400 pad-and-rotor job once it's gone metal-on-metal.

Each warning sign means something

Brakes talk to you, and the language is specific: a squeal is the wear indicator saying the pads are low, a grind is metal-on-metal (too late), a pulsing pedal is a warped rotor, a soft or sinking pedal is air or a fluid problem, and pulling to one side is usually a sticking caliper.

Brakes are more than just pads

Pads get the attention, but rotors warp and score, calipers seize, brake fluid absorbs water and goes dark, and the lines and hoses age and weep. A real inspection looks at all of it — not just whether there's pad left — because any one of them can take the brakes down.

How often should you inspect them?

Once a year — or about every 25,000 miles, which works out to roughly every other tire rotation — is the sensible default for a brake check. But a steady diet of city stop-and-go, mountain grades, or towing wears pads far faster, and any symptom at all should pull the next look way forward. Answer four quick questions:

1. What's most of your driving like?
Brakes wear with use, not just miles. Constant stop-and-go, long downhill grades, and towing heavy loads all cook the pads far faster than steady highway cruising, where you barely touch the brake.
2. How long since the last brake service?
If it's been many thousands of miles or you bought the car and never checked, the pads could be near the end. "I don't know" is itself a reason to look soon — you can't manage what you've never measured.
3. Any noise, pulsing, soft pedal, or pulling?
A squeal, a grind, a pedal that pulses or sinks, a car that pulls to one side, or a brake warning light is the brakes telling you not to wait. Any real symptom should pull the inspection forward to now.
4. How hands-on do you want to be?
You can read a lot just by listening and peeking through the spokes. Pulling a wheel gets you an exact measurement — but a free shop inspection is a perfectly good answer too, especially if a symptom has you worried.

    The cost of skipping it

    The inspection itself costs nothing — looking and listening is free, and many shops will check your brakes for free hoping to earn the work. What skipping it costs climbs fast: catch the pads in time and it's a cheap, expected job; let them grind into the rotors and the bill multiplies; ignore a real failure and the price isn't measured in dollars at all.

    How to inspect your brakes

    This is an inspection, not a brake job — the goal is to read the warning signs correctly and catch thin pads before they wreck the rotors. Start by listening and feeling on a normal drive, then peek at the pads through the wheel, glance at the rotor and the fluid, and decide whether you're fine, due for pads soon, or headed straight to a shop. Read the techniques first, then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically. The actual pad swap lives in our replacing brake pads guide.

    ⚠️ Brakes are safety-critical — read this first

    • Any real warning means stop driving and get it checked NOW. Grinding, a soft or sinking pedal, a brake warning light, pulling hard to one side, or a burning smell after braking are not "watch and wait" symptoms — don't keep driving on them. Get the car to a shop, or have it towed, today.
    • Never crawl under a car held up only by a jack. A jack lifts; it does not hold. Use jack stands on level, hard ground, with the parking brake set and the wheels you're not lifting chocked. Nothing about a brake check is worth being under a car that can drop.
    • Don't breathe brake dust — and never blow it out with compressed air. Brake dust can contain irritants and, in older parts, worse. Leave it settled, wipe it with a damp cloth, and don't aerosolize it. Wash your hands when you're done.
    • This is an inspection, not a full brake job. Reading pads and rotors and checking the fluid is fair driveway work. Replacing rotors, rebuilding or freeing a stuck caliper, bleeding the system, and any brake-line or hose work are jobs for a pro — get them wrong and the brakes fail.
    • Let everything cool before you touch it. Rotors and calipers get hot enough to burn after a drive. Park, wait, and check by sight first; only handle parts once they're cool.
    • Test gently after any work. If you (or a shop) touch the brakes, pump the pedal until it's firm before you move, then test at low speed in a safe, empty spot before trusting them in traffic.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Read the symptoms

    Each sound and feel means something specific: a squeal is the wear indicator (pads getting low), a grind is metal-on-metal (too late — stop driving), a pulsing pedal is a warped rotor, a soft or sinking pedal is air or a fluid leak, and pulling to one side is usually a sticking caliper.

    Peek at pad thickness through the spokes

    Shine a light through the wheel and find the pad pressed against the rotor. Roughly a quarter inch (about 3 mm) of pad left means plan to replace it soon; much less than that — down toward the metal backing — means replace it now, before it scores the rotor.

    Look at the rotor surface

    A light, even shine is normal. What you don't want: deep grooves you can catch a fingernail in, a pronounced raised lip around the outer edge, or blue or discolored "hot spots" from overheating. Any of those means the rotor needs a pro's measurement.

    Check the brake fluid

    Pop the hood and find the brake-fluid reservoir. The level should sit between the MIN and MAX lines — low fluid can mean worn pads or a leak. Color matters too: clear or light amber is healthy; dark, dirty fluid means it's absorbed water and a flush is due.

    The wheel-off close inspection

    For the real answer, pull the wheel (on jack stands, wheels chocked) and measure the pad and rotor properly — or have a shop do it. The easy time to do this is during a tire rotation, when the wheels are already off and you can check tire pressure too.

    Signs to get to a shop — and what's pro-only

    A healthy brake is quiet, firm, and even: no noise, a pedal that stops where it should, a car that tracks straight. These are the signals that the inspection is over and it's time for a professional — some of them today — plus the brake work you should always leave to a shop:

    • Grinding when you brake. A metallic grind means the pads are gone and metal is now on metal, chewing the rotors with every stop. Stop driving on it — every mile makes the repair bigger and the stopping distance longer. To a shop now.
    • A brake warning light on the dash. The brake or ABS light means the system has flagged a problem — low fluid, a worn-pad sensor, an ABS fault, or worse. Don't dismiss it; have it read and diagnosed right away.
    • A soft, low, or sinking pedal. If the pedal feels spongy, goes closer to the floor than it used to, or slowly sinks while you hold it, there's air or a fluid leak in the system — a serious, urgent problem. Get it checked immediately, and don't drive far on it.
    • Pulling hard to one side under braking. A car that veers when you brake usually has a sticking or seized caliper, or uneven pads — both shop jobs, and both worth dealing with promptly before they get worse or unsafe.
    • A burning smell after braking. A sharp, hot, chemical smell means the brakes are overheating — often a stuck caliper or a dragging pad. Pull over, let them cool, and have it looked at before you keep driving.
    • Vibration or a pulse through the pedal or wheel. A steady shudder when you brake points to warped or unevenly worn rotors. It's not an emergency like a grind, but it's a shop visit — rotors get measured and either resurfaced or replaced.
    • Pro tip: rotor machining or replacement, caliper rebuilds, brake-line and hose repair, and fluid bleeds or flushes are all professional territory — these are the parts of the system that, done wrong, make the brakes fail. Inspecting is yours; this work is theirs.

    Common questions

    How do I know if my brakes are bad?

    The brakes will usually tell you, and the trick is reading the message. The classic warning is a high-pitched squeal when you brake — that's a metal wear indicator deliberately scraping the rotor to say the pads are getting low. A harsh grinding noise is worse: that's metal-on-metal, the pads gone entirely, and you should stop driving on it. Beyond noise, watch the pedal: one that pulses or vibrates points to a warped rotor, and one that feels soft, spongy, or sinks toward the floor means air or a fluid problem. A car that pulls to one side under braking, a burning smell, or longer-than-usual stopping distances are all red flags too. And any brake or ABS warning light deserves immediate attention. If you've got several of these, don't wait — get the brakes inspected right away.

    How often should brakes be inspected?

    A good default is to give the brakes a look at least once a year, or about every 25,000 miles — which conveniently works out to roughly every other tire rotation, so you can fold it in when the wheels are already off. That's a baseline, not a ceiling. If you do a lot of city stop-and-go, drive in hills or mountains, or tow, your pads wear faster and a check every six months makes sense. And the schedule goes out the window the moment you notice a symptom — any squeal, grind, pulse, soft pedal, pulling, or warning light means inspect now, not at the next interval. The inspection itself is free; the only thing that costs money is putting it off.

    Can I check my brakes without removing the wheel?

    Yes — you can learn a lot without taking anything apart. The most important check is free: drive the car and pay attention. Listen for squealing or grinding, feel whether the pedal is firm or soft, notice any pulsing or pulling. With the car parked and cool, shine a flashlight through the wheel spokes and you can usually see the pad pressed against the rotor; if there's only a sliver of pad material left, it's time. You can also pop the hood and check the brake-fluid level and color. What the wheel-off check adds is precision — an exact pad and rotor measurement you can't get by eye. For that, pull a wheel on jack stands (or let a shop do it, often for free). Peeking through the spokes is plenty to flag a problem; the wheel comes off to confirm it.

    What do squealing vs. grinding brakes mean?

    They're two different stages, and the difference matters a lot. A squeal — a high, sometimes intermittent whine when you press the brake — is usually the wear indicator, a small metal tab built into the pad that's designed to start scraping the rotor once the pad wears thin. It's an early warning: the pads are getting low and you should plan to replace them soon, but you're not in danger yet. A grind — a harsh, metallic, grating sound — is the next stage and it's bad news: it means the pad material is gone and the metal backing plate is now grinding directly on the rotor. That ruins rotors quickly and dramatically lengthens your stopping distance. Squealing means "schedule pads"; grinding means "stop driving and fix it now." (A brief squeal on a cold or damp morning that clears after a stop or two is usually just surface rust and nothing to worry about.)

    How long do brake pads last?

    It varies enormously with how and where you drive — anywhere from about 25,000 to 70,000 miles is a typical range, with many cars landing somewhere in the middle. The biggest factor is braking, not mileage: constant city stop-and-go, mountain grades, towing, and a heavy right foot all wear pads far faster, while steady highway driving is easy on them. Front pads usually wear faster than rears because the front brakes do most of the work. The number is why a measurement beats a guess: rather than trying to predict the mileage, inspect once a year (or every 25k miles), measure the pad thickness, and replace when you're down to roughly a quarter inch — about 3 mm — so you're never running on the last of it.

    What does a soft or low brake pedal mean — and when do rotors need replacing?

    A soft, spongy, or sinking pedal is one of the more serious symptoms, because it almost always points to the hydraulic system rather than the pads: air in the brake lines, low or contaminated fluid, or an actual leak somewhere in the lines, a hose, the master cylinder, or a caliper. A healthy pedal should feel firm and stop in a consistent spot; if it goes closer to the floor than it used to or slowly sinks while you hold steady pressure, get it inspected immediately and don't drive far — this is a stopping-power problem. As for rotors: they need attention when they're worn below the minimum thickness stamped on them, when they've got deep grooves or a pronounced edge lip, when they're warped (you'll feel a pulsing pedal or steering-wheel shimmy when braking), or when they've been scored by metal-on-metal pad wear. Depending on condition they're either resurfaced or replaced — and that, along with any fluid, line, or caliper work, is a job for a professional.

    Brakes wear so gradually you adapt without noticing.

    You don't feel the pads thinning a fraction at a time — until a squeal becomes a grind and a cheap job becomes an expensive one.
