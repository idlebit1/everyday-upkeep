Clean Your Grill

Every cookout leaves a little grease and carbon behind, and it all adds up to flare-ups, off flavors, rusted grates — and the occasional grease fire. The fix is a two-minute scrape after each cook, a monthly grate clean, and a proper deep clean once or twice a year. Here's the whole routine.

🔥 2-min scrape after every cook 🗓️ Grate clean monthly in season 🧽 Deep clean 1–2× a year 💸 Skipping it risks a $200–800+ new grill

Why it matters

Grease and carbon don't just look bad — they're fuel, insulation, and pest bait all at once. Here's what a dirty grill actually costs you (typical U.S. ranges):

Grease fires — the big one

Drippings collect in the firebox and grease tray until one hot day they ignite. Grill fires send people to the ER and spread to decks and siding every summer — and most start with built-up grease.

The #1 reason to clean

Off flavors & acrid smoke

Old grease doesn't season your food — it burns and coats it in bitter, sooty smoke. If last month's salmon is haunting today's chicken, the grate is overdue.

Every meal pays

Flare-ups

Grease pooled on flavorizer bars and grates ignites the moment fat drips on it — torching dinner on the outside while the inside stays raw, and singeing eyebrows on the way.

Burnt dinners + burns

Rusted grates

Salty marinades, acidic sauces, and trapped moisture eat through grates left dirty — especially over winter. A scrape and a light coat of oil after each cook is all the prevention they need.

$30 – $150 to replace

Pests move in

To a mouse, ant, or raccoon, a greasy grill is a stocked pantry with a roof. Spiders love nesting inside burner tubes, too — and their webs block gas flow.

Nests, droppings, chewed lines

Uneven heat

On gas grills, grease and debris clog the little ports along each burner tube. Result: hot spots, cold zones, and a grill that takes forever to come up to temp.

$40 – $100 per burner set

How often should you clean it?

Everyone gets the same skeleton — scrape after every cook, clean the grates monthly in season, deep clean once or twice a year. How hard you lean on each layer depends on three things:

    How to clean your grill

    The routine clean takes 15 minutes; the deep clean is a satisfying hour, once or twice a year. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can pick it back up after the burgers.

    ⚠️ Safety first — you're mixing grease, gas, and heat

    • Gas OFF and the tank valve closed before any cleaning beyond the warm-grate scrape. Disconnect the tank for a deep clean.
    • The grill should be fully cool for everything except the after-cooking grate scrape — that one works best warm.
    • Wire-brush bristles break off, stick to grates, and end up in food (and occasionally in throats — it's a real ER injury). Inspect a wire brush before every use and toss it at the first loose bristle, or skip the risk entirely with a coil brush, wooden scraper, or balled-up foil held in tongs.
    • After reassembling a gas grill, do a soapy-water leak test: paint connections with soapy water, open the tank valve, and watch for growing bubbles. Bubbles = leak = close the valve and re-seat before lighting.
    • Degrease over a tarp or lawn, not the patio — grease stains concrete permanently.

    📐 Three techniques worth knowing

    The warm-grate scrape

    Right after cooking (or after 10 minutes on high), residue is soft and lifts in one pass. Cold carbon is concrete. Two minutes now beats twenty later.

    Brush burner ports crosswise

    Stroke across the row of ports, not along the tube. Lengthwise strokes sweep debris from one port into the next; crosswise flicks it out.

    Know your grease trap

    Every drop of fat ends up in the drip tray or cup below the firebox. Line it with foil or a disposable insert, and emptying it becomes a ten-second job instead of a scraping session.

    When to repair — or replace

    • Rusted-through firebox or lid. Surface rust scrubs off; holes don't. Once the firebox is perforated, heat control is gone and the structure is failing — that's replacement territory.
    • Corroded or cracked burners. Ports that stay clogged after cleaning, visible splits, or lopsided yellow flames mean the burner is done. Replacements run $40–$100 and install in under an hour — well worth it on an otherwise solid grill.
    • Failing regulator. Weak heat on a full tank, flames that sigh up and down, or a gas smell at the hose are regulator red flags. They're $20–$40 — replace, never "repair," anything in the gas path, and leak-test after.
    • Flaking porcelain or chrome on grates. Once the coating chips into food territory, scrubbing can't fix it. New grates are $30–$150 and instantly upgrade the cooking surface.
    • Rule of thumb: if repairs top half the price of a comparable new grill, put the money toward the new one — and a cover.

    Common questions

    Are wire grill brushes really dangerous?

    Dangerous enough that ERs see a steady trickle of swallowed-bristle injuries every grilling season. A loose bristle sticks to the grate, transfers to a burger, and can lodge in the throat or gut. If you use a wire brush, inspect it before every use and replace it yearly. Safer: a coil-style brush, a wooden scraper that molds to your grate, or a ball of foil gripped in tongs — then wipe the grate with an oiled paper towel as a final check.

    Do I clean stainless steel and porcelain-coated grates differently?

    Yes. Stainless and bare cast iron tolerate firm scraping; porcelain-coated grates need a softer touch — a nylon brush or wooden scraper — because chipped coating exposes the steel underneath to rust and can flake into food. For both, warm soapy water and a nylon pad handle the deep clean; finish cast iron and steel with a light coat of high-heat oil.

    Does the onion trick actually work?

    Surprisingly well, as a finishing step. Halve an onion, spear it on a fork, and rub the cut face along hot grates: the moisture steams residue loose and the onion adds a faint seasoning. It won't replace a real scrape on a crusty grate, but it's a great bristle-free touch-up right before food goes on.

    Can I use oven cleaner on my grill?

    Only with care, and only in the right places. Conventional oven cleaners are harsh lye-based formulas — fine on cold cast-iron or steel grates rinsed thoroughly afterward, but they can discolor stainless, strip paint from the lid and firebox exterior, and must never touch burners or gas components. For most grills, hot soapy water plus elbow grease (or a purpose-made grill degreaser) does the job without the risk.

    How should I put the grill away for winter?

    Give it the full deep clean first — grease left over winter is rust fuel and pest bait. Coat grates and burners lightly with cooking oil, close the tank valve, and leave the propane tank outside (never in a garage or basement), connected or stored upright. Wrap burner openings or the grill in a breathable cover; plastic bags trap condensation. Come spring, do the season-opening check: spider webs out of the burner tubes, fresh leak test, then fire it up.
