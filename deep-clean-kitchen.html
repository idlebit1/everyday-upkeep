Deep Clean Your Kitchen

The daily wipe-down keeps the kitchen functional, but a film is quietly accumulating where you never look: a sticky grease haze on the hood and the cabinet faces above the stove, crumbs and grime packed behind the fridge, a disposal that's started to smell, dusty coils making the fridge work harder. A monthly deep clean is the reset that catches all of it — degrease the hood and backsplash, clean the appliances inside and out, pull out the stove and fridge to get the zone everyone skips, disinfect the germ hotspots, and finish with the floors and edges. The trick is a fixed order — top to bottom, back to front — so you never re-dirty what you just cleaned. Do it monthly and the kitchen stays genuinely pleasant to cook in instead of slowly turning grimy on you.

🗓️ Typically every month ⏱️ ~1–2 hours top to bottom 🍳 A kitchen that's nicer to cook in 💸 Supplies ~$15–25

Why it matters

A kitchen looks clean long after it has quietly stopped being clean. The daily wipe handles the counters and the visible mess, but grease creeps onto surfaces you never touch, food residue gathers in the spots you can't see, and the appliances slowly clog and strain. Here's what a monthly reset actually heads off:

Grease migrates everywhere

Every time you cook, a fine mist of oil rises and settles — onto the hood, the backsplash, and especially the cabinet faces above the stove, where it cures into a sticky brown film. Left alone it gets harder to remove each month, and a grease-caked hood filter is a genuine fire hazard.

A sticky film & grease-fire risk

Residue breeds odors & pests

Crumbs and spills behind the appliances, and food gunk down in the disposal, are a buffet for bacteria, smells, and pantry pests. The crud you can't see is exactly what starts to stink and what draws ants, roaches, and pantry moths into the kitchen.

Bacteria, odors & pests

Neglected appliances fail sooner

A clogged hood filter can't pull smoke, dusty refrigerator coils make the fridge run hot and overwork the compressor, and a grimy oven smokes and bakes unevenly. Skip the cleaning and you trade a few minutes now for higher energy bills and earlier repairs.

Higher bills & early repairs

A reset kitchen is nicer to use

Beyond the hygiene, a freshly reset kitchen is simply faster and more pleasant to cook in — clear counters, a sink that drains clean, no sticky cabinet pulls, no lingering smell. It's the difference between a room you tolerate and one you actually enjoy working in.

A kitchen you dread cooking in

How often should you deep clean?

A top-to-bottom deep clean once a month is the sensible default, sitting on top of the daily wipe and the weekly upkeep of the high-touch zones. But a household that cooks from scratch every night, fries a lot, runs busy, or has pets pushes toward every two to three weeks — while a light-cooking home of one or two can stretch to every two or three months. Answer four quick questions:

1. How much do you actually cook?
Cooking is what dirties a kitchen. The more meals you make on the stove, the faster grease, splatter, and crumbs build up on every surface.
2. How greasy is the cooking?
High-heat frying throws the most airborne grease — it's what coats the hood, the backsplash, and the cabinets above the stove fastest and hardest.
3. How big and busy is the household?
More people means more meals, more dishes, more hands on the handles and pulls — the whole kitchen cycles through dirty faster.
4. Pets, or any pest worries?
Pets add hair, food bits, and bowls to clean around; any history of ants, roaches, or pantry moths means staying ahead of the crumbs they feed on really pays off.

    The cost of skipping it

    The supplies for a deep clean are about as cheap as it gets — a degreaser, a box of baking soda, white vinegar, dish soap, and a few microfiber cloths, most of it reusable for $15–25. What skipping it costs is far steeper: pest control visits, appliance repairs and shortened lifespans from neglect, and the very real grease-fire risk of a caked hood filter.     How to deep clean your kitchen

    The order is the whole secret: work top to bottom and back to front so debris always falls onto a surface you haven't cleaned yet. This page is the hub — for the big sub-tasks, lean on the focused guides: clean the range hood filter, deep clean the oven, clean the garbage disposal, clean the refrigerator coils, clean the dishwasher filter, and descale the coffee maker. Read the techniques first, then work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Work safe — fumes, water, heat, and heavy appliances

    • Ventilate when using degreasers or oven cleaner. Open a window and run the hood fan. These chemicals give off strong fumes in a closed kitchen; fresh air keeps the room from turning into a gas chamber while you scrub.
    • NEVER mix bleach with ammonia or vinegar. The combination creates toxic gases that can seriously hurt you. Use one cleaner at a time, rinse surfaces between products, and read the labels before you reach for a second bottle.
    • Unplug small appliances before cleaning them. Toaster, kettle, coffee maker, microwave — pull the plug first, and keep water away from outlets, controls, and heating elements. Wipe electrical parts with a barely damp cloth, never a soaking one.
    • Watch for slippery wet floors. Soapy water and degreaser make tile and vinyl treacherous. Wipe up drips as you go, do the floor last, and keep a dry towel nearby so a quick mop doesn't become a fall.
    • Wear gloves with harsh degreasers and oven cleaner. These products are rough on skin and can cause burns or irritation with prolonged contact. Rubber gloves also give you a better grip on greasy parts.
    • Let the stove and oven cool fully before cleaning. Cleaning a hot cooktop or oven risks burns and lets cleaner flash off uselessly. Cook your last meal, then come back to a cold appliance.
    • Be careful pulling out heavy appliances. The stove and fridge are heavy and tethered — a gas range has a flexible gas line and a fridge often has a water line. Rock them out slowly and straight, don't yank, and have a hand to help if you can.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Work top-to-bottom, back-to-front

    Start high — upper cabinets and the hood — then counters and backsplash, then appliances, then the sink, and the floor dead last. Debris always falls onto a surface you haven't cleaned yet, so you never re-dirty finished work.

    Degrease the hood filter by soaking

    Pop the metal filter out and soak it in hot, soapy water with a scoop of baking soda — the grease lifts off the mesh with almost no scrubbing. Full walkthrough in clean the range hood filter.

    Pull out the stove & fridge

    Rock the stove and fridge out — carefully, minding the gas or water line — and clean the crumb-and-grease zone behind and under them, plus the dusty fridge coils. See clean the refrigerator coils.

    Disinfect the germ hotspots

    The dirtiest spots aren't the visible ones — they're the touch points: faucet handles, fridge and oven handles, cabinet pulls, and light switches. Wipe each one with a disinfecting cloth so the germs don't ride your hands around the kitchen.

    Refresh the sink, then the floor

    Scour the sink and deodorize the drain and disposal with baking soda and vinegar — details in clean the garbage disposal — then mop the floor and wipe the edges and baseboards last, once everything above has dropped its crumbs.

    Signs your kitchen is overdue — and when to hand off a sub-task

    A reset kitchen barely registers: clear counters, a clean smell, appliances that just work. These are the signals it's overdue for a deep clean — and the cues that one specific sub-task (or a pro) needs attention more than the whole room does:

    • A sticky film on the cabinets near the stove. Run a finger across the cabinet faces above the cooktop — if it drags through a tacky brown layer, grease has been building for a while and the whole upper zone is due for a degrease.
    • Lingering odors you can't place. A kitchen that smells faintly off even after the dishes are done usually has food residue working away somewhere you can't see — the disposal, behind an appliance, or a forgotten spill under the fridge. Time to clean the garbage disposal and pull things out.
    • Pantry moths or other pests. Little moths flitting out of the cupboard, or ants and roaches turning up, mean there are crumbs feeding them. Deep clean, clear the hidden food sources, and seal what's left — and call pest control if they've established.
    • A hood that doesn't pull smoke anymore. If steam and smoke now linger over the cooktop instead of getting whisked away, the filter is grease-clogged and the fan is straining. Go straight to clean the range hood filter.
    • An oven that smokes when you preheat. Smoke or a burning smell on preheat means baked-on spills are carbonizing on the oven floor. That's your cue to deep clean the oven before it gets worse.
    • A fridge that runs hot or constantly. If the fridge feels warm at the back, hums nonstop, or the food isn't as cold as it should be, dusty coils are likely choking it. Tackle clean the refrigerator coils first.
    • Call a pro for what cleaning can't fix: a hood fan motor that's failing, a gas-range issue, a fridge that won't hold temperature after the coils are clean, or a pest infestation that keeps coming back. Cleaning prevents most problems — but a dead compressor or a real infestation is a specialist's job, not a sponge's.

    Common questions

    How often should I deep clean versus just tidy daily?

    Think of it as three layers. Daily, you wipe the counters and cooktop, do the dishes, and sweep up obvious crumbs — that's the upkeep that keeps the kitchen usable. Weekly, you hit the high-touch and high-mess zones: scrub the sink, wipe the appliance fronts and handles, and clean the microwave. Then about once a month comes the full deep clean in this guide — degreasing the hood and backsplash, cleaning appliances inside and out, pulling out the stove and fridge, disinfecting the hotspots, and doing the floors and edges. Heavy cooks, big households, and homes with pets or pest worries should tighten the deep clean toward every two to three weeks; a light-cooking home of one or two can stretch it to every two or three months. The daily and weekly habits don't replace the monthly reset — they make it quick, because nothing's had a chance to cake on.

    What's the best degreaser for kitchen grease?

    For everyday kitchen grease, a commercial degreaser spray makes the work faster — spray it on, let it dwell a few minutes so it can break down the oil, then wipe. But you don't need to buy anything special: a squirt of dish soap in hot water cuts grease beautifully (it's literally engineered to), and a paste of baking soda and a little water works wonders on the stubborn, baked-on stuff like the hood filter and cabinet tops — let it sit, then scrub gently. For sticky residue, a 50/50 vinegar-and-water spray helps cut it and leaves no film. The real trick with any degreaser is dwell time: give it a few minutes to do the chemistry instead of scrubbing immediately. Wear gloves with the harsh commercial stuff, ventilate the room, and never mix products.

    What order should I clean the kitchen in?

    Top to bottom and back to front — that single rule saves you from re-cleaning. Start high with the upper cabinet faces, the hood, and the top of the fridge, where grease and dust settle. Move down to the counters and backsplash. Then do the appliances, inside and out, and pull out the stove and fridge to get behind and underneath. Refresh the sink, drain, and disposal next. Finally, do the floor and the edges — baseboards and the kick plates under the cabinets — dead last. The logic is simple: every surface drops crumbs, dust, and drips as you clean it, and gravity carries all of that downward. If you mop first and degrease the hood after, you'll just be mopping again. Work with gravity, not against it, and you only clean each surface once.

    How do I clean behind and under the stove and fridge?

    This is the crumb-and-grease zone everyone skips, and it's often the source of mystery smells and pests. Start by easing the appliance straight out from the wall — slowly, because a gas range has a flexible gas line and the fridge usually has a water line you must not yank or kink. With it out, vacuum up the crumbs and dust bunnies, then wipe the floor and the sides of the cabinets with a degreasing solution; greasy dust back there can be surprisingly thick. While the fridge is out, take a few minutes to vacuum or brush the coils on the back or beneath it — see clean the refrigerator coils for that — since dusty coils make the fridge work much harder. Clean the back of the appliance itself too, then ease it back into place, again watching the lines. If it's too heavy to move safely alone, get a second pair of hands.

    Do natural cleaners like baking soda and vinegar actually work?

    Yes, for a lot of kitchen jobs — they're cheap, non-toxic, and genuinely effective. Baking soda is a mild abrasive and deodorizer: a paste of it lifts baked-on grease and scours sinks and stovetops without scratching. White vinegar is an acid that dissolves mineral scale, cuts grease, and deodorizes — great for descaling the kettle or the coffee maker, freshening the drain, and wiping down surfaces. They're not a cure-all, though: vinegar shouldn't go on natural stone like marble or granite (the acid can etch it), and neither is a registered disinfectant, so for true sanitizing of cutting boards or after raw meat you still want soap and hot water or a proper disinfectant. And the famous baking-soda-and-vinegar fizz is mostly for show once they neutralize each other — use them in sequence for the cleaning power, not mixed in a bottle.

    How do I keep the kitchen clean longer between deep cleans?

    Small habits do most of the work. Run the hood fan every time you cook — especially when frying — so airborne grease goes up the vent instead of onto your cabinets. Wipe spills on the cooktop and counters while they're fresh, before they bake or cure into something you have to scrub. Do the dishes daily and run the disposal with cold water and a little dish soap so food doesn't sit and stink. Keep dry goods in sealed containers to starve any pantry pests, and take the trash out before it gets ripe. Once a week, give the sink a quick scour and wipe the handles and high-touch spots. None of this is the full deep clean — but it keeps the grease, crumbs, and grime from ever building up, so when the monthly reset comes around it's a quick job instead of an excavation.
