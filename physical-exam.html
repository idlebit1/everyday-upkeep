Annual physical exam

The yearly checkup with your primary care doctor is maintenance for the one machine you can't replace. The conditions that do the most damage — high blood pressure, high cholesterol, early diabetes — are silent for years, and this is the visit built to catch them. Here's how often to go, what it costs, and how to make the hour count.

🗓️ Typically every year ⏱️ 30–60 minutes per visit 💸 Often $0 with insurance 🩺 Catches problems while they're cheap

Why it matters

Nothing about an annual physical feels urgent — and that's exactly the point. It's the visit that finds the problems that don't announce themselves. Here's what one hour a year actually buys you:

Silent conditions get caught

High blood pressure, high cholesterol, and prediabetes typically cause no symptoms at all — for years. Nearly half of U.S. adults have high blood pressure, and a large share don't know it. A cuff and a blood draw is how you find out before your arteries do.

Symptom-free until late

Screenings start on time

Colon cancer screening at 45, mammograms at 40, diabetes screening at 35 if you're overweight — the start dates keep moving, and your doctor is the one tracking them. Cancers caught by on-schedule screening are dramatically more survivable and cheaper to treat.

Early stage vs. late stage

Your baseline gets written down

A single blood pressure or cholesterol reading means little; the trend across five years means everything. Yearly visits build the data series that lets your doctor say "this is new for you" — the most valuable sentence in medicine.

Trends beat snapshots

Vaccines stay current

Flu every year, tetanus every 10 years, shingles at 50, pneumococcal at 50 — adult vaccines are easy to lose track of because nobody sends you to the school nurse anymore. The annual visit is where the gaps get spotted and filled.

10-year gaps happen

A doctor who knows you

Being an established patient is its own insurance policy: when something does go wrong, you get a same-week appointment with someone who has your history — instead of an urgent care or ER visit with a stranger starting from zero.

$150 visit vs. $2,000 ER

The conversation catches what tests miss

Sleep, mood, weight changes, alcohol, stress — most visits now include a brief depression screen, and the "anything else bothering you?" question surfaces problems no lab panel can. It only works if you show up and answer honestly.

No lab test for this

How often should you go?

"Once a year" is the classic default, and after 50 it's the clear answer. Before that, guidance is risk-based: a healthy 28-year-old can sometimes stretch the interval, while chronic conditions compress it. Answer four questions for a personalized schedule:

1. How old are you?
2. Any chronic conditions? (blood pressure, diabetes, thyroid, asthma…)
3. Close family history of heart disease, diabetes, or cancer?
4. When was your last checkup?

    What it costs

    For most insured Americans this is the rare medical visit that genuinely costs nothing. Without insurance, it's still one of the cheapest items on the healthcare menu — especially compared with what skipping it invites. Typical U.S. figures (estimates; prices vary widely by region and clinic):

    🛡️ With insurance

    Usually $0

    The Affordable Care Act requires ACA-compliant plans to cover in-network preventive care — including the annual wellness visit and recommended screenings — at no copay and no deductible.

    • Covered: the preventive visit, blood pressure check, recommended screenings and labs, routine vaccines
    • Watch for: if you also discuss a new problem at the same visit, that part can be billed as a regular office visit
    • Exceptions: grandfathered and short-term plans don't have to follow these rules

    💵 Without insurance

    $100 – $300

    A self-pay physical at a typical primary care office, before labs. Many practices post cash prices — ask up front.

    • Community health centers charge on a sliding scale based on income
    • Direct primary care memberships run roughly $50–$100/month and include visits

    How to prepare — and what to expect

    You can't DIY this one, but preparation is the difference between a rushed once-over and a genuinely useful hour with someone paid to think about your health. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically.

    ⭐ The #1 habit: book next year's visit before you leave this one

    • Schedule the next physical at the front desk on your way out. Twelve months is far too long to remember on your own — people who book before leaving keep the cadence; people who plan to "call in the spring" mostly don't.
    • Bring a written list: your questions, your medications, and anything new since last year. Chair-side amnesia is real.
    • Before you leave, ask: "What screenings or vaccines am I due for, and is there anything you're watching?" That one question is most of the visit's value.

    📐 Four things worth knowing before you go

    What actually gets checked

    Vitals first, then a head-to-toe pass: eyes, ears, throat, lymph nodes, heart and lung sounds, abdomen, skin, reflexes. None of it hurts, and most of it takes seconds each.

    Screenings stack up by decade

    Each decade adds a layer: cholesterol checks in your 20s, diabetes screening at 35, mammograms at 40, colonoscopy or stool tests at 45, shingles vaccine at 50, bone density at 65. Your doctor tracks the start dates so you don't have to.

    Fasting, decoded

    If fasting labs are ordered: nothing but water for 8–12 hours beforehand — black coffee counts as breaking it for some tests, so skip it. Book a morning slot and eat right after the draw. Most regular medications are still taken; confirm with the office.

    Know your numbers

    When the cuff comes off, ask what it read. Under 120/80 is normal; 130/80 and up is high (per current U.S. guidelines). Same for cholesterol and blood sugar — numbers you know are numbers you can watch.

    See a doctor sooner if you notice

    The annual cadence is for maintenance, not for symptoms. Don't sit on any of these until your next scheduled visit:

    • Chest pain, trouble breathing, sudden weakness or numbness, face drooping, or slurred speech — call 911. These are emergencies, not appointments.
    • Unexplained weight loss — losing weight without trying is one of the most consistently meaningful symptoms in medicine.
    • A new lump, a changing mole, or a sore that won't heal — almost always benign, but exactly the things worth a prompt look.
    • Blood where it shouldn't be — in stool, urine, or a cough — or a clear change in bowel habits lasting more than a couple of weeks.
    • Fatigue, fever, or pain that persists beyond a couple of weeks without an obvious cause.
    • Mood changes that interfere with life — persistent low mood, anxiety, or sleep problems are medical issues your primary care doctor treats every day, and the visit can happen this week, not next year.

    Common questions

    Is the annual physical actually evidence-based?

    For healthy young adults, the rigid "full exam every 365 days" has weaker evidence than you'd guess — some studies show little benefit from the ritual exam itself. What clearly does pay off: blood pressure and cholesterol checks on schedule, cancer screenings starting on time, vaccines staying current, and having an established doctor. The yearly visit is simply the easiest container for all of that, which is why most primary care doctors still recommend it — and why guidance gets firmly annual from 50 on.

    Do I need to fast before the visit?

    Only if fasting labs are ordered — and increasingly they aren't, since modern guidelines accept non-fasting cholesterol panels for most people. Ask when you book: if fasting is needed, it's typically 8–12 hours with water (and usually your regular medications) allowed. Easiest move: book a morning slot and bring a snack for after the blood draw.

    Will the visit really be free with insurance?

    The preventive visit itself — at an in-network provider on an ACA-compliant plan — must be covered with no copay or deductible, along with recommended screenings and vaccines. The famous gotcha: if you also bring up a new or existing problem ("while I'm here, my knee…"), the doctor can bill that discussion as a separate office visit, which is when surprise charges appear. It's fine to discuss problems — just know it may convert part of the visit, or ask the office how they handle billing.

    What if I don't have insurance?

    You still have solid options. Federally funded community health centers charge on a sliding scale based on income and exist in nearly every county. Many practices post cash prices around $100–$300 for a physical. Direct primary care memberships (roughly $50–$100/month) include visits and cheap labs. And direct-to-consumer lab services sell basic panels for far less than hospital lab pricing. Prevention is the part of healthcare where paying out of pocket is genuinely feasible.

    Which vaccines might come up at the visit?

    The usual adult lineup: a flu shot every year, a tetanus booster (Td or Tdap) every 10 years, the shingles vaccine starting at 50, pneumococcal vaccination starting at 50, updated COVID-19 vaccines per current guidance, and RSV vaccination for older adults. Your doctor checks your record against the current schedule — one of the highest-value two-minute conversations in the whole visit.

    I'm on Medicare — is this the same as the "Annual Wellness Visit"?

    Not quite, and the difference matters for billing. Medicare fully covers an Annual Wellness Visit every 12 months — a prevention-focused review of your history, risks, medications, and a personalized screening plan — but it's not a full hands-on physical, and a traditional head-to-toe physical isn't a covered Medicare benefit by itself. Many practices combine the two; ask how yours bills it so nothing surprises you.

    Do I need blood work every single year?

    Not necessarily. For a healthy adult with normal past results, cholesterol every 4–6 years is the standard guideline, and diabetes screening (ages 35–70 if overweight) repeats about every 3 years when normal. Doctors often order panels more frequently because trends are useful and the marginal cost is low — but "no labs this year" can be a perfectly good plan. What should happen every visit is the blood pressure cuff.
