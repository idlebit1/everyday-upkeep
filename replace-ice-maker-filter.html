Replace Your Ice Maker Filter

Your ice maker doesn't just freeze water — it filters it first, pulling out the sediment, chlorine, minerals, and off-tastes that would otherwise turn up frozen in your glass. But that filter clogs. As it does, the ice starts coming out cloudy, small, and slow, and it quietly picks up flavors from the water you can no longer taste anywhere else. Here's the catch most people miss: this may be the very same cartridge as your fridge's water filter, or a separate dedicated ice-maker filter, or the filter tucked inside a standalone ice machine — and some setups have both. Swap the right one about every six months and the ice comes back clear, fast, and clean-tasting; forget it and it fades for a year without anyone noticing why.

🗓️ Typically every 6 months ⏱️ ~5–10 min plus a flush 🧊 Clear, fast, clean-tasting ice 💸 Costs ~$15–50

Why it matters

Ice is the one thing in the kitchen you make from filtered water and then eat — it goes straight into the drink. When the filter behind it clogs, the whole system slides downhill quietly: the ice looks a little worse, tastes a little off, and comes a little slower, and because it happens over months you just adapt. Here's what a spent filter actually does:

A clogged filter means bad ice

Once the filter can't keep up, the ice comes out cloudy, small, and slow-forming, and it picks up the chlorine and off-tastes from the now-unfiltered water — a faint pool-water or stale flavor that ends up in every drink you pour over it.

Cloudy, funny-tasting cubes

It slows and strains the maker

A clogged cartridge chokes the flow of water reaching the ice maker. That means noticeably less ice, longer waits between batches, and a unit that has to work harder against the restriction — the appliance equivalent of drinking through a pinched straw.

Less ice, a straining unit

A saturated filter stops protecting

Filter media only holds so much. Once it's saturated it no longer reduces sediment, chlorine, or minerals — it just passes them straight through. Worse, a wet, spent cartridge left in place can harbor its own buildup, so old ice isn't just unfiltered, it can be picking up flavors from the filter itself.

No protection, its own buildup

It's an easy-to-miss filter

Plenty of people dutifully change the fridge's water filter and never realize the ice maker has its own — or that a standalone ice machine has one at all. So one filter gets swapped on schedule while the other goes years untouched, and the ice keeps quietly declining while everyone blames the water.

Years unchanged & forgotten

How often should you replace it?

About every six months — or whatever your appliance's manual says — is the sensible default for an ice maker filter. But hard, sediment-heavy water clogs a cartridge faster, and a big household running the ice maker hard all day burns through its capacity sooner, both of which pull you toward every three months. Your setup answer decides which filter (or filters) you're actually replacing. Answer four quick questions:

1. What's your water like?
Hard, sediment-heavy water loads the filter media far faster — the same cartridge that lasts six months on clean water can clog in three when it's fighting minerals and grit.
2. How much ice does your household go through?
A filter is rated by how much water passes through it, not by the calendar. A big family emptying the bin all day pushes far more water through the cartridge, using up its capacity sooner.
3. What's your ice maker setup?
This answer decides which filter you replace. On many fridges the ice and dispensed water run through the same cartridge; some have a second dedicated ice-maker or inline filter; standalone machines have their own — and a few homes have both a fridge filter and a separate one.
4. How's the ice right now?
Cloudy, small, slow, or off-tasting ice is the filter telling you it's already overdue — don't wait for the calendar. Clear, fast ice means you're on track and can go by the schedule.

    How to replace your ice maker filter

    The whole job is short, but the first move is figuring out which filter you have — because replacing the wrong one, or leaving a second one untouched, is exactly how people end up with bad ice after a "new filter." Read the techniques first (especially "identify your filter" and the flush step), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Figure out your setup first — and flush the new filter

    • Identify your filter(s) before you buy anything. On many fridges the ice and the dispensed water run through the same cartridge, so you change it once. But some have a separate dedicated ice-maker or inline filter, and standalone or countertop ice machines have their own. Check the manual — and if there are two, don't leave the second one unchanged.
    • Turn off the ice maker and shut the water supply first. Switch the ice maker off and close the saddle valve or shut-off feeding it — or simply follow your fridge's built-in filter-change procedure, which on many models handles the water shut-off for you. That keeps you from a wet, dripping swap.
    • Flush the new filter and discard the first ice. A fresh carbon filter sheds fine black carbon "fines" and trapped air on its first use. Run the flush per the instructions, then throw out the first batch or two of ice and pour off a few glasses of dispensed water so the cloudy, fizzy, gritty first pass never reaches your drink.
    • Use the correct model and seat it fully. Match your appliance's filter number (or a certified-compatible equivalent) and push or twist it home until it locks. A filter that isn't fully seated lets water bypass it — so you get all the hassle and none of the filtering.
    • Reset the filter indicator light. Most fridges have a status light or menu that counts down filter life. After the swap, reset it (usually a press-and-hold) so the reminder is accurate next time — otherwise it either nags early or, worse, never warns you.
    • Know its limits — and clean the maker too. This filter improves taste and reduces sediment and chlorine, but it isn't a purifier. For lead or a specific contaminant, use a filter certified to remove it. And remember: changing the filter is not the same as cleaning the ice maker — do both.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Identify your filter(s) first

    Figure out whether your ice and water share one filter, there's a separate ice-maker or inline filter, or it's a standalone machine — the manual will tell you. Some homes have both a fridge filter and a dedicated one, so nail this down before you buy or swap anything.

    Shut it down

    Turn the ice maker off and close the water shut-off feeding it — or just follow your fridge's built-in filter-change procedure, which often handles the shut-off for you. Either way, you don't want water running while the cartridge is out.

    Swap the filter

    Twist or pull the old cartridge out of its slot (a little water may spill — that's normal), then insert the correct new one and seat it fully until it clicks or locks. If it isn't seated home, water bypasses it and nothing gets filtered.

    Flush & discard

    Run the flush per the instructions, then throw out the first batch or two of ice and pour off a few glasses of dispensed water. A new carbon filter sheds fine black carbon and trapped air on first use — you want that gone before it reaches your glass.

    Reset & restart

    Reset the filter indicator light, switch the ice maker back on, and log the date so the next swap lands in about six months. If your setup has a second filter — a dedicated ice one alongside the fridge water filter — this is your cue not to forget it.

    Signs the filter is overdue — and when to call a pro

    A good filter is invisible: clear ice, fast batches, water that tastes like nothing. These are the signals it's time to swap the cartridge (or cartridges) — and the handful of problems that aren't a filter job at all:

    • Cloudy, small, or slow-forming ice. When the cubes go milky, shrink, or take noticeably longer to drop, the filter is clogging and starving the maker of clean flow. Swap it — and if you have two filters, check both.
    • Off-tasting or smelly ice. A faint chlorine, plastic, or stale flavor in the ice (or the water) means the carbon is spent and no longer scrubbing the taste out. A fresh filter and a flush usually fixes it in a day.
    • Reduced ice output. If the bin used to fill overnight and now doesn't keep up, a restricted filter throttling the water supply is a prime suspect — replace it before assuming the ice maker itself is failing.
    • A filter-change indicator light. The status light or menu counter is your reminder; when it turns red or flags "replace," change the cartridge and reset it. Don't just clear the light and leave the old filter in.
    • You've never changed it — or you change the water filter but not the ice one. If you can't remember the last swap, or you religiously replace the fridge water filter and never touch a separate ice-maker or inline filter, it's overdue. Track down the second one.
    • New ice still bad after a fresh filter and a clean maker. If you've replaced the filter, flushed it, discarded the first batches, and cleaned the ice maker, and the ice is still cloudy, slow, or off — the problem may be low water pressure, a supply issue, or the appliance itself. Check the water pressure and supply line, then call appliance service.
    • Pro tip: a filter change and a machine cleaning are two different jobs, and they work best together — a fresh filter feeding a scaled-up, mildewy machine still makes bad ice, and a spotless machine on a clogged filter does too. Do the pair on the same day.

    Common questions

    How often should I replace the ice maker filter?

    About every six months is the sensible default, and it's what most fridge and ice-machine makers print in the manual — so follow yours if it differs. Two things shorten that window. Hard, sediment-heavy water clogs the media faster, and heavy ice use pushes more water through the cartridge, so a big household on hard water may be closer to every three months. Two things stretch it: soft, clean water and light use. The filter is rated by gallons passed, not by the calendar, so treat six months as a reminder and let the ice itself be the tiebreaker — cloudy, small, slow, or off-tasting means change it now, whatever the date says.

    Is the ice maker filter the same as the fridge water filter?

    Sometimes — and this is the single most confusing part. On many refrigerators, the ice maker and the dispensed water run through the same cartridge, so changing that one filter covers both, and there's nothing else to do. But some fridges have a separate, dedicated ice-maker filter or an inline filter on the supply line in addition to the main water filter, and standalone or countertop ice machines have their own filter entirely. That means a few homes have two filters. The classic mistake is faithfully replacing the water filter while a second ice filter goes years untouched. Check your manual (or look for a second cartridge behind or beneath the fridge, or on the water line) so you know exactly what you're dealing with.

    Why is my ice cloudy, small, or bad-tasting?

    A spent or clogged filter is the most common cause. As the media saturates, it stops reducing chlorine, minerals, and sediment, so the ice picks up off-tastes and can turn cloudy; and as it clogs, it restricts flow, so cubes come out smaller and slower. Start there: replace the filter, flush it, and discard the first batches of ice. Cloudiness can also come from dissolved air and minerals freezing into the cube (partly a water-quality thing), and off-tastes can come from a dirty ice bin or machine that needs cleaning — which is a separate job from the filter. If a fresh filter, a flush, and a clean maker don't fix it, look at water pressure and the supply line before suspecting the appliance.

    Do I need to flush a new filter?

    Yes — always. A fresh carbon filter sheds fine black carbon particles (called "fines") and trapped air the first time water runs through it, which is why the first water looks cloudy or grey and the first ice can come out fizzy, small, or specked. Run the flush your manual specifies — typically a few gallons of dispensed water, or several minutes of run time — then throw out the first batch or two of ice and pour off a few glasses of water before you trust it. Skipping the flush is the number-one reason people install a new filter and think the ice got worse. Give it that first flush and the fines clear out fast.

    How do I reset the filter light?

    Most refrigerators track filter life with a status light or an on-screen counter, and it doesn't reset itself when you swap the cartridge — you have to clear it manually. The usual method is to press and hold the "filter" or "filter reset" button for about three seconds until the light turns from red back to green (or blue); on touchscreen models it's often a menu option. Your manual has the exact combination for your model. Resetting matters in both directions: if you don't, the reminder stays inaccurate — nagging you when the filter is fresh, or, if you cleared it without changing the filter, never warning you when it's actually due.

    Does the filter remove lead — is it a purifier?

    No, not on its own. A standard ice maker or fridge filter is built mainly to improve taste and odor and to reduce chlorine and sediment — it makes the ice clearer and cleaner-tasting, and that's genuinely worthwhile, but it is not a purifier and it does not automatically remove lead, PFAS, or specific contaminants. If you're concerned about a particular contaminant, look for a filter that is certified to reduce it (NSF/ANSI standards spell out what each certification covers — Standard 42 for taste and chlorine, Standard 53 for health contaminants like lead). Match a certified filter to your actual water concern rather than assuming the default cartridge handles everything, and test your water if you're unsure what's in it.
