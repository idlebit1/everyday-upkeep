Service Your HVAC System

Your heating and cooling system is the most expensive machine in the house, and it fails at the worst possible moment — the AC dies in the July heat wave, the furnace quits on the coldest night of January. Almost none of that is bad luck. A licensed tech spends an hour once a year checking the refrigerant charge, cleaning the coils, testing the capacitor, and — on a gas furnace — inspecting the heat exchanger for the crack that leaks carbon monoxide. They catch the weak part in spring, when it's a cheap fix and not a 2 a.m. emergency. The tune-up runs efficiently, holds your warranty, and pays for itself in lower bills. This guide covers what a proper visit includes, how to hire a good tech and dodge the upsell, and the handful of things you should do yourself between visits.

🗓️ Pro tune-up once a year 🔧 ~$75–200 per visit 🌡️ Combo or heat pump → twice a year 🧰 DIY filter ~monthly

Why it matters

HVAC is easy to ignore because it works silently right up until it doesn't — and by then you're paying peak-season emergency rates for a repair that a spring tune-up would have caught for a fraction. Beyond breakdowns, a neglected system quietly wastes energy every month, and a skipped gas-furnace check is a genuine safety risk. Here's what the annual visit buys you:

A tuned system runs efficiently

Dirty coils, a low refrigerant charge, and a struggling blower all force the system to run longer and harder to hit the same temperature — burning far more energy while cooling and heating worse. A clean, correctly charged system does the job in fewer runtime hours, so the visit often pays for itself in the bills.

Higher energy bills every month

It catches failures early

A weak run capacitor, a slowly leaking refrigerant charge, or a worn contactor is cheap and quick to fix in April — and a dead AC in a 98-degree July heat wave when every tech in town is booked solid. Finding the small problem on a calm spring afternoon is the whole point of the visit.

A breakdown at the worst moment

Gas furnaces get a CO safety check

A proper furnace tune-up inspects the heat exchanger and checks combustion — because a cracked heat exchanger can leak carbon monoxide, an odorless, deadly gas, into the air you breathe. This isn't just about efficiency; it's a safety visit. Pair it with working CO detectors and you sleep easier all heating season.

A carbon-monoxide risk you can't smell

It keeps your warranty valid

Most manufacturer warranties on furnaces, ACs, and heat pumps require documented annual professional maintenance. Skip it and if the compressor fails at year six, the maker can deny the claim — leaving you paying full price on a part that should have been covered. The tune-up receipt is the proof they'll ask for.

A voided warranty on a $5,000+ system

How often should you book a tune-up?

Once a year is the sensible floor for any HVAC system. But a combined AC-and-furnace setup — or a heat pump that runs year-round — ideally gets two visits: cooling service in spring, heating service in fall. An older system, one that runs constantly in an extreme climate, or one that's already showing symptoms all push toward twice a year and a sooner call. (Note: the DIY filter change is a separate, roughly monthly job — this quiz is about the professional visit.) Answer four quick questions:

1. What kind of system do you have?
A combo system has two machines to service, and a heat pump runs in both seasons — both ideally get a spring cooling check and a fall heating check. A single-season system needs one well-timed visit before its season starts.
2. How old is the system?
Parts wear with age and hours. An older or hard-run system has more to inspect and is likelier to have a marginal capacitor or a low charge that a second visit a year will catch before it strands you.
3. How hard does it work — climate and use?
A system running flat-out through brutal summers or hard winters logs far more hours and stress than one in a mild climate — more runtime means more wear, and more reason to check it twice a year.
4. Any warning signs lately?
Weak or warm airflow, odd noises, short-cycling, uneven room temps, or a creeping energy bill are the system telling you something's off. Any of these means don't wait for the next scheduled visit — book a look now.

    The cost of skipping it

    A tune-up is one of the cheapest line items your HVAC system will ever generate — and one of the highest-leverage. A single visit runs about $75–200; a maintenance plan bundling two visits a year usually lands around $150–300. Set that against what neglect actually costs: a burned-out compressor, a full system replacement, a peak-season emergency call, wasted energy every month, and a warranty you've quietly voided:

    How to service your HVAC system

    The heart of this is a licensed pro's job — book the visits at the right time, know what a good tune-up should cover so you can tell a real one from an upsell, and then handle the small, safe DIY tasks between visits: the filter, the outdoor unit, and the vents. Read the safety notes first (refrigerant, gas, and electrical work are not DIY), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ This is mostly a licensed pro's job — here's where the line is

    • Leave refrigerant, gas, combustion, and electrical work to a licensed HVAC technician. DIY there risks refrigerant burns and frostbite, gas leaks, carbon-monoxide exposure, and electrical shock — and it voids your warranty. Handling refrigerant without an EPA certification is also illegal. This is not a save-money-by-doing-it-yourself system.
    • A gas-furnace tune-up should include a heat-exchanger and combustion / CO check. A cracked heat exchanger can leak carbon monoxide into your home. Make sure the tech inspects it and tests combustion, and back that up with working CO detectors on every level and near sleeping areas.
    • Change the air filter about monthly — it's the single biggest DIY win. A clogged filter chokes airflow, drives up your bill, and can freeze the coil or overheat the furnace. Note the size printed on the frame. (See our companion guide on changing the HVAC filter.)
    • Keep the outdoor condenser clear — power OFF first. Rake leaves and debris away and keep 2+ feet of clearance around the unit. Before you clear inside the fins or gently rinse them, shut off power at the disconnect box by the unit (or the breaker) so the fan can't start.
    • Keep supply and return vents open and unblocked. Furniture, rugs, and closed vents starve the system of airflow and make it work harder. And check the thermostat — replace its batteries if it takes them.
    • Get a written tune-up checklist, and be wary of high-pressure upsells. A good tech gives you documented readings. If someone insists you "need a whole new system" on the spot, get a second opinion before committing to a big-ticket job — and never let anyone bypass a safety switch to keep a failing system limping along.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Book the right visits

    Schedule cooling service in spring and heating service in fall — before the season hits, when techs are cheaper, available, and not slammed with emergencies. Ask exactly what the tune-up includes before you book.

    What a good tune-up covers

    A real visit checks the refrigerant charge, cleans the coils, tests the capacitor and electrical, inspects the blower and belts, checks airflow and the drain line, calibrates the thermostat — and on furnaces, inspects the heat exchanger and burners.

    Your monthly DIY: the filter

    The one job that's fully yours and matters most: swap the air filter about once a month and note the size printed on the frame. A clean filter is the biggest thing you control between professional visits.

    Clear the outdoor unit

    Shut the power off at the disconnect, rake away leaves and debris, keep 2 feet of clearance all around, and gently rinse the fins from the outside with a hose — never a pressure washer, which bends the delicate fins.

    Between visits

    Keep vents open and unblocked, watch for the warning signs below, and keep your service records — the paper trail your warranty depends on. Log the date of the next tune-up so it doesn't slip.

    Signs your system needs attention — and when to call a pro

    A healthy HVAC system is quiet and even: steady airflow, consistent temperatures, a bill that doesn't creep. These are the signals it's overdue for a look — and the ones that mean call a licensed HVAC pro now, not at the next scheduled visit:

    • Weak or warm airflow. If air is barely coming out of the vents, or the AC is blowing air that isn't cold (or the furnace air that isn't warm), the system isn't doing its job — a clogged filter, low refrigerant, or a failing blower. Change the filter first; if that doesn't fix it, call a pro.
    • Short-cycling — turning on and off every few minutes. A system that rapidly starts and stops never reaches temperature, wears itself out, and spikes your bill. It can signal a refrigerant, thermostat, or sizing problem, or an overheating furnace. Get it diagnosed.
    • Strange noises or smells. Grinding, screeching, banging, or rattling means a mechanical problem; a burning or musty smell, or especially a rotten-egg (gas) odor, is urgent. Shut it down and call — a gas smell means leave and call the gas company.
    • Ice on the refrigerant lines or coil. Ice on an AC in summer points to low refrigerant or airflow problems. Turn the system off to let it thaw and call a technician — running it iced-up can damage the compressor.
    • Water pooling near the indoor unit. A clogged condensate drain line or a cracked pan can leak water around the furnace or air handler, risking mold and water damage. It's a common, fixable problem — but don't let it sit.
    • Rising energy bills or uneven room temperatures. A bill climbing with no change in the weather, or rooms that are always too hot or too cold, means the system is losing efficiency. A tune-up often restores it; persistent unevenness may need a duct or airflow look.
    • A furnace that won't stay lit, or a tripped CO alarm. A furnace that keeps shutting off may have a safety sensor doing its job — don't bypass it. And if a carbon-monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone out, then call 911 from outside. Don't re-enter until it's cleared.

    Common questions

    How often should I service my HVAC system?

    Once a year is the minimum, and it's the sensible default for a single-season system: an AC-only setup serviced in spring, or a furnace-only setup serviced in fall. But a combined AC-and-furnace system has two machines to maintain, and a heat pump runs in both seasons — so both ideally get two professional visits a year, cooling service in spring and heating service in fall. Older systems, systems that run hard in an extreme climate, or any system showing warning signs should lean toward twice a year too. Separately from all of this, you should change the air filter yourself about once a month — that's a small DIY task, not the professional tune-up. Timing matters: booking in the shoulder seasons (spring and fall), before the summer and winter rush, gets you better availability and often better prices.

    What does an HVAC tune-up include?

    A thorough tune-up is more than a quick look. On the cooling side, the tech checks the refrigerant charge, cleans the condenser and evaporator coils, tests the run capacitor and electrical connections, inspects the blower motor and any belts, checks airflow, and clears the condensate drain line. On the heating side — especially a gas furnace — they inspect the heat exchanger for cracks, check the burners and the flame, and test combustion and carbon-monoxide levels, which makes it a safety inspection as well as a maintenance visit. They'll also calibrate or test the thermostat and lubricate moving parts as needed. A good technician hands you a written checklist with the actual readings so you can see what was measured. If a "tune-up" is just a filter swap and a glance, you're not getting what you paid for.

    Can I service my HVAC system myself?

    Partly. The tasks that are genuinely DIY — and that you should do between professional visits — are changing the air filter about monthly (the single biggest thing you control), keeping the outdoor condenser clear of leaves and debris with 2+ feet of clearance and an occasional gentle rinse of the fins (power off at the disconnect first), keeping supply and return vents open and unblocked, and checking the thermostat and its batteries. What you should not DIY is anything involving refrigerant, gas lines, combustion, or electrical work. Those carry real risks — refrigerant burns, gas leaks, carbon-monoxide exposure, electrical shock — handling refrigerant without certification is illegal, and DIY there typically voids your warranty. The rule of thumb: if it involves the refrigerant loop, the gas or the burner, or opening up the electrical, it's a licensed technician's job.

    Does skipping maintenance void my warranty?

    It can, and it's one of the most expensive surprises homeowners hit. Most manufacturer warranties on furnaces, air conditioners, and heat pumps include language requiring documented annual professional maintenance to keep the coverage valid. If a major component — say the compressor — fails while under warranty, the manufacturer can ask for proof that the system was properly maintained, and if you can't produce service records, they can deny the claim. On a system worth thousands of dollars, that turns a covered repair into a full-price one. The fix is simple: have the annual tune-up done by a licensed pro and keep the receipts and the written checklist. That paper trail is exactly what a warranty claim asks for, and it costs far less than the coverage it protects.

    Is annual service really worth the money?

    For a machine that costs $5,000–12,000 or more to replace, yes — it's cheap insurance. A tune-up runs about $75–200 a visit (less on a maintenance plan), and it earns that back several ways. It keeps the system running efficiently, so you're not quietly overpaying on energy bills every month a dirty, low-charged unit runs harder than it should. It catches small problems — a weak capacitor, a low charge, a worn contactor — while they're cheap fixes in a calm season, instead of emergency breakdowns during a peak-season heat wave at after-hours rates. For gas furnaces, it's a carbon-monoxide safety check. And it preserves the warranty that protects your biggest parts. The one visit that doesn't pay off is the rare year nothing was wrong — but you can't know that in advance, and the combustion check alone is worth it.

    What's the difference between the tune-up and changing the filter?

    They're two different maintenance layers, and you need both. Changing the air filter is a quick DIY task you do yourself about once a month: you slide out the dirty filter and slide in a fresh one of the same size. It protects airflow and efficiency, and it's the single most impactful thing you can do between professional visits — but it's not a substitute for service. The annual tune-up is a licensed technician's job: checking refrigerant, cleaning coils, testing electrical components, inspecting the heat exchanger, and testing combustion — work that requires tools, training, and often certifications you don't have. Think of the filter as brushing your teeth and the tune-up as the dentist visit. Doing the filter faithfully keeps the system healthier between checkups, but it doesn't replace the checkup.
