Pressure Wash Your Deck or Patio

A season of pollen, algae, and grime turns outdoor surfaces slippery, blotchy, and — on wood — quietly rotten underneath. One careful wash a year strips all of it off in an afternoon. The whole trick is matching pressure to surface: what cleans concrete will carve a wood deck.

🗓️ Typically once a year ⏱️ 2–4 hours for a typical deck 💸 Rental ~$40–100/day 🚫 Never a 0° tip — on anything

Why it matters

The green film on a shaded deck isn't just ugly — it's algae and mildew, and they're at their slickest exactly when it rains. Add the grit that traps moisture against wood and the stains that set in permanently, and the annual wash starts looking less like cosmetics and more like structural upkeep:

Slip-and-fall hazard

Algae and mildew form a film that turns genuinely slick the moment it's wet — wood, composite, and smooth concrete alike. Steps and shaded corners are the classic spots, and it's a real injury risk, not a cosmetic one.

Slickest right when it rains

Rot from trapped grime

Dirt, leaf litter, and organic film hold moisture against wood long after the rain stops — and damp wood is what fungus eats. Grimy board gaps and skirting are where decks rot first, years ahead of clean ones.

$30+ per board replaced

Stains that set for good

Mold, tannin, rust, and leaf stains start as surface film and slowly soak into wood grain and porous concrete. Wash this season and they rinse off; wait a few seasons and no pressure setting will pull them back out.

Permanent blotches

A reseal that won't stick

Stain and sealer only bond to clean, dry wood. Brush them over grime or mildew and they peel within a season — so a thorough wash isn't optional prep, it's the first half of every reseal job.

Failed $100+ stain job

How often should you wash it?

Once a year is the standard rhythm for most decks and patios — spring, after the pollen dump, is the classic slot, or right before you reseal. But shade, humidity, and surface material move the interval a lot. Answer four questions:

1. What's the surface?
This also decides your pressure and nozzle — wood wants low pressure and a wide fan; concrete shrugs off far more.
2. How much shade and tree cover does it get?
Shade keeps surfaces damp, and overhanging trees add sap, pollen, and leaf litter — the full algae buffet.
3. What's your climate like?
4. Is the surface on a stain-or-seal schedule?
Most wood decks want stain or sealer every 2–3 years, and the wash always comes first.

    How to pressure wash a deck or patio

    Plan on 2–4 hours for a typical deck: sweep, soap, dwell, wash, rinse. The techniques below — nozzle, distance, and direction — are what separate "clean" from "chewed up." Work through the checklist; your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Safety first — a pressure washer is a cutting tool that looks like a hose

    • Never point it at people, pets, or your own feet. Close-range spray can cut skin and drive water and debris deep under it. Injection injuries look minor on the surface and are surgical emergencies — if it breaks skin, get medical care the same day.
    • Eyes and ears: safety glasses always (the spray throws grit back at you), and hearing protection with gas units.
    • Feet on the ground, never on a ladder. The wand kicks back when you pull the trigger — a spraying wand and a ladder don't mix. Wear closed shoes with grip; you're creating wet, slick surfaces as you go.
    • Electric units: plug into a GFCI-protected outlet, keep cords and connections out of the water path, and use an outdoor-rated extension cord only if the manual allows one.
    • Gas units: run them outdoors only — exhaust is carbon monoxide — and never refuel a hot engine.
    • Mind what's around: windows, light fixtures, outlets, and anyone who might walk through the spray. Brace before you pull the trigger; the kickback is real.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Pressure washer (rented or owned) 25° and 40° fan tips (or adjustable wand) Deck/concrete cleaner or washer detergent Stiff broom Garden hose Safety glasses Closed shoes with grip Plastic sheeting / painter's tape Surface cleaner attachment (concrete, optional)

    📐 Four techniques worth knowing

    Pick the nozzle by surface

    Wood and composite: 40° to start, 25° at most, and low pressure (~500–1,200 PSI for soft wood). Concrete: 25° or a surface cleaner, 15° only for stubborn spots. The red 0° tip cuts like a blade — never use it on anything you own.

    Distance is your pressure dial

    On wood, hold the tip 12–18 inches off the surface and creep closer only if it isn't cleaning. High pressure up close "furs" the grain into fuzz and carves wand-shaped gouges — damage no sealer hides.

    Sweep with the grain

    On wood, run long, steady strokes parallel to the boards, overlapping each pass and keeping the wand moving — pausing in one spot etches a mark. Feather the start and end of each stroke so passes don't leave lines.

    Let the detergent do the work

    Apply cleaner with the soap nozzle or a pump sprayer, let it dwell ~5–10 minutes without drying, then rinse. Chemistry loosens the grime so low pressure can carry it away — that's how you clean wood without shredding it.

    Rent, buy, or hire?

    For a once-a-year job, all three options are defensible — it comes down to how much surface you have and how much you enjoy this kind of Saturday:

    • Rent (~$40–100/day, typical): the right call for most one-deck households. Rental units are usually gas machines with more power than wood needs, so confirm the pressure adjusts down (or stay far back with a 40° tip). One day covers a deck, patio, and walkway easily — sweep and pre-treat before you pick it up so the rental clock works for you.
    • Buy electric (~$100–400): the sweet spot if you'll also wash siding trim, fences, cars-adjacent driveways, or patio furniture. Electric units in the 1,500–2,000 PSI range are quieter, lighter, fume-free, and — usefully — closer to the gentle end wood wants. They pay for themselves in two or three skipped rentals.
    • Buy gas (~$300–700+): worth it mainly for big properties, long driveways, or lots of concrete. More power than a wood deck should ever see up close; plan on the wide tips and real distance.
    • Hire a pro (often ~$0.15–0.40+/sq ft, commonly $100–300 a visit): the hedge when the surface is delicate, two stories are involved, or the deck is overdue for a stain anyway — many staining contractors bundle the wash. A good pro also knows when to put the pressure washer down and soft-wash instead, which is exactly the judgment that protects old wood.
    • Or skip the machine entirely: for soft, old, or delicate surfaces, a garden hose, deck cleaner, and a stiff brush do the same chemistry with zero gouge risk. Slower, gentler, and often the right answer for weathered cedar and historic masonry.

    What not to blast

    A pressure washer makes everything look like a target. These aren't:

    • People, pets, and feet — including spraying "near" them for fun. Close-range spray cuts skin and causes injection injuries that need same-day medical attention.
    • Window seals and screens. High pressure drives water past glazing and weatherstripping into the wall, and shreds screens. Wash near windows with the hose, low and angled away.
    • Electrical anything: outlets, light fixtures, meter boxes, AC condensers, doorbells. Tape plastic over outdoor outlets before you start and give fixtures a wide berth.
    • Old or soft mortar. Pressure rakes the joints right out of older brick and stone patios. Use low pressure at an angle, or the brush-and-hose method.
    • Your car. Automotive paint is thinner than it looks; pressure washers chip edges, peel clear coat, and force water past seals. A bucket wash or a foam cannon at low pressure is the car-safe version.
    • Anything painted before ~1978. Blasting old paint can scatter lead dust and chips across your yard. If the structure predates 1978 and the paint is original layers, test it before disturbing it — that's an EPA-rules job, not a Saturday rinse.
    • Roofs and gutters from below — and not from a ladder either. Shingle granules wash off, water drives under the courses, and the kickback problem gets worse with height. Roofs are soft-wash territory.

    Common questions

    Will a pressure washer damage my wood deck?

    Only if you let it. Wood is the most damage-prone surface you'll ever point a wand at: too much pressure or too close a tip "furs" the grain into fuzz, etches wand marks, and gouges soft spots. The safe recipe is low pressure (roughly 500–1,200 PSI for soft woods like cedar and pine; pressure-treated lumber tolerates somewhat more), a wide 40° or 25° fan tip, 12–18 inches of distance, steady passes with the grain, and detergent doing the heavy lifting. Test a hidden corner first — if the surface fuzzes, back off the pressure or step back.

    Can I pressure wash composite decking?

    Usually yes, but check your manufacturer's care guide first — some brands cap the pressure (often in the 1,500–3,100 PSI range with a fan tip at distance), and exceeding their instructions can void the warranty. Composite doesn't rot, but it does scratch and etch, and mildew grows on the organic film that settles on it. Low pressure, wide tip, composite-safe cleaner, and you're fine. When in doubt, soap and a soft brush with a hose rinse is always within spec.

    Do I really need detergent, or is water enough?

    Use the detergent. Plain water at high pressure can blast grime off, but on wood "high pressure" is exactly what you're trying to avoid — the cleaner is what lets low pressure work. A deck or concrete cleaner (or the washer manufacturer's detergent) breaks down algae, mildew, and embedded dirt during a 5–10 minute dwell, so the rinse pass just carries it away. Skip it and you'll be tempted to compensate with pressure, which is how decks get chewed up.

    How soon after washing can I stain or seal?

    Wait until the wood is truly dry — typically 24–48 hours of warm, dry weather, longer if it's humid or shaded. Stain and sealer applied to damp wood won't penetrate or bond, and you'll watch it peel by next summer. Many stain labels specify a moisture threshold; the simple field test is to sprinkle a few drops of water on a board — if it soaks in within a minute or two, the wood is ready to accept stain.

    Can I pressure wash my siding and windows while I'm at it?

    Siding: cautiously, and mostly no for the windows. Vinyl siding tolerates careful low-pressure washing if you keep the spray angled slightly downward — spraying upward drives water behind the panels and into the wall. Wood and fiber-cement siding want even gentler treatment, and stucco and older brick can be damaged outright. Windows, their seals, and screens should never take direct pressure; clean those with a hose and a brush. For a whole-house exterior, "soft washing" (low pressure plus cleaning solution) is the standard pro method.

    What about my car?

    No. Automotive clear coat chips and peels under concentrated pressure, especially at panel edges and anywhere there's an existing stone chip, and high pressure forces water past door and trim seals. If you want to use the machine on the car, it needs the lowest setting, the widest tip, real distance, and a foam attachment — at which point a bucket and mitt are honestly faster and safer.

    Electric or gas — which should I get?

    For decks and patios, electric is the quiet favorite: 1,500–2,000 PSI units are light, fume-free, start with a switch, and sit naturally in the gentle range wood prefers — just keep them on a GFCI outlet and the cord out of the runoff. Gas units bring 2,800–3,300+ PSI and faster flow, which is great for big concrete areas and long fences but overkill up close on wood, plus noise, exhaust, and winter fuel storage. Match the machine to your biggest regular surface, not your toughest once-ever stain.
