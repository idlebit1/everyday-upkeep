Wash Your Interior Windows

It happens so gradually you stop seeing it: a soft film of dust, cooking haze, fingerprints, and pet nose-prints settles on the glass until the whole room feels a little dim and a little tired. Then you wash one window the right way — dust first, wash with suds, pull a squeegee down in a clean reverse-S — and the difference is startling. The light pours in, the room reads cared-for, and the tracks no longer hide a damp little museum of dead bugs and grime. Done twice a year with the right method, it's streak-free glass in an afternoon instead of the endless smearing most people give up on.

🗓️ Typically every 6 months ⏱️ ~5–10 min per window ☀️ Lets in noticeably more light 💸 Costs ~$5–15

Why it matters

Glass is the one surface in the house you look through rather than at, so the film that builds on it sneaks up on you — the room just slowly dims and feels a little less fresh without any obvious culprit. Then there's everything the window collects that you do see, once you go looking. Here's what the buildup does:

Clean glass lets in more light

A film of dust and haze scatters daylight before it reaches the room, so dirty windows quietly make a space feel dim and grey. Wash them and you get a noticeable bump in natural light — the cheapest way there is to make a room feel brighter and cared-for.

A dimmer, tireder-looking room

Film and prints build up

Dust, cooking haze, hairspray, hands, and pet nose-prints all leave a layer on the glass. It accumulates so slowly you stop seeing it — until a low sun rakes across the window and lights up every smudge and streak at once.

Fingerprints & nose-prints everywhere

Tracks & sills collect grime

The tracks and sills are a gutter for everything that drifts in: dust, dead bugs, pollen, and grit. Add the condensation that runs down the glass and you get damp, packed dirt — and in a chronically wet window, even mildew or mold growing in the corners.

Dead bugs, grit & even mold

The right method beats smearing

Most people's windows end up streakier after cleaning — too much cleaner, a dry rag, and direct sun guarantee it. Dust first, wash with a little suds, then squeegee or buff dry, and you get genuinely streak-free glass instead of an afternoon of chasing smears.

Endless streaks & do-overs

How often should you wash them?

Twice a year — spring and fall — is the sensible default for interior glass. But a houseful of kids and dogs leaving prints, hard water that spots fast, or a wall of sunny south-facing windows all push toward quarterly, while a tidy guest room can stretch to once a year. Answer four quick questions:

1. How dirty do the windows get — and how many?
More glass, a kitchen nearby, or a busy household all load the windows faster — and the more there are, the more it pays to stay ahead so the job never becomes an all-day slog.
2. Pets or kids in the house?
Little hands and curious noses press right against the glass, so kid-and-pet homes get a fresh crop of smudges and nose-prints on the lower panes within days of a wash.
3. Do your windows spot up with hard water?
Hard water leaves mineral spots as it dries, and over time those etch in. Spotty glass is worth washing more often (and drying fully) so the minerals never get a chance to bake on.
4. Lots of bright, sunny-side windows?
Sunny windows show every streak the moment the light hits them, so they reward more frequent washing — and they're the ones you must clean on a cloudy day or the cleaner dries before you can wipe it.

    The cost of skipping it

    The supplies are about as cheap as cleaning gets — a squeegee, a couple of microfiber cloths, and either a glass cleaner or a homemade vinegar-and-water mix.     How to wash your interior windows

    The order is the whole secret: dry-dust the frame, tracks, and sills first, then wash the glass top to bottom, squeegee or buff it dry, catch the edges, and finish with the tracks. Read the techniques first (especially "never in the sun" and "dust before you wet anything"), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Work smart — sun, ladders, and the right cleaner

    • Never wash windows in direct sun or on hot glass. The cleaner dries before you can wipe it and bakes into streaks you then have to redo. Work on a cloudy day, in the morning or evening, or on whichever side of the house is in shade right now.
    • Stand on a stable step stool — never furniture. Reaching tall windows from a chair, a windowsill, or a stack of boxes is how an easy chore turns into a fall. Use a step stool or short ladder on a level, hard floor, and keep your weight centered.
    • Don't lean out of an open window to reach the outside. This guide is for interior glass; reaching out over a sill — especially above the ground floor — isn't worth the risk. Save the exterior for a day you can do it safely from outside, or hire it out.
    • Skip ammonia cleaners on tinted or filmed glass. Ammonia (and many blue commercial cleaners) can cloud or peel low-E coatings, privacy film, and window tint over time. On treated glass, use a mild soap-and-water or vinegar mix instead, and check any film's care instructions.
    • Wring it out — nothing should drip. A sopping cloth or squeegee runs water down into the sash, behind trim, and onto the floor or wall below. Wring the cloth and wipe the squeegee blade so it's damp, not dripping, and lay a towel along the sill to catch the rest.
    • Test a new cleaner on a corner first. Especially on older or specialty glass, dab a small low corner and check before you do the whole pane, so a reaction shows up somewhere it won't matter.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Dry-dust the frame & tracks first

    Before any water comes near it, vacuum or brush the loose dust out of the frame, the corners, the tracks, and the sill. Wet that dust and you just make mud that smears everywhere — dry removal first is the step everyone skips and then regrets.

    Wash with suds, top to bottom

    Wet the glass with a little glass cleaner or a sudsy sponge and loosen the film, always working from the top down so the dirty runoff lands on glass you haven't done yet — not on a pane you just finished.

    Squeegee in a reverse-S

    Pull the squeegee down and across in an overlapping S, wiping the blade dry on a cloth after every pass. Each stroke clears the glass and the runoff together, so it dries clear instead of in streaks. No squeegee? Buff dry with a clean microfiber.

    Dry the edges & catch drips

    The squeegee leaves a little water in the corners and along the bottom rail, and that's where streaks and water marks form as it dries. Run a dry microfiber around the perimeter and over the bottom edge to catch every last drip.

    Scrub & clear the tracks

    Loosen the packed grime in the corners with an old toothbrush, then vacuum or wipe the debris away — wet it down with a little soapy water only after the loose stuff is out. Finish by wiping down the sill so the whole window reads clean.

    Signs your windows are overdue — and when to call a pro

    A clean window is nearly invisible: clear glass, bright light, tracks you never think about. These are the signals it's overdue for a wash — and the handful of problems that aren't a cleaning job at all and are worth a pro:

    • A film you only see when the sun hits the glass. If a low morning or evening sun lights up haze, smears, and old streaks, the windows are due — that film is scattering daylight all day, you just can't see it head-on.
    • Visible fingerprints, nose-prints, or splatter. Once you're noticing the smudges from across the room, it's well past time, especially on the lower panes in a kid-and-pet household.
    • Grime, dead bugs, or grit packed in the tracks. The tracks are the early-warning sign — they fill up faster than the glass shows dirt, so a grimy track usually means the whole window is ready for attention.
    • Fog or moisture between the panes. Condensation trapped inside a double- or triple-pane unit means the seal has failed — and no amount of cleaning reaches it, because the surfaces are inside the sealed glass. That's an insulated-glass-unit (IGU) replacement or a glazing repair, not a cleaning. Call a window or glazing pro.
    • Cloudy, permanent hard-water etching. If spots stay after a thorough wash and feel rough or look milky, minerals have etched into the glass surface. Mild cases sometimes respond to a polishing compound or specialty hard-water remover; deep etching needs glass restoration or replacement — a pro's call.
    • High, fixed, or hard-to-reach windows. Tall stairwell glass, big fixed picture windows, second-story panes you'd have to lean out to reach, or anything needing a tall ladder is a job for a professional window cleaner with the right gear — not worth a fall.
    • Pro tip: interior glass twice a year pairs with exterior glass about once a year. If the outside is reachable and safe, do it on a cool, overcast day; if it isn't, a window-cleaning service does inside-and-out in one visit, often for less than you'd expect.

    Common questions

    How do I get genuinely streak-free windows?

    Three things do most of the work. First, never clean glass in direct sun or when it's warm — the cleaner flashes off before you can wipe it, and that's the number-one cause of streaks; work in the shade or on a cloudy day. Second, use less cleaner than you think; a thin film wiped fully off beats a flood that leaves residue behind. Third, finish by drying — pull a squeegee down in overlapping passes, wiping the blade after each one, or buff the glass with a clean, dry microfiber until it squeaks. Dust the frame and tracks before you wet anything, and dry the edges and corners last, and you'll get clear glass instead of an afternoon of chasing smears.

    Vinegar and water, or a commercial glass cleaner?

    Both work well; it's mostly preference and budget. A DIY mix — roughly one part white vinegar to four parts water, sometimes with a single drop of dish soap to cut grease — costs almost nothing, leaves no haze, and is gentle on most glass. Commercial glass cleaners are convenient and often cut greasy kitchen film a little faster. The one real caution is ammonia: many blue commercial cleaners contain it, and ammonia can cloud or degrade low-E coatings, tint, and privacy film over time. If your glass is treated or filmed, use the vinegar mix or a plain mild soap-and-water solution, and check the film's care instructions. For grease, warm water with a drop of dish soap to wash, then a vinegar-water spray to finish, is a reliable combination.

    Why can't I clean windows when the sun is on them?

    Because the glass is warm and the cleaner evaporates almost as fast as you spray it. It dries into a film before you have a chance to wipe it away, and that dried residue is exactly what shows up as streaks and cloudiness — which then have to be re-washed and re-dried, usually more than once. The fix is simple: clean each window when it's in shade. Follow the sun around the house and do whichever side is shaded, or pick an overcast day, or work early morning or evening. Cool glass gives you time to wash, squeegee, and dry before anything sets.

    Is newspaper better than microfiber?

    It's the classic trick and it does work — crumpled newspaper is mildly abrasive, lint-free, and the ink used to help buff glass to a shine, which is why your grandparents swore by it. But modern microfiber is genuinely better for most people: it's far more absorbent, leaves no lint or ink residue, can be machine-washed and reused for years, and is easier on your hands. Today's newsprint also uses different inks and thinner paper, so it can transfer ink to painted frames or your fingers. Keep a couple of microfiber cloths — one to wash and one dry to buff and dry the edges — and you won't miss the newspaper.

    How do I clean grimy, packed window tracks?

    Work dry first. Vacuum or brush out as much loose dust, grit, and dead bugs as you can before adding any water, because wetting that debris just turns it to mud. Then loosen the packed grime in the corners with an old toothbrush — a little baking soda or a spritz of vinegar helps on stubborn buildup — and scrub along the channel. Wipe or vacuum out the loosened muck, give the track a final wipe with a soapy cloth, and dry it. For a track that won't come clean with a cloth, wrap a thin cloth or paper towel around a flathead screwdriver or butter knife to reach right into the corner. If you find mildew or mold, clean it with a vinegar or diluted-bleach solution and dry the track fully, since standing moisture is what grew it.

    How often should I wash windows — inside vs. outside?

    For interior glass, about every six months — spring and fall — is the sensible default. Tighten to quarterly if you have kids or pets pressing on the glass, hard water that spots quickly, lots of sunny-side windows that show every streak, or simply a lot of windows in a busy house; a tidy, low-traffic room can stretch to once a year. Exterior glass typically only needs doing about once a year, since you're not fighting indoor cooking film and fingerprints out there — though it picks up more from rain, pollen, and dust, so do it on a cool, overcast day for the same streak-free result. Many people do the outside themselves once a year and have a pro handle anything high or hard to reach.
