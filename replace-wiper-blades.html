Replace Your Wiper Blades

Wiper rubber doesn't wear out from wiping — it dies of old age. Sun, ozone, heat, and ice harden and crack the edge whether you used the wipers or not, and you find out in the first downpour. Streaky wipers mean it's time: ten minutes, no tools, and the rain disappears again.

🗓️ Typically every 6–12 months ⏱️ ~10 min, no tools 💵 $20–60 a pair, DIY 🛡️ Protects a $300–1,500 windshield

Why it matters

Wipers are the one wear item that fails exactly when you need it most — the rubber degrades quietly in dry weather, then the first storm reveals it. Here's what a $25 blade is actually protecting:

You drive by sight

Most of the information you use to drive comes in through the windshield. Worn blades smear instead of clearing, and at night every smear scatters oncoming headlights into glare — right when seeing the lane line matters most.

Worst exactly when it rains

Torn rubber scratches glass

When the rubber splits or pulls loose, the blade's bare metal or hard plastic frame rides directly on the glass. A few sweeps can grind a permanent arc-shaped scratch that no polish removes — and that's a new windshield, plus camera recalibration on many newer cars.

$300–$1,500 windshield

It can fail inspection

In states with safety inspections, torn or non-functioning wipers are a standard check item — a $25 part can mean a failed sticker, a re-test fee, and another trip to the station. Inspectors look for exactly the cracking you've been ignoring.

Failed sticker + re-test trip

They age even parked

UV light, ozone, and heat harden the rubber; winter adds ice scraping and freeze-thaw cracking. A car that sat all summer can have blades that crumble at the first autumn storm — sudden failure, in the dark, on the highway.

Sudden failure mid-storm

How often should you replace them?

"Every 6 to 12 months" is the typical range — hot-sun and harsh-winter climates live at the short end, mild garage-kept cars at the long end. And symptoms outrank any calendar. Answer four questions:

1. What does your weather do to rubber?
2. Where does the car spend its days?
3. How do the current blades behave in rain?
4. How much wiping do they actually do?

    How to replace your wiper blades

    No tools, no jack, no grease — the whole job is two clicks per blade. Read the techniques first (the cardboard trick alone is worth the visit), then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ One real hazard: the arm is a loaded spring

    • A raised wiper arm is spring-loaded against the glass. If a bare metal arm snaps back with no blade on it, it can chip or crack the windshield. Keep a hand on it, always.
    • Lay folded cardboard or a towel on the glass under the arm the whole time it's up. If your grip slips, the pad takes the hit instead of the glass.
    • Never walk away from a raised, bladeless arm — not even for the ten seconds it takes to unwrap the new blade. Unwrap first.
    • Park with the key off so a bumped wiper stalk can't sweep a bare arm across the windshield mid-swap.
    • If the release tab won't give, stop and check the instructions — forcing it bends the arm, and a bent arm means streaks no new blade can fix.

    📐 Four things worth knowing

    The J-hook release

    Most cars use a J-hook: the arm's curled end hooks over the blade's center connector. Press or lift the small tab, then slide the blade toward the arm's base and out of the hook. Pin and bayonet arms exist too — aftermarket blades ship with adapters for all three.

    The cardboard trick

    The arm spring is strong enough that a bare arm snapping back can crack the glass. Fold cardboard (or a towel) onto the windshield under the raised arm before you touch the release — now a slip costs nothing.

    Sizes differ per side

    Driver and passenger blades are often several inches apart, and the rear hatch blade — the one everyone forgets — is usually smaller still and sometimes a special fitting. Use the lookup book or app at the parts store, or measure each old blade.

    Finish the job: glass and fluid

    New rubber on dirty glass streaks on day one — road film and wax are half of all "bad wiper" complaints. Clean the windshield while you're there, and top the washer fluid so the new blades never have to wipe dry.

    Make your blades last longer

    You can't stop rubber from aging, but you can stop helping it along. A little care stretches a 6-month blade toward the 12-month end of the range:

    • Never run the wipers on dry glass. Dry wiping grinds dust across the edge like sandpaper. One squirt of washer fluid first — it's there to lubricate the wipe, not just rinse bugs.
    • Don't use the wipers as an ice scraper. Dragging blades across frozen glass tears chunks out of the edge. Scrape and defrost first; the wipers handle the meltwater.
    • Free frozen blades gently before driving. If the rubber is frozen to the glass, peeling the arm up rips the edge. Run the defroster until they release on their own.
    • Before snow, lift the arms or use the service position. Blades that aren't frozen to the glass survive the storm — and you can scrape the windshield without working around them. (Some manuals advise against leaving arms up for long periods in wind; a windshield cover works too.)
    • Wipe the rubber edge monthly with a damp cloth or a bit of washer fluid on a paper towel. The black grime that comes off is what's been streaking — this alone often buys a quiet month or two.
    • Keep the washer reservoir topped. An empty tank tempts you into dry wipes, and in winter, fluid rated for freezing temperatures keeps the whole system working when you need it most.
    • Park in shade or a garage when you can. UV and heat are the biggest rubber-killers — the same parking habit that protects your paint protects the blades.

    Common questions

    Can I just replace the rubber refill instead of the whole blade?

    Sometimes. Refills (just the rubber insert) cost a few dollars and work fine if the frame is straight, the hinges still articulate, and the maker actually sells refills for your blade — common for conventional frames, rare for beam blades. The trade-off is fiddliness: sliding a new insert into the little claws without kinking it takes patience. Whole blades cost more but are two clicks and foolproof. If the frame is bent or the blade chatters even with good rubber, replace the whole thing.

    Why do my brand-new blades still streak?

    Usually it's the glass, not the blade. Road film, exhaust residue, and old wax build an invisible layer that fresh rubber skips over. Clean the windshield thoroughly with glass cleaner (or a little rubbing alcohol on a cloth) and the streaks typically vanish. Two other suspects: a protective plastic sleeve some new blades ship with — check that it's been peeled off the rubber — and a bent wiper arm that no blade can compensate for.

    What do the symptoms mean — streaking vs. chattering vs. squealing?

    Streaking (lines of water left behind) means the edge is worn, torn, or dirty. Chattering or skipping (the blade judders across in hops) usually means the rubber has hardened and taken a curved "set," or the arm is slightly twisted. Squealing often means dry glass or hardened rubber. Bands of missed water mean the frame is bent or a hinge is seized. A visibly split or torn edge means stop using that wiper — the frame can touch glass.

    Beam vs. conventional vs. hybrid — which should I buy?

    Conventional (frame-style) blades are cheapest and work fine in mild climates, but the exposed frame packs with snow and ice. Beam blades are a single curved spring strip — even pressure on curved glass, nothing for ice to clog, usually a few dollars more, and they're what most new cars ship with. Hybrids put an aero shell over a frame and sit in between. In a hard-winter climate, beam (or a rubber-booted winter blade) is the easy call; elsewhere, buy the best rubber you can rather than the fanciest style.

    Do I need dedicated winter blades?

    If your winters bring real snow and ice, they help: winter blades wrap the frame in a rubber boot (or use a beam design) so slush can't pack the hinges and lift the edge off the glass. Many people in snow states run winter blades from the first freeze to spring, then switch back — which conveniently lands on the same twice-a-year rhythm as replacement. In mild climates, a good beam blade year-round covers you.

    What size do I buy — and does close-enough count?

    Look it up per side: the lookup book or kiosk at any parts store, the store's website, or your owner's manual lists exact lengths for driver, passenger, and rear. Sides often differ by several inches, and an oversized blade can hit the trim or the other wiper mid-sweep, while an undersized one leaves a band of windshield unwiped. Stick to the listed size; if you must substitute, never go longer.

    What about the rear wiper?

    Same rubber, same sun, same aging — it just gets forgotten because you rarely look through it. Rear blades are usually shorter and often use a different attachment (some pull straight off a pivot, some use small plastic adapters), so check the fitting before you buy. Replace it on the same schedule as the fronts, or at minimum once a year — a hatchback's rear window in winter road spray earns its keep.
