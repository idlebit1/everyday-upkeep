Bleed Your Radiators

There's a particular kind of cold-radiator mystery that drives people to crank the thermostat in autumn: the boiler's roaring, the pipes are warm, and yet one radiator sits stone-cold across its top half while the room never quite warms. The culprit is almost always trapped air. In a hot-water system, air bubbles rise to the top of each radiator and form a pocket that hot water can't push through — so the top stays cold, the room underperforms, and the boiler burns extra fuel for nothing. Bleeding lets that air out with a quarter-turn of a small key. It's the classic ten-minute job to do every autumn before the heating season kicks in, plus any time a radiator goes cold up top or starts gurgling.

🗓️ Typically once a year ⏱️ ~2–5 min per radiator 🔥 Warmer rooms, lower bills 💸 Costs ~$2–5 (a key)

Why it matters

A radiator is the most honest thing in your house: lay a hand on it and it tells you exactly what's wrong. Cold across the top while warm at the bottom means trapped air, and that single pocket of air does more harm than its size suggests — it dims the heat, drives up the bill, and quietly corrodes the system from the inside. Here's what trapped air costs you:

Cold tops, rooms that never warm

Air rises to the top of each radiator and forms a pocket the hot water can't push past, so the upper half sits cold and the radiator gives out a fraction of its heat. The room never gets cozy — and you end up cranking the thermostat to chase warmth that isn't coming.

A cold room you can't heat

The boiler burns more fuel

To force heat through an air-blocked radiator, the boiler and pump run harder and longer, fighting a system that can't release what it makes. Bleeding clears the path so heat flows freely — one of the cheapest things you can do to cut a winter heating bill.

A heating bill inflated all winter

Trapped air corrodes the system

That pocket isn't just dead space — the oxygen it carries reacts with the steel inside your radiators and pipes, breeding rust, black magnetic sludge, and eventually pinhole leaks. Letting the air out keeps oxygen from feeding the corrosion that wears a system out early.

Rust, sludge & pinhole leaks

Gurgling & banging are warnings

Bubbling, gurgling, tapping, or uneven warmth aren't quirks — they're the system telling you air is sloshing around inside. Listening to those signs and bleeding promptly means catching the problem while it's still a two-minute job, not a service call.

Noise that signals trouble brewing

How often should you bleed them?

Once a year — every autumn, before you fire up the heating for the season — is the sensible default for a hot-water system. But a radiator that's cold up top right now, gurgling pipes, a system you've recently drained or refilled, or a boiler whose pressure keeps dropping all push toward doing it more often (and investigating why air keeps getting in). Answer four quick questions:

1. What are the radiators doing right now?
A radiator that's cold across its top, or one you can hear bubbling and gurgling, has trapped air in it today — that's a bleed-now signal, not a wait-for-autumn one.
2. What kind of heating system is it?
This guide is for hot-water (hydronic) radiators — the ones with a little square bleed valve at a top corner. Steam radiators are a different animal and must not be bled the same way (see the safety note and FAQ).
3. How often does air seem to come back?
Air that returns quickly, or a sealed system whose pressure keeps falling, means air is getting drawn in somewhere — usually a small leak or a worn part — so bleed more often and have the cause looked at.
4. Any recent work on the system?
Anytime the system's been drained and refilled, or a new radiator added, fresh air comes in with the water — so plan to bleed once or twice in the weeks after, until the system settles.

    How to bleed your radiators

    The whole job is calm and simple if you do it in order: turn the heating off and let everything cool, find the bleed valve at a top corner, open it a quarter-turn until air hisses out, wait for a steady dribble of water, then close it snugly. Work through every radiator from the lowest floor up, then turn the heating back on and — on a sealed system — check and restore the boiler pressure. Read the safety box first, then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Cool it down, mind the steam, and check the pressure after

    • Turn the heating OFF and let the radiators go cold first. Bleeding a hot system sends out scalding water and pressurized hot air. Switch the heating off and wait — usually an hour or more — until the radiators are cool to the touch before you open any valve.
    • This is for HOT-WATER (hydronic) radiators only — never bleed a STEAM radiator this way. Steam systems use air vents and a completely different method; forcing a steam radiator's vent or fitting can scald you or break it. If you have steam radiators (typically one pipe and a little vent on the side), stop and read the FAQ — or call a pro.
    • Have a cloth and a small cup or container ready. A little water always follows the air, and it can be dirty. Hold a cloth under the valve and a cup beneath that to catch the dribble so it doesn't run down the radiator, the wall, or the floor.
    • Open the valve only a quarter to half turn — never remove it. Turn just until you hear air hiss; once water dribbles steadily, close it again. Fully unscrewing the valve can shoot it out under pressure and leave you fighting an open jet of water.
    • On a sealed or combi system, check the boiler pressure afterward. Letting air (and a little water) out drops the pressure. When you're done, top it up via the filling loop to the normal range — usually about 1–1.5 bar — following your boiler's instructions.
    • Call a heating engineer if water comes out brown or sludgy, the pressure won't hold, or air keeps coming back fast. Those point to corrosion sludge, a leak, or air being drawn in — problems bleeding alone won't fix, and worth a professional's eyes.

    🧰 What you'll need

    A radiator bleed key (or a flat screwdriver) An old cloth or rag A small cup or container Maybe rubber gloves (the water can be dirty) A towel for the floor Your boiler's pressure-gauge / filling-loop instructions

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Turn the heating off & let it cool

    Switch the heating off at the thermostat or boiler and wait until the radiators are cool to the touch — usually an hour or more. Bleeding a hot system sends out scalding water and hot air, so this is the step you never skip.

    Find the valve & fit the key

    The bleed valve is the small square nipple at a top corner of the radiator. Fit your bleed key onto it (or a flat screwdriver if it has a slot), and hold a cloth and a small cup right underneath to catch what comes out.

    Quarter-turn until air hisses

    Turn the key slowly anticlockwise — only a quarter to half turn. You'll hear the trapped air hiss out. That hiss is exactly what you're after, so don't open it any further; never fully unscrew the valve.

    Wait for water, then close it

    When the hissing stops and a steady dribble of water appears, all the air is out. Close the valve snugly — firm but not forced; overtightening can damage it. The dribble should stop with the valve shut.

    Work through the house, then check pressure

    Bleed every radiator, going lowest floor to highest so the air all clears upward. Then turn the heating back on, and on a sealed or combi system check the boiler pressure — top it up via the filling loop to about 1–1.5 bar if it's dropped.

    Signs your radiators are overdue — and when to call a pro

    A healthy hot-water system is quiet and warms each radiator evenly, top to bottom. These are the signals it's overdue for a bleed — and the handful of problems that bleeding won't fix and that are worth a heating engineer's eyes:

    • Cold at the top, warm at the bottom. The classic trapped-air signature: lay a hand on the radiator and feel the divide. That's the textbook case for a bleed, and it usually clears in a couple of minutes.
    • Gurgling, bubbling, or banging. Noises from the radiators or pipes when the heating's running mean air is sloshing around inside. Bleed promptly; if banging persists after bleeding, it can also be air still in the pipework or a balancing issue worth a pro's look.
    • Some radiators stone-cold while others are hot. Often air, sometimes balancing — bleed first, and if a radiator still won't warm, it may need balancing or have a stuck valve.
    • Still cold in the middle or across the bottom after bleeding. A cold strip low down or a cold center usually means sludge and cold spots, not air — that points to a flush or a balancing job, which is a heating engineer's call, not a bleed.
    • Boiler pressure that won't hold or keeps dropping. If you keep having to top up a sealed system, or the gauge falls again soon after, suspect a leak somewhere — radiator, valve, or pipe. Have it traced before it does damage.
    • Brown, rusty, or sludgy bleed water. A little discoloration can be normal, but consistently brown or gritty water signals internal corrosion — the system may need a chemical clean or power-flush. Worth a professional assessment.
    • A steam system, or you're not sure what you have. Steam radiators are bled and maintained completely differently and can scald or break if you treat them like a hot-water radiator. If you have (or suspect) steam, don't improvise — call a heating professional.
    • Pro tip: bleeding is the autumn heating-prep job, and it pairs naturally with two others — give the boiler a quick service, and tackle the rest of your seasonal list at the same time, like replacing the HVAC filter and flushing the water heater.

    Common questions

    How do I know my radiators need bleeding?

    The surest test is your hand. Run the heating until the radiators are hot, then feel each one from top to bottom: if a radiator is warm at the bottom but cold across the top, there's trapped air and it wants bleeding. Other tells are gurgling, bubbling, tapping, or banging noises when the heating runs; a room that won't warm up no matter how high you set the thermostat; or a boiler that's working hard while the radiators underperform. Cold at the top almost always means air; cold at the bottom is a different problem (see below). When in doubt, bleeding does no harm on a hot-water system — if no air comes out and water dribbles straight away, that radiator simply didn't need it.

    How often should I bleed them?

    For a typical hot-water system, once a year is the right rhythm — do it every autumn before you fire up the heating for the season, so you start winter with every radiator working at full output. Beyond that, bleed any time a radiator goes cold at the top, starts gurgling, or a room stops heating properly. You should also bleed once or twice in the weeks after any work that introduces air: a system drain and refill, a new radiator, or repairs. If you find yourself bleeding the same radiators often, or a sealed system's pressure keeps dropping, that's a sign air is being drawn in somewhere — worth investigating rather than just bleeding repeatedly.

    Hot-water vs steam radiators — does the method differ?

    Completely, and this matters for your safety. This guide is for hot-water (hydronic) radiators — the kind with a small square bleed valve at a top corner, fed and returned by two pipes, where you release trapped air with a bleed key. Steam radiators are different: they're usually fed by a single pipe and have an air vent on the side that opens and closes automatically as steam rises. You do not bleed a steam radiator with a key — forcing its vent or fittings can scald you with steam or damage the system. Steam radiators have their own maintenance (keeping the air vent clear and working, checking the pitch so condensate drains). If you have steam radiators, or you genuinely can't tell which type you have, don't improvise — look it up for your specific system or call a heating engineer.

    Why is my radiator cold at the top (vs cold at the bottom)?

    The location of the cold spot is a diagnosis. Cold at the top, warm below means trapped air has risen and pooled at the top, blocking the hot water — that's exactly what bleeding fixes. Cold at the bottom, warm at the top is the opposite story: it usually means sludge — that black, magnetic corrosion debris — has settled in the bottom of the radiator and is blocking flow there. Bleeding won't touch sludge; that's a job for flushing the radiator or a system power-flush. Cold in patches or across the middle also points to sludge or cold spots. And if a whole radiator stays cold while others are hot, suspect a closed or stuck valve, or a system that needs balancing. So: top-cold, bleed it; bottom- or middle-cold, it's likely sludge and a pro's job.

    Do I need to re-pressurize my boiler after?

    On a sealed or combi system — most modern setups, with a pressure gauge on the boiler — yes, usually. Letting air and a little water out drops the system pressure, and if it falls too low the boiler may stop working or lock out. After you've finished bleeding, check the gauge: if it's below the normal range, top it up using the filling loop (the little braided hose or valves near the boiler) until it reads in the usual band, which for most homes is about 1–1.5 bar cold. Follow your boiler's own instructions for exactly how to do this. On an older open-vented system with a header tank in the loft, you generally don't need to re-pressurize — the tank tops the system up automatically. If you can't get the pressure to hold, or it keeps dropping, that suggests a leak and is worth a professional's attention.

    Why does air keep coming back?

    If you bleed a radiator and it's full of air again within days or weeks, the air isn't just lingering — it's getting drawn in, and that's worth investigating. Common causes include a small leak somewhere in the system (so as water seeps out, air is pulled in to replace it — often shown by pressure that keeps dropping on a sealed system), a faulty automatic air vent or a failing expansion vessel, a pump set too fast that pulls air past seals, or corrosion inside producing hydrogen gas — which often comes with black, sludgy bleed water. The occasional bit of air after a refill is normal and settles in a week or two. But persistent, fast-returning air, especially with falling pressure or dirty water, is a symptom rather than the problem — have a heating engineer trace the cause rather than bleeding on repeat all winter.
