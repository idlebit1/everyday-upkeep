Test Your Garage Door Safety Sensors

Down near the floor on each side of your garage door track sit two little eyes — the photo-eye sensors — pointing an invisible beam at each other. They are the entire reason an automatic door reverses instead of crushing whatever's underneath it: a kid chasing a ball, a dog, a bumper, a foot. They've been required on every opener since 1993, and they quietly drift out of alignment, get bumped, gum up with dust, or simply fail — usually without telling you. A two-minute test confirms the door actually reverses: wave something through the beam, then close the door onto a flat 2x4. If it doesn't bounce back, that's not a chore, that's an emergency — stop using the opener until it's fixed.

🗓️ Test every 6 months ⏱️ ~2 min for both tests 🛡️ A genuine life-safety check 💸 Costs $0 to test

Why it matters

Most garage-door upkeep is about comfort and cost — a quieter door, a longer-lived opener. This one is different. The photo-eye sensors are the single safety device that stands between a heavy, motorized door and whatever ends up under it. Here's why a two-minute test belongs on your calendar:

It's the auto-reverse that saves lives

The photo-eye beam is what makes a closing door stop and reverse instead of crushing a child, a pet, or a car bumper. This isn't a convenience feature — it's the safety system, and a test is how you know it still works.

A door that crushes instead of reversing

Sensors drift and fail silently

A bumped bracket, a cobweb over a lens, a knocked-loose wire, or simple age can leave a sensor misaligned or dead. A misaligned eye leaves the door unsafe — or refusing to close at all — and nothing announces it until you check.

An unsafe door you don't know about

The door is heavy enough to kill

A residential door weighs well over a hundred pounds and travels with real force. The reversing system is the only thing standing between that weight and whatever's beneath it — which is exactly why it's worth confirming it works.

Serious injury under a heavy door

A quick test catches it in time

Two minutes — wave something through the beam, close the door on a board — and you know for certain. The whole point is to find a failed sensor on purpose, on a calm afternoon, instead of the hard way at the worst possible moment.

Finding out the hard way

How often should you test them?

Every six months is the sensible default — and honestly, monthly is the ideal, which is what many opener manufacturers actually recommend. A garage with young kids or pets, a door that cycles all day, or an opener that's been acting up all push toward testing every month. And one answer trumps everything: a pre-1993 opener with no photo-eyes at all needs replacing, not testing. Four quick questions:

1. Who's around the garage?
The auto-reverse exists above all for the small and fast — a toddler darting under a closing door, a dog slipping out. If kids or pets are ever near the garage, you want this confirmed working far more often.
2. How much does the door get used?
Every cycle is a chance for a sensor to get bumped, for a bracket to shift, or for the beam to inch out of alignment. A heavily used door deserves more frequent checks because there's more that can knock it off.
3. How old is the opener?
Photo-eyes have been federally required since 1993. If your opener predates them and has no sensors near the floor, no test will make it safe — it needs to be replaced with a modern opener.
4. How's the door behaving lately?
A door that reverses with nothing in the way, refuses to close, or shows blinking sensor lights is telling you the eyes are dirty or misaligned right now — test and realign today, then keep a close eye on it.

    How to test your garage door safety sensors

    Two tests, both of which the door must pass: the beam test (break the invisible beam and watch the door reverse) and the contact-reversal test (close the door onto a flat 2x4 and watch it bounce back). Read the safety box first — it tells you exactly what to do if the door fails, because that's an emergency, not a to-do item. Then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Read this first — what a failed test actually means

    • If the door does NOT reverse in either test, stop using the automatic opener immediately. Pull the manual-release cord to disconnect the opener and operate the door by hand, and don't let kids or pets near it until it's repaired. A door that won't reverse is genuinely dangerous.
    • NEVER disable, bypass, or tape over the sensors to force a balky door to close. The internet is full of "tricks" to make a door close past misaligned eyes — every one of them removes the life-safety system. If the eyes won't let the door close, fix the eyes; don't defeat them.
    • Do BOTH tests. The beam test confirms the door reverses when something breaks the beam; the contact-reversal (2x4) test confirms it reverses when it physically touches an obstacle and checks the opener's force setting. They cover different failures — a door can pass one and fail the other.
    • Keep the beam path and the area under the door clear when it's closing, and never walk, run, or drive under a moving door. The sensors are a backup, not a license — treat the door as if it won't stop, every time.
    • Openers made before 1993 have no photo-eyes at all. Federal law has required them since then. If yours has no sensors near the floor, no test makes it safe — it should be replaced with a modern opener.
    • If you're not sure it's working right, call a garage-door technician. Sensor alignment and force adjustments can be fiddly, and this is the one device where "close enough" isn't good enough. A pro visit is cheap peace of mind.

    🧰 What you'll need

    A roll of paper towel, a cardboard box, or a broom A flat 2x4 (or a similar sturdy board) A soft, dry cloth for the lenses A flashlight or headlamp Nothing else — no tools to test

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Find the two sensors

    The photo-eyes are mounted low — within about six inches of the floor — on each track. Confirm both indicator LEDs are lit and steady. A blinking light almost always means the two eyes aren't aimed at each other.

    Clean the lenses & check alignment

    Wipe dust and cobwebs off both lenses and make sure nothing — a bin, a bike, a leaf pile — blocks them. If a light is blinking, gently nudge the sensor in its bracket until both lights glow solid.

    The BEAM test

    Start the door closing, then wave a broom, a box, or your foot through the beam — never your hand near the door's edge. The instant the beam breaks, the door should stop and reverse all the way back up.

    The CONTACT-reversal test

    Lay a 2x4 flat on the floor in the door's path and close the door. When the bottom edge touches the wood, it should reverse — this checks the opener's force setting, the second layer of protection beyond the beam.

    If either test fails

    Disconnect the opener with the manual-release cord, keep everyone clear, and realign or replace the sensors — or call a technician. Don't return the door to automatic use until it passes. Then log the date you tested.

    Signs something's wrong — and when to call a pro

    A healthy setup is quiet about itself: two solid lights, a door that closes smoothly and reverses the instant anything's in the way. These are the signals that the sensors — or the opener — need attention now, and the cases that belong to a garage-door technician:

    • The door won't close, or reverses randomly. The most common symptom of dirty or misaligned photo-eyes. Clean the lenses, clear anything in the beam path, and nudge the sensors back into alignment until both lights are solid — that fixes most cases.
    • A sensor light blinks and won't go solid. ⚠️ A blinking LED means the eyes aren't seeing each other. If gentle realignment and a lens wipe don't get a steady light, the bracket, wiring, or sensor may be the problem — and a technician can sort it quickly.
    • The door doesn't reverse on the beam test or the board. ⚠️ This is the serious one. Stop using the automatic opener, operate the door by hand with the release cord, and get it repaired before anyone uses it again. A door that won't reverse is an emergency.
    • A sensor is cracked, damaged, or hanging loose. Physical damage means a replacement. A photo-eye pair is inexpensive and many people swap it themselves, but if the wiring or alignment is beyond you, a pro handles it fast.
    • Any opener made before 1993. ⚠️ If there are no photo-eyes near the floor, the door has no auto-reverse beam at all. No test makes it safe — it needs a modern opener with sensors, which is a worthwhile upgrade for the safety alone.
    • The force setting seems off. If the door slams shut without reversing on the 2x4, or won't close at all, the opener's down-force may need adjusting — a setting best left to a technician on most units rather than guessed at.
    • Pro tip: fold this into your home-safety round so it never gets skipped — it pairs naturally with the same five-minute pass you make for the rest of the house's safety devices.

    Common questions

    How do I test my garage door safety sensors?

    Two short tests, and the door should pass both. First, the beam test: with the door open, press the wall button or remote to start it closing, then wave a broom, a cardboard box, or your foot through the invisible beam between the two low sensors — keeping your hand well away from the door's edge. The door should stop and reverse all the way back up the instant the beam is broken. Second, the contact-reversal test: lay a flat 2x4 on the floor in the door's path, directly under the door, and close it; when the bottom edge touches the wood, the door should reverse. The beam test checks the photo-eyes; the contact test checks the opener's force setting. Both are quick, both are free, and if the door fails either one, stop using the automatic opener until it's fixed. This pairs nicely with lubricating the garage door, since you're already down at the tracks.

    How often should I test them?

    Every six months is a sensible default, and it slots neatly into a spring-and-fall maintenance rhythm. That said, monthly is the ideal — and it's what many opener manufacturers actually recommend, because the sensors are the device most worth confirming and the test is so quick. Tighten to monthly if you have young kids or pets around the garage, if the door is your main entrance and cycles many times a day, or if it's recently been misbehaving. Whatever the interval, the test never expires from your memory the way it should from your calendar: if the door ever reverses for no reason, refuses to close, or shows a blinking sensor light, test it that day regardless of when you last did.     Why does my garage door reverse before it closes, or won't close at all?

    Nine times out of ten it's the photo-eye sensors, and the fix is simple. The two eyes have to "see" each other across the beam; if one is dirty, knocked out of alignment, or blocked, the opener assumes something is in the way and refuses to let the door close — that's the safety system doing exactly its job. Start by wiping both lenses clean with a soft, dry cloth — dust, cobwebs, and even bright low sun shining into a lens can break the connection. Then clear anything in the beam path (a trash bin, a bike, leaves) and check that both indicator lights are glowing solid. If one is blinking, gently nudge that sensor in its bracket until the light goes steady. Loose wiring or a failed sensor can also be the culprit; if cleaning and realigning don't bring the lights solid, the sensor or its wiring likely needs attention. Whatever you do, don't tape over or bypass the eyes to force the door shut — that removes the protection entirely.

    The sensor light is blinking — what does that mean?

    A blinking light is the sensor's way of saying "I can't see my partner." The two photo-eyes have to be aimed precisely at each other; a blink usually means one has been bumped out of alignment, has a dirty or obstructed lens, or has a loose connection. Start easy: wipe both lenses with a soft dry cloth and make sure nothing is sitting in the beam path. If it's still blinking, look at the bracket — gently bend or nudge the sensor a hair at a time until the light glows solid and steady, which is your signal the eyes are locked on each other again. If you can't get a steady light no matter how you adjust it, suspect the wiring (check for a chewed or pinched wire) or a failed sensor, both of which a garage-door technician can resolve quickly. Until both lights are solid, the door may not close — and shouldn't be forced to.

    My door doesn't reverse — what do I do?

    Treat this as an emergency, not a weekend project. A door that won't reverse on the beam test or the 2x4 has lost its safety system, and it can seriously injure or kill a child, pet, or anyone underneath it. Right now: stop using the automatic opener. Pull the manual-release cord (the rope with the red handle hanging from the opener rail) to disconnect the door from the motor, and operate it by hand until it's fixed. Keep kids and pets well away from it. Then diagnose: clean and realign the photo-eyes and re-run the beam test; if it now reverses, great. If the door reverses on the beam but not on the 2x4, the opener's down-force setting likely needs adjusting — often a technician's call. And never, ever disable or tape over the sensors to make the door close; that doesn't fix anything, it just removes the protection. If you can't get both tests to pass, call a garage-door technician before putting the door back on automatic.

    My opener has no photo-eyes — is that legal or safe?

    If your opener has no sensors near the floor, it predates 1993 — and it should be replaced. Federal law (UL 325) has required photo-eye sensors and an auto-reverse on every residential garage door opener manufactured since 1993, precisely because doors without them were injuring and killing children. An older opener with only a contact-reversal feature (and no beam) offers far less protection: it only reverses after the door has already pressed down on something. A modern opener with photo-eyes is inexpensive, dramatically safer, quieter, and adds conveniences like battery backup and smartphone control. If you have an old unit with no beam sensors, fold this into your home-safety round alongside testing your smoke detectors and GFCI outlets, and put "replace the garage opener" near the top of the list. It's one of the highest-value safety upgrades a home can get.
