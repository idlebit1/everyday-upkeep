Inspect Your Roof

Your roof is the single most expensive thing standing between you and the weather, and it fails the way most expensive things do — quietly, from a problem you could have seen a year earlier. A lifted shingle here, a cracked bead of flashing sealant there, a patch where the granules have worn off and the shingle's gone bald. Left alone, that pinhole becomes a drip, the drip rots the decking, and one rainy season later you're looking at soaked insulation, a stained ceiling, mold in the framing, and a five-figure repair. Once a year — and after every major storm — you walk the yard with a pair of binoculars, glass each roof plane, peek in the attic, and catch the small things while they're still small. You never have to set foot on the roof to do it.

🗓️ Typically once a year 🌩️ Plus after every major storm 🔭 Costs ~$0 from the ground 💸 Catches $300 vs $15,000 problems

Why it matters

Roofs are out of sight, so they're out of mind — right up until water is dripping into a bedroom. But almost every catastrophic, budget-wrecking roof failure starts as something small and visible that sat unnoticed for a season or two. A yearly look (and a fast one after big storms) is the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy on the most expensive surface of your house. Here's what's at stake:

A small leak turns catastrophic

Water that gets past the roof doesn't stop — it rots the wood decking and framing, ruins ceilings and insulation, and grows mold in the dark. Catching it early is the difference between a quick patch and tearing out a corner of your house.

A $300 fix vs a $15,000 one

Shingles age out

Granule loss, curling, cracking, and bald spots are how an asphalt roof tells you it's near the end of its life. Spot the wear before a windy night turns a tired shingle into an open hole — and time a planned replacement instead of an emergency one.

A roof that fails on its own schedule

Flashing leaks first

The field of shingles usually outlasts the metal and sealant around chimneys, vents, skylights, and valleys. Those penetrations are where water sneaks in long before a shingle fails — so they're the first place a good inspection looks.

Leaks where you'd never think to look

Insurance has a clock

An annual and post-storm look documents wind and hail damage while you can still file a claim — those windows are often surprisingly short. Photos with dates turn "the roof just got old" into a covered storm loss.

A missed claim window costs thousands

How often should you inspect?

Once a year is the baseline for any roof — plus a quick look after every major storm, always. But an aging asphalt roof, a punishing climate, heavy tree cover, or a recent hail or wind event all push you toward twice a year and an immediate post-storm check. Answer four quick questions:

1. How old is your roof — and what is it?
Asphalt shingles last roughly 20–25 years, so a roof past 15 is in the zone where small problems start cascading. Metal and tile last far longer and fail more slowly — but they still need eyes on the flashing and fasteners.
2. What's the weather like where you live?
Hail bruises shingles, wind lifts and tears them, ice dams force water backward under them, and relentless UV bakes them brittle. The harsher your climate, the more often it's worth a look.
3. How much tree cover is over the roof?
Overhanging limbs drop leaves and debris that clog valleys and gutters, scrape shingles in the wind, and shade the roof so moss and algae take hold and lift the shingle edges.
4. Any major storms since your last look?
A major storm is its own trigger, regardless of the calendar. The point of a post-storm check is to catch (and photograph) fresh wind or hail damage while it's still within the insurance filing window.

    How to inspect your roof — safely

    The whole method is built around one rule: do not walk on the roof. You can spot the overwhelming majority of problems from the ground with binoculars, from a ladder at the very edge, and from inside the attic — without ever stepping onto the surface. Read the safety box first (it's the most important part of this guide), then the techniques, then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ The one rule: stay off the roof

    • Do not walk on the roof — falls are the real danger here, not the leak. A wet, steep, mossy, brittle, or sun-baked roof is genuinely deadly, and a fall from roof height is life-changing or fatal. Nothing you'd find up there is worth it. Inspect from the ground, the eave, and the attic instead.
    • Glass it from the ground first. A pair of binoculars from the yard shows you missing, curling, and dark patches across every roof plane — most inspections are done before you ever touch a ladder.
    • From a ladder, look ALONG the surface — never step onto it. A stable ladder at the eave lets you see the first courses, the drip edge, and the gutters up close. Stay on the ladder; do not climb off onto the roof.
    • For a steep or tall roof, use a drone or hire a pro. If you can't see the field of the roof from the ground or the eave, that's exactly what a drone or a licensed roofer is for. Don't improvise your way onto a high, steep surface.
    • Follow ladder safety to the letter. Firm, level ground; the correct 4-to-1 angle; three points of contact at all times; and a spotter to foot the ladder and steady it. Never lean or overreach — move the ladder instead.
    • Watch for power lines at the service entrance, and never inspect during or right after a storm. Keep yourself and the ladder well clear of the lines where power enters the house, and wait until surfaces are fully dry — a wet roof and a wet ladder are how a routine check goes wrong.

    Binoculars A phone or camera (for dated photos) A stable extension or step ladder A spotter to foot the ladder A flashlight for the attic Work gloves A notepad or notes app A drone (optional, for steep roofs)

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    1. The ground scan

    Walk the whole perimeter and glass each roof plane with binoculars, quadrant by quadrant. You're hunting for missing shingles, curling or cupped edges, and dark or patchy areas. This catches most of what's wrong — and you do it with both feet on the grass.

    2. The eave & gutter close-up

    From a stable ladder at the edge — looking along the surface, never climbing on — check the first courses and the drip edge. Then look in the gutters: a buildup of sand-like shingle granules is a clear sign the shingles are wearing out.

    3. Flashing, valleys & penetrations

    Zero in on every spot where something pokes through the roof — chimneys, vent boots, skylights — plus the valleys where two planes meet. Look for lifted, rusted, or bent metal flashing and cracked, missing sealant. This is where leaks almost always begin.

    4. The attic, from inside

    The safest view of your roof is from underneath it. With a flashlight, check the deck for water stains, pinpoints of daylight, damp or matted insulation, sagging or soft spots, and the musty smell of mold. Problems show here long before the ceiling does.

    5. The post-storm check

    After any major hail or wind event, do a fresh ground-and-attic pass and photograph anything that looks new — with the date. Insurance claim windows are often short, and dated photos are what turn storm damage into a covered claim instead of an argument.

    Signs your roof needs attention — and when to call a pro

    A healthy roof is one you never think about: shingles flat and intact, flashing tight, attic dry. These are the signals that something's wrong — and the line where the job stops being a look and becomes a roofer's call. The rule throughout: if seeing or fixing it means getting onto the roof, that's a pro's job, not yours.

    • Granules in the gutters and bald shingle spots. A buildup of sand-like granules in the gutters, and patches where the shingle's underlying mat is showing through, both mean the shingles are wearing out. Do it the same day you're cleaning the gutters — clogged gutters and granule loss go hand in hand.
    • Curling, cupping, cracked, or missing shingles. Edges lifting up, centers domed, cracks, or outright gaps all mean the field is failing and water has a way in. A few missing shingles may be a repair; widespread curling usually means the roof is near end of life.
    • Daylight or stains in the attic. Any pinpoint of light through the deck, any water stain on the underside, damp or matted insulation, or a musty smell means water is already getting in. Don't wait for the ceiling to confirm it.
    • A sagging or dipping roofline. A roof plane or ridge that's no longer straight can mean rotted decking or a structural problem underneath. That's never a DIY fix — call a roofer promptly.
    • Any active leak, stain, or drip indoors. A spreading ceiling stain or an actual drip means the water has already arrived. Get it diagnosed before the next rain makes it worse.
    • Anything that requires getting onto the roof. If you can't assess or address it from the ground, the eave, or the attic, stop. A licensed, insured roofer has the harnesses, training, and ladders to do safely what you shouldn't.
    • Pro tip: tie this to the seasons. Pair the yearly inspection with fall leaf cleanup and gutter cleaning, and make sure you're reviewing your insurance so you know your roof and storm coverage before you need to file a claim.

    Common questions

    Can I inspect my own roof — and should I walk on it?

    You can absolutely do a thorough inspection yourself, and no, you should not walk on the roof to do it. The overwhelming majority of problems — missing, curling, and cracked shingles, granule loss, damaged flashing, sagging, blocked valleys — are visible from the ground with a pair of binoculars, from a stable ladder at the eave looking along the surface, and from inside the attic. Walking the roof is where the real danger is: a wet, steep, mossy, brittle, or sun-baked surface is genuinely deadly, and a fall from that height is life-altering. Nothing you'd see up close is worth that risk. If you truly can't see the field of a steep or tall roof from the ground or the eave, that's exactly what a drone or a licensed roofer is for. Glass it from the yard, check the eaves from a properly footed ladder, and inspect from underneath in the attic — and stay off the surface.

    What are the signs I need a new roof?

    A few things, especially in combination, signal a roof that's near or at the end of its life rather than one needing a spot repair. Widespread granule loss — bare patches and granules piling up in the gutters — means the shingles have lost the protective layer that shields them from UV. Curling, cupping, and cracking across many shingles (not just one or two) is age, not damage. Multiple leaks or recurring leaks in different spots suggest the whole system is failing, not one weak point. A sagging roofline can mean rotted decking underneath. And age itself matters: most asphalt roofs last about 20–25 years, so if yours is in that range and showing these signs, it's time to budget for replacement. The best move is a professional inspection — a roofer can tell you honestly whether you're looking at a $600 repair or a planned replacement, and timing a replacement beats waiting for an emergency one.

    What does it mean if I find granules in my gutters?

    Asphalt shingles are coated with a layer of small mineral granules — the gritty, sand-like surface — that protect the asphalt underneath from the sun's UV and add fire resistance and color. As shingles age, those granules loosen and wash down into the gutters. A little granule loss is normal, especially on a brand-new roof shedding loose grit. But a steady accumulation of granules — enough that the bottom of the gutter looks like coarse sand — means the shingles are genuinely wearing out and approaching the end of their service life. Pair that with bald patches you can see from the ground and curling edges, and it's a clear signal to start planning. It's also a great reason to check the gutters and the roof on the same day: you're already up there with the ladder, and clogged gutters and granule loss are linked.

    How often should I inspect — and what about after storms?

    Once a year is the baseline for any roof — a relaxed, twenty-minute look from the ground, the eave, and the attic. Step up to twice a year if your roof is older asphalt (past 15 years), if you're in a harsh climate with hail, high wind, heavy snow and ice dams, or intense sun, or if heavy tree cover is dropping debris and shading the roof. On top of the calendar, always do a check after every major storm — hail, damaging wind, or a heavy ice event. Storm damage doesn't wait for your annual review, and the whole point of a post-storm look is to catch and document fresh damage while you can still file an insurance claim. Wait until everything is fully dry before you inspect, never during or right after the storm while surfaces are slick.

    Are drone roof inspections worth it?

    For a steep, tall, or complex roof, yes — a drone is often the safest and clearest way to see the field of the roof without anyone getting near it. A camera drone can fly close over the surface and capture high-resolution images and video of every plane, the ridge, the valleys, and the flashing, giving you a detailed look you'd otherwise risk a climb to get. Many homeowners with a capable drone do their own aerial scan from the ground; many roofing companies now offer drone inspections as a standard service. The trade-off is that a drone shows you the surface but can't feel for soft spots, lift a shingle to check what's underneath, or test how brittle things have gotten — so it complements a hands-on professional inspection rather than fully replacing one. As a way to inspect safely without walking the roof, though, it's an excellent tool, particularly for roofs that are dangerous to access.

    How does storm and hail damage work with insurance?

    Most homeowner's policies cover sudden, accidental roof damage from events like wind and hail, but not the gradual wear of an aging roof — which is exactly why timing and documentation matter. After a major storm, do your inspection promptly and photograph everything that looks new, with the date captured. Insurance claim filing windows are often surprisingly short — sometimes a year or even less from the date of the event, depending on your policy and state — so a leisurely "I'll deal with it eventually" can quietly cost you the whole claim. Dated photos taken right after the storm are what distinguish a covered storm loss from "the roof just got old." Review your policy so you know your deductible, your coverage type (replacement cost versus actual cash value can mean thousands of dollars of difference), and the filing window before you need it — it's worth reviewing your insurance while the sun's out, not in the middle of a leak. For anything beyond a quick documentation pass, a licensed roofer can assess storm damage and many will help with the claim.
