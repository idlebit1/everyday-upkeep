Regrip Your Golf Clubs

It creeps up shot by shot: the tacky rubber that used to stick to your palms hardens, glazes, and wears smooth until the club feels like a polished stick in your hands. So you squeeze harder to hold on — and grip tension is one of the quietest swing-killers there is, robbing clubhead speed and control and adding mishits you can't explain. Then you slide a fresh, tacky grip onto that same shaft, hold the club lightly again, and the whole swing loosens up. Regripping is a genuinely satisfying afternoon: peel the old grip, strip the tape, wrap new double-sided tape, hit it with solvent, and slide the new grip on straight. A few dollars a club, or a cheap shop job — and rule of thumb, it's due about every 40 rounds or every year or two.

Why it matters

Your grip is the only place you actually touch the club, so it does far more work than it gets credit for. When the rubber goes hard and slick, everything downstream in the swing quietly gets worse — and because it happens so slowly, most golfers blame their swing long before they blame their grips. Here's what worn grips are really costing you:

Slick grips make you squeeze

A hard, shiny grip you can't quite hold onto forces you to clamp down — and grip tension is a swing-killer. Squeeze the handle and your forearms tense, your wrists stop releasing, and you lose clubhead speed and control while adding the odd inexplicable mishit.

Lost speed, control & mishits

Grips wear out on a schedule

Tacky rubber isn't forever — it hardens, glazes, and wears smooth over rounds and seasons, and heat and humidity speed it right up. Grips that "still work" are often well past their best; you've just adapted to them one round at a time without noticing.

Hardened, glazed, worn smooth

Fresh grips transform feel

New, tacky grips let you hold the club lightly and confidently — the club sits in your fingers instead of being crushed in your palms. That's most obvious in the rain or with sweaty hands, where a worn grip turns and a fresh one just holds.

Hold it light & confident

It's cheap and DIY-able

A grip is only a few dollars, the tape and solvent cost almost nothing, and the whole job is an hour on the bench with a hook blade. Set that against the effect worn grips have on every single swing and it's the best-value "upgrade" in the bag.

A few dollars beats every swing

How often should you regrip?

Every 40 rounds or so — roughly once a year or two for a regular player — is the sensible default. But if you play most weekends, live somewhere hot and humid, or leave your clubs baking in a hot trunk, that clock runs faster; an occasional player with mild weather can stretch it. And no matter the schedule, slick, hard, shiny grips mean it's due now. Answer four quick questions:

1. How much do you play?
Every round and range session rubs the tack off the rubber a little more. The more you play, the sooner your grips glaze over — frequent players regrip on a much shorter clock than weekend-a-month golfers.
2. What's your climate — and do your hands sweat?
Heat, humidity, sunscreen, and sweat all break down grip rubber and leave it slick faster. Hot, sticky climates chew through grips noticeably quicker than cool, dry ones.
3. How do the grips feel right now?
This is the tiebreaker. If the rubber is glazed and hard, or you catch yourself gripping tighter to hold on, the schedule is beside the point — they're done, regrip now.
4. Where do the clubs live between rounds?
A trunk in summer bakes at oven temperatures, and that heat hardens and glazes grip rubber even when the clubs aren't being played. Cool, dry storage lets your grips last as long as they should.

    How to regrip your golf clubs

    The rhythm is simple: clamp the shaft, cut the old grip off, strip the tape, wrap fresh double-sided tape, wet it with solvent, and slide the new grip on straight — fast, before the adhesive grabs. Read the safety notes first (the blade and the solvent are the two places to be careful), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Work smart — blade, solvent, and a clamped shaft

    • Work in a well-ventilated area, away from flames. Grip solvent — and mineral spirits, a common substitute — is flammable and gives off fumes. Open a window or work in the garage with the door up, and keep it well away from any open flame, pilot light, or spark.
    • Use a hook blade and cut away from yourself. A straight utility blade can slip and cut you, or nick a graphite shaft and weaken it. A hook blade rides under the grip and is far safer — especially on graphite. Either way, cut slowly and always pull the blade away from your body.
    • Clamp the shaft in a rubber shaft clamp in a vise. The rubber clamp spreads the pressure so you don't crush or crack the shaft. Snug it firmly but never overtighten — a graphite shaft especially can be cracked by a bare or over-cranked vise.
    • Work fast once the solvent's on. Solvent activates the tape's adhesive and buys you only about 30–60 seconds to slide the grip fully on and align it before it sets. Have the grip ready, soak the tape and the inside of the grip, and go without hesitating.
    • Seat it fully and align it straight. Push the grip all the way down until it's snug against the butt end of the shaft, then square up any logo or alignment marking while it still slides. Check it from the address position before it grabs.
    • Let it cure before you play. Give the adhesive a few hours — ideally overnight — to set so the grip doesn't twist on the shaft. Wipe off excess solvent, and dispose of solvent-soaked tape and rags safely, since they're flammable. Not comfortable with the blade and solvent? A golf shop does the whole thing cheaply.

    🧰 What you'll need

    New grips (one per club) Double-sided grip tape Grip solvent (or mineral spirits) A hook blade or grip cutter A vise + rubber shaft clamp A shallow tray to catch solvent Rags or paper towels A well-ventilated space

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Know when it's time

    Run your hands down each grip. If the rubber feels slick, looks shiny, has gone hard, or shows cracks — or you notice yourself gripping tighter to hold on — it's done. Trust your hands here; grips wear so slowly you adapt without realizing.

    Remove the old grip

    Clamp the shaft in the vise with a rubber shaft clamp, then run a hook blade up under the old grip, cutting away from your body. Peel the grip off in one piece. The hook blade rides safely under the rubber without nicking the shaft.

    Strip & re-tape

    Peel and scrub off every bit of the old tape and adhesive until the shaft is clean, then wrap one fresh strip of double-sided grip tape down the butt end. Tape residue left behind makes the new grip lumpy — take the extra minute to get it bare.

    Solvent & slide

    Soak the taped shaft and the inside of the new grip with solvent, then quickly slide the grip on all the way until it seats against the butt end. You've got only about 30–60 seconds before the adhesive grabs, so don't stop halfway — push it home.

    Set & cure

    While the grip still slides, square up the alignment so any logo sits straight at address. Wipe off the excess solvent, then let it cure a few hours — ideally overnight — before you play. Finish by logging the regrip so you know when the clock resets.

    Signs your grips are done — and when to hand it to a shop

    A good grip is invisible: you hold the club lightly and never think about it. These are the signals it's overdue — and the times regripping is worth handing to a golf shop instead of doing it on your own bench:

    • The rubber feels slick or looks shiny. If the surface has glazed over and slides in a light grip, the tack is gone — this is the clearest single sign it's time to regrip, especially on the clubs you swing most.
    • The grip has gone hard, or it's cracked. Rubber that's stiffened up or split — often first near the lower hand or along the seams — has aged out. Hard, cracked grips don't hold and can pinch or blister your hands.
    • You catch yourself squeezing to hold on. If your hands are working harder than they should, or the club turns in wet or sweaty conditions, your grips are telling you they're worn even before they look it.
    • The most-used clubs feel worst. Your driver, wedges, and go-to irons wear first. If those feel slick while the rest of the bag feels fine, do the whole set anyway — matched grips keep the feel consistent club to club.
    • Prefer not to handle the blade and solvent? If cutting off grips and working with flammable solvent isn't your thing, a golf shop regrips for just a few dollars a club in labor. It's a fast, cheap, no-fumes way to get it done right.
    • Pricey graphite shafts. Graphite is easy to nick or crack with a slipped blade or an over-tightened vise. If you've got expensive graphite shafts and you're not confident, let a shop with a proper cutter and vise setup handle them.
    • Pro tip: a full-set regrip is a lot of repetitive tape-and-solvent work against the clock. If you'd rather not spend the afternoon, drop the bag at a shop — and while you're thinking about gear, the racquet-sport equivalent is restringing a tennis racquet, and keep the rest of your sports gear current like your running shoes.

    Common questions

    How often should I regrip my golf clubs?

    The rule of thumb is about every 40 rounds, or once every one to two years for a regular player — whichever comes first. That said, the schedule is only a guide; the real test is how the grips feel. If you play a lot, live somewhere hot and humid, or store your clubs in a hot car, they'll wear faster and you might regrip yearly or even sooner. An occasional player in a mild climate can comfortably stretch to two years. The clubs you swing most — driver, wedges, favorite irons — wear first, so check those, and when a couple are clearly gone it's usually worth doing the whole set at once so the feel stays consistent from club to club.

    How do I know my grips are worn out?

    Feel them. Fresh grip rubber is soft and tacky — it grabs your palms and fingers. Worn grips go slick and slippery, look shiny or glazed where your hands sit, and feel hard rather than cushioned; older ones may crack or split, often near the lower hand or along a seam. The subtler tell is your own hands: if you catch yourself squeezing tighter to keep control, or the club turns in the rain or when your hands are sweaty, the tack is gone even if the grips don't look dramatic yet. Because they wear so gradually, most golfers adapt without noticing — so if you can't remember the last time you regripped, they're probably overdue.

    Can I regrip clubs myself?

    Yes — it's one of the most satisfying and beginner-friendly club repairs there is. The process is: clamp the shaft in a vise with a rubber shaft clamp, cut the old grip off with a hook blade (cutting away from yourself), strip off the old tape and adhesive, wrap fresh double-sided grip tape, soak the tape and the inside of the new grip with solvent, and quickly slide the new grip on and align it straight before the adhesive sets. The whole thing takes about ten to fifteen minutes per club once you find your rhythm. The two things to respect are the blade (a hook blade is much safer, especially on graphite) and the solvent (flammable, so work with ventilation and away from flames). If either makes you uneasy, a shop does it cheaply.

    What do I need to regrip a club?

    Not much, and most of it is cheap. You'll want new grips (one per club), a roll of double-sided grip tape, grip solvent, a hook blade or grip-cutting tool, and a vise fitted with a rubber shaft clamp so you can hold the shaft without crushing it. A shallow tray under the shaft catches the solvent that runs off, and a rag handles the excess. That's genuinely it. If you don't own a vise, many players get by clamping the shaft carefully or buying an inexpensive rubber clamp, but a vise makes the job far easier and safer — and always use the rubber clamp, never bare metal jaws, especially on graphite shafts.

    Does grip solvent have to be special?

    Not necessarily — purpose-made grip solvent is convenient and evaporates cleanly, but plenty of people use mineral spirits, which is a common and inexpensive substitute that works much the same way. Its job is to activate the adhesive on the double-sided tape so the grip slides on, then flash off so the tape grabs and holds. Whatever you use, treat it as flammable: work in a well-ventilated space, keep it away from open flames, pilot lights, and sparks, and dispose of solvent-soaked tape and rags safely rather than balling them up in a bin. Avoid random household solvents you're unsure about — grip solvent or mineral spirits are the standard, predictable choices.

    How long before I can play after regripping?

    Give the adhesive time to cure. A few hours is usually enough for the tape to set and the grip to lock onto the shaft, but overnight is the safe bet, especially if it's cool or humid. Playing too soon risks the grip twisting on the shaft under the force of a swing, which undoes all your careful alignment. Right after you slide each grip on, square up the alignment and wipe off the excess solvent, then set the club aside and let it rest. If you regripped a full set the evening before a round, they'll typically be ready by morning — but if you're unsure, an extra few hours never hurts.
