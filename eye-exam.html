Keep Up Your Eye Exams

It's easy to think of an eye exam as a glasses check — if you can still read the road signs, why go? But a comprehensive exam is really a health check for your eyes, and it's the one place a doctor looks straight at your blood vessels and optic nerve without a single incision. Some of the most serious eye diseases — glaucoma, macular degeneration, diabetic damage — do their harm quietly, with no pain and no blur, until sight you can't get back is already gone. The upkeep job isn't complicated: don't let the appointment lapse, keep it on the calendar, know how to prepare (dilation means a ride and sunglasses), and update your prescription when it drifts. How often you need one is your eye doctor's call — this guide just helps you stay on top of it.

Why it matters

The trap with eyes is that "I can see fine" and "my eyes are healthy" are not the same thing. Vision is what you notice; eye health is what an exam checks — and the gap between them is exactly where the serious, sight-stealing conditions hide. Here's what a regular, comprehensive exam is really doing for you:

It catches silent eye disease early

Glaucoma and several other conditions cause no early symptoms — no pain, no blur — while they quietly damage your sight, and what's lost can't be restored. A comprehensive exam can spot the signs long before you'd ever notice, when treatment can still protect the vision you have.

Irreversible vision loss, missed

It's a window into whole-body health

The eye is the one spot a doctor can see your blood vessels and nerves directly, without cutting anything. That means an exam can turn up the first signs of diabetes, high blood pressure, and other conditions — sometimes before your regular doctor knows they're there.

Whole-body clues, unnoticed

Your prescription drifts

Vision changes gradually, so you adapt without realizing it — squinting, holding your phone farther away, driving a little less at night. An out-of-date prescription brings headaches, eye strain, and unsafe driving. An exam brings the world back into focus and your glasses back up to date.

Headaches, strain & unsafe driving

Kids especially need it

A child can't tell you that everyone else sees the board more clearly — to them, blurry is normal. Undetected vision problems hurt reading and learning and are easy to miss, and some (like a lazy eye) are best treated young. A proper exam catches what a quick school screening can't.

Learning problems, mistaken

How often should you get one?

There's no single answer — it depends on your age, whether you wear glasses or contacts, your health, and your family history. As a very general starting point, healthy adults often go every one to two years, kids and older adults more often, and anyone with diabetes or an eye condition on whatever schedule their doctor sets. Answer four quick questions for a rough sense of where you land — but treat it as a conversation-starter, not a prescription. Your eye doctor sets your real schedule.

1. What life stage are you (or the person) in?
Kids' eyes are still developing and problems affect learning, while risk of age-related conditions climbs after 60 — both groups are usually seen more often than a healthy younger adult.
2. Do you wear glasses or contacts?
If you rely on correction your prescription needs checking, and contact-lens wearers are usually seen at least yearly to keep the lenses safe for the eye's surface.
3. Any health or family risk factors?
Diabetes can damage the retina silently and usually calls for a yearly dilated exam; high blood pressure, a family history of glaucoma or macular degeneration, or an existing eye condition also push toward closer monitoring.
4. Any symptoms, or how long since your last exam?
New blur, headaches, or eye strain are reasons to go sooner rather than wait; and if it's simply been years, a comprehensive exam is overdue regardless of how fine your vision feels.

    The cost of skipping it

    An eye exam is one of the cheaper checkups you can get — and one of the highest-leverage, because the thing it's screening for is your eyesight, which you can't buy back. A routine exam runs roughly $50–150 out of pocket, and vision insurance often covers most or all of it.     How to keep up your eye exams

    The "how-to" here isn't performing the exam — that's your eye doctor's job — it's the upkeep around it: booking the right kind of exam, showing up prepared, planning for dilation, updating your eyewear, and knowing which symptoms can't wait for the next appointment. Read the safety box first (it's the most important part), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you go

    Book a comprehensive exam

    Ask specifically for a comprehensive eye exam, not just a vision screening — a screening only checks whether you can read a chart. Then put the appointment on the calendar so it doesn't quietly slip by another year.

    Prepare what to bring

    Gather your current glasses and contacts, your medical and family eye history, your insurance card, and sunglasses. Knowing your contact brand and prescription — and jotting down any symptoms — makes the visit smoother and more useful.

    Plan for dilation

    If your pupils are dilated, your vision goes blurry and light-sensitive for a few hours. Line up a ride or plan not to drive right after, and bring sunglasses for the way home. Ask when you book whether they'll dilate.

    Update eyewear, keep the Rx

    If your prescription changed, update your glasses or contacts so you're not straining through old lenses. Keep a copy of the prescription — you're entitled to it — and note its expiration so it doesn't lapse before your next exam.

    Watch for urgent symptoms

    Between exams, treat sudden vision loss, flashes of light, a curtain or shadow, a burst of new floaters, eye pain, or double vision as an emergency and seek care right away — don't wait. Then get the next routine exam back on the calendar.

    Signs it's time to book — and symptoms that mean go NOW

    Some of the most important eye conditions have no early warning at all, which is the whole reason to keep exams on a schedule. But there are also signs it's overdue for a routine visit — and a short list of symptoms that are urgent and shouldn't wait for one. This is general information, not medical advice; when in doubt, call your eye doctor:

    • Blurry vision, up close or far away. If print, screens, or road signs aren't as sharp as they were, or you're squinting and holding things at arm's length, your eyes want a look — book a comprehensive exam.
    • Frequent headaches or eye strain. Regular headaches, tired or achy eyes, especially after screens or reading, often trace back to an out-of-date prescription or an uncorrected issue worth checking.
    • Trouble seeing at night. New glare, halos around lights, or difficulty driving after dark can signal changes worth an exam sooner rather than later.
    • An outdated or expiring prescription. If your glasses or contacts feel "off," or the prescription is near its expiration, it's time — you're entitled to a fresh one, but it takes an exam to write it.
    • It's simply been over a year or two. Even if everything feels fine, that's precisely when a silent condition would be advancing unnoticed. Feeling fine is not the same as being checked.
    • ⛑️ Treat these as urgent — seek care immediately: sudden vision loss, flashes of light, a curtain or shadow moving across your vision, a sudden shower of new floaters, eye pain, or double vision. These can signal something serious like a retinal detachment. Don't wait for your routine appointment — get seen right away.

    Common questions

    How often should I get an eye exam?

    There isn't one number that fits everyone, and this guide can't tell you yours — your eye doctor sets it based on your age, whether you wear glasses or contacts, your health (diabetes in particular), and your family history. As a very rough, general starting point, many healthy adults go about every one to two years, kids and adults over 60 are usually seen more often, and people with diabetes or an existing eye condition follow whatever schedule their doctor recommends — often at least yearly. Use those ranges to open the conversation, then let your eye doctor tell you what's right for you.

    What does a comprehensive eye exam actually check?

    Much more than whether you need new glasses. A comprehensive exam checks the health of your eyes, not just your vision — it can look at the retina and optic nerve, measure the pressure inside the eye, and inspect the blood vessels, which is how it can catch conditions like glaucoma, macular degeneration, and diabetic damage before you'd notice any symptoms. Because the eye is the one place a doctor sees blood vessels directly, an exam can even turn up early signs of whole-body conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure. The glasses check is real, but it's only one part of a much broader health check.

    Is a vision screening the same as an eye exam?

    No — and this is an important difference. A vision screening, like the ones done at school, work, or the DMV, is a quick check of whether you can read a chart at a certain distance. It's useful for flagging obvious problems, but it does not examine the health of your eyes and can easily pass someone who has a serious, symptomless condition developing. A comprehensive eye exam, done by an eye doctor, is a far more thorough evaluation of both your vision and your eye health. Passing a screening is not a substitute for a real exam.

    What is dilation, and will I need a ride?

    Dilation is when the eye doctor puts in drops that widen your pupils so they can see more of the inside of your eye — the retina and optic nerve especially. It's a routine and valuable part of many exams. The trade-off is that for a few hours afterward your vision is blurry (especially up close) and your eyes are very sensitive to light. That's why it's smart to arrange a ride or plan not to drive right after your appointment, and to bring sunglasses for the trip home. Ask when you book whether they plan to dilate so you can plan the day around it.

    Do kids really need eye exams?

    Yes. A child usually can't tell you their vision is off — if the world has always looked a little blurry, that's simply "normal" to them. Undetected vision problems can quietly hurt reading, schoolwork, and confidence, and some conditions (like a lazy eye) are far easier to treat when caught early, while the eyes are still developing. A quick school screening is helpful but limited; it can miss things a comprehensive exam would catch. Ask your child's doctor or an eye doctor how often your child should be seen — the right schedule depends on their age and any risk factors.

    What symptoms mean I should go NOW instead of waiting?

    Some eye symptoms are potential emergencies and shouldn't wait for a routine appointment. Seek care right away for sudden vision loss, flashes of light, a curtain or shadow moving across part of your vision, a sudden shower of new floaters, eye pain, or double vision. These can be signs of something serious, such as a retinal detachment, where getting seen quickly can make the difference for your sight. When in doubt, don't wait it out — contact your eye doctor or seek urgent care. This is general information, not medical advice.
