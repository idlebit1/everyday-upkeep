Wash Your Yoga Mat

Every session, your mat soaks up sweat, body oils, skin cells, and whatever was on the studio floor — and that film is exactly what makes a mat smell, breed bacteria, and turn slick under your hands. A quick wipe after class and a proper wash each week keeps it hygienic, grippy, and lasting for years. Here's how often, why, and exactly how.

🗓️ Wipe after every use 🧼 Deep clean weekly 💧 The key step: dry it fully 💸 DIY for $0–10

Why it matters

A yoga mat spends its life pressed against bare skin, then rolled up warm and damp in a closet. That's a near-perfect home for bacteria and odor — and the same buildup that makes it smell is what quietly destroys your grip. Here's what a little regular cleaning prevents:

Sweat and oils soak in

Every flow leaves sweat, body oils, lotion, and dead skin cells on the surface. They don't just sit there — on absorbent mats they sink in, and on all mats they build into a film you can feel.

Builds up every session

Bacteria, odor & breakouts

Warm, damp, and rolled up tight, that film becomes a breeding ground for bacteria, fungus, and mildew. The payoff is a musty smell — and skin pressed to it can mean breakouts or worse.

Musty smell, skin issues

Lost grip is a safety issue

Built-up oils make the surface slick, so your hands and feet slide right when you need them planted. A mat you can't trust in down dog isn't just annoying — it's how wrists and ankles get tweaked.

Slips & strains

It doubles the mat's life

Gentle, regular cleaning lifts away the grit and oils that degrade the surface over time. A cared-for mat keeps its texture and cushioning for years; a neglected one flakes and goes slick early.

$40–120 mat, protected

Material decides the care

Closed-cell mats (PVC, TPE) wipe clean and shrug off water. Open-cell and natural mats (rubber, jute, cork) drink up moisture and need a gentler touch and thorough drying. Know which you own.

Closed vs. open cell

Harsh cleaners backfire

Bleach, alcohol, and loads of essential oil break down the material and ruin the grip — the very thing you're trying to protect. Mild soap or diluted vinegar does the job without the damage.

Avoid the strong stuff

How often should you wash it?

A quick wipe after every session plus a deeper wash once a week is the baseline for most people. But how hard you sweat, how often you practice, and what your mat is made of all move the dial. Answer four quick questions for a personalized routine:

1. How often do you practice?
2. How much do you sweat on it?
3. Notice any odor or breakouts?
4. What's your mat made of?

    How to wash your yoga mat

    The whole routine is soap, water, and patience — the drying is what most people get wrong. Work through the checklist; your progress saves automatically, so you can step away while the mat air-dries.

    🧴 Care rules that protect the mat (and its grip)

    • Mild only. Use a drop of gentle dish soap or a diluted white-vinegar-and-water solution — never bleach, harsh cleaners, alcohol, or piles of essential oil. They strip the grip and break the material down.
    • Don't soak natural or open-cell mats. Rubber, cork, and jute drink up water; a damp wipe and gentle scrub is plenty. Don't machine-wash unless the maker explicitly says it's OK — many shrink, crack, or fall apart.
    • Dry it fully before rolling — this is the one that matters. Rolling up a damp mat traps moisture and grows mildew and odor. Let it air-dry completely flat or over a rack, both sides, before it goes away.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Spray bottle Mild dish soap or white vinegar Water Soft microfiber cloth or sponge A clean towel A rack or rail to dry it on The mat's care label (check it first)

    📐 Five moves worth knowing

    1. Quick wipe after class

    Mist both sides lightly and wipe the mat down with a soft cloth right after you practice. Thirty seconds knocks back the day's sweat before it has a chance to soak in.

    2. Weekly soapy scrub

    Lay the mat flat. With a drop of mild soap in water (or diluted vinegar), gently scrub both sides in small circles. No need to flood it — a damp, soapy cloth is enough.

    3. Rinse off the residue

    Go back over the mat with a clean, water-damp cloth to lift any soap film. Leftover soap is slippery — skip this and your freshly cleaned mat is somehow grippier than before, but slick.

    4. Press out the water

    Lay a dry towel over the mat and press along its length to pull out moisture — or roll the mat and towel up together and lean on it. Don't wring or twist; that creases and damages the mat.

    5. Air-dry completely

    Hang it over a rack or lay it flat out of direct sun until bone dry on both sides — usually a few hours. Only then roll it up. A damp roll is exactly how mats end up smelling forever.

    When to replace the mat instead

    • The surface is flaking or crumbling. When bits of the top layer shed onto your hands or the floor, the material has broken down — no wash brings that back.
    • Permanent slick spots where the grip is gone. If high-contact areas stay slippery even after a thorough clean and full dry, the texture is worn out for good.
    • It's thinned out and no longer cushions your joints. A mat that lets your knees and wrists feel the hard floor isn't protecting you anymore.
    • An odor that simply won't wash out. If smell persists after cleaning, drying fully, and a vinegar treatment, bacteria have set up deep in the material — time for a new one.
    • The care label forbids the cleaning it needs. Some specialty mats can't be wet-cleaned at all; once they're soiled, replacement is the only real option. A good mat runs $40–120+ — caring for the one you have is what keeps that purchase years away.

    Common questions

    Can I put my yoga mat in the washing machine?

    Usually no — and check the maker's label before you even consider it. Most mats, especially open-cell, rubber, and natural ones, can crack, stretch, shrink, or shed their texture in a machine, and the agitation and spin cycle are rough on them. A few brands explicitly say a cold, gentle, delicate cycle is fine; if yours does, follow their instructions exactly and always air-dry afterward (never a tumble dryer). When in doubt, hand-clean instead — it's gentler and just as effective.

    Vinegar or dish soap — which is better?

    Both work; they just do slightly different jobs. A drop of mild dish soap in water is great for cutting body oils and grime — the everyday film. A diluted white-vinegar-and-water solution (roughly one part vinegar to a few parts water) is mildly antibacterial and good for knocking out odor. Many people use soap for the weekly wash and reach for vinegar when the mat starts to smell. Whichever you pick, keep it dilute and rinse off any residue so the surface doesn't turn slick.

    How do I get the smell out of my mat?

    Smell means bacteria, and the fix is a deeper clean plus complete drying. Wipe the mat down with a diluted white-vinegar solution, let it sit a few minutes, then go over it with a clean damp cloth and air-dry it fully — both sides, bone dry — before rolling. A light sprinkle of baking soda left on, then wiped or brushed off, can absorb stubborn odor. The biggest cause of lingering smell is rolling the mat up while it's still damp, so nailing the drying step usually solves it for good.

    How can I dry it faster?

    Press the bulk of the water out first: lay a dry towel over the mat and press, or roll the mat up inside a towel and lean on it to wick moisture away. Then hang it over a rack, shower rod, or railing rather than leaving it flat, so air reaches both sides. A spot with good airflow — near a fan or an open window — speeds things up. Avoid prolonged direct sunlight and any heat source; UV and heat degrade the material and can fade and warp it.

    My new mat is really slippery — how do I fix the grip?

    Lots of new mats, especially natural rubber and PVC ones, ship with a thin manufacturing film that's slick until it wears off. Give the mat a wash with mild soap and water to cut that film, then let it fully dry; repeat a couple of times over your first few sessions. A light scrub with a coarse salt or baking-soda paste can help break it in faster. The grip improves as you use and clean it — and keeping it clean afterward is what stops the oils from making it slick all over again.
