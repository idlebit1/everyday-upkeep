Renew Your Passport

A passport is good for ten years (five for kids) — but "still valid" and "good enough to travel on" are two different things. Many countries turn you away unless your passport is valid at least six months past your trip, and processing takes weeks. The fix is simple: renew early, before a deadline is anywhere in sight.

🗓️ Valid 10 years (5 for children) ⏱️ Routine processing: weeks, not days 🛂 The six-month rule bites early 💸 Last-minute = expedite fees

Why it matters

A passport doesn't fail on its printed expiration date — it fails earlier, quietly, in ways that only surface at the airport check-in desk or a border. Renewing early is how you sidestep every one of these:

The six-month rule

Many countries require your passport to stay valid for at least six months beyond your travel dates. A passport that's "still valid" can still get you denied boarding or turned around at entry.

Denied at the border

Processing takes weeks

Routine renewals are measured in weeks, not days — and stretch out further in peak spring and summer season. Start late and your only options are expensive expediting or a canceled trip.

Weeks, longer in season

Damage & full pages

A passport that's water-damaged, torn, or out of blank visa pages may be refused even before it expires. Either one is its own reason to replace the book, on its own timeline.

Refused before expiry

Early kills the rush

Renew with a year of validity to spare and the whole thing is a calm, by-mail errand. Wait until a trip is booked and it becomes a stressful, pricier scramble against the clock.

$0 stress vs. rush fees

Kids' passports are different

Children's passports are valid only five years and generally can't be renewed by mail — they mean an in-person visit, usually with both parents. That's slower, so plan further ahead.

5 years · in person

When should you renew?

The default is straightforward — renew about a year before it expires — but your expiration date, any upcoming travel, the book's condition, and whether it's a child's passport all shift the answer. Answer four questions for a personalized verdict:

1. When does the passport expire?
2. Any international travel coming up?
3. Condition of the book?
4. Whose passport is it?

    📌 Make it stick: the cadence is roughly every 10 years (5 for kids) — far too long to remember on your own. Set a reminder for about a year before the expiration date printed in the book, so the six-month rule never catches you out.

    When to plan extra time — or get help

    • You're traveling within a few weeks. Routine processing won't make it. Look into the official expedited service or an in-person appointment at a passport agency — book through the official site only, and check the current expedite timelines and fees there first.
    • Your name changed (marriage, divorce, court order). You'll usually need to submit documentation of the change, and the right form depends on how recently your current passport was issued — the official site spells out which path applies.
    • It was lost or stolen. This isn't a renewal — it's a replacement, done in person, and you'll typically need to report the old one lost or stolen first so it can't be misused.
    • It's damaged. Water damage, tears, or significant wear mean applying in person rather than by mail. Don't assume a worn book will still be accepted abroad.
    • It's a child's passport. Children's passports generally require an in-person visit, both parents or guardians present (or documented consent), and proof of the child's citizenship and relationship. Plan well ahead — these can't be rushed by mail.

    Common questions

    How early should I renew?

    A good rule of thumb is roughly 9–12 months before the printed expiration date. That sounds like a lot, but the six-month-validity rule effectively shortens a passport's useful life — many countries won't admit you in the final six months — and routine processing eats several more weeks. Renewing with a year to spare keeps you out of both traps and off the expedite track.

    Can I renew by mail, or do I have to go in person?

    It depends on your situation. Many adults can renew by mail if their most recent passport is undamaged, was issued when they were an adult, and isn't too old — the official site lists the exact eligibility rules. You generally must apply in person if it's a first passport, a child's passport, or if the current one was lost, stolen, or damaged. Check which path applies before you start so you don't gather the wrong form.

    How long does it take?

    Routine processing is typically measured in several weeks and runs longer during the busy spring and summer travel season; expedited service is faster for an extra fee, and a passport agency appointment can be faster still for urgent, soon travel. Exact times change constantly, so treat any number you remember as out of date and confirm the current estimate on the official government site.

    What is the six-month-validity rule?

    Many destinations require your passport to be valid for at least six months beyond your planned travel dates (some use three months, some none — it varies by country). Practically, it means a passport in its final six months may not be usable for international travel even though it hasn't technically expired. When in doubt, check the entry requirements for your specific destination — and just renew early so it never becomes a question.

    Do passport photos really have strict rules?

    Yes — and a bad photo is one of the most common reasons applications get rejected and delayed. Requirements typically cover exact dimensions, a plain light-colored background, your full face with a neutral expression, no glasses, recent date, and no filters or heavy editing. A pharmacy or shipping store will take a compliant one cheaply, or you can use the official photo tool; don't freehand a selfie and hope.

    Where do I actually apply?

    Only through your country's official government passport site or agency — for the U.S., that's the State Department at travel.state.gov. Avoid third-party "expediter" and look-alike sites that add fees or harvest your information, and never email scans of your passport or IDs to an address you didn't verify as official. Start from the government site directly rather than a search ad, and confirm the current fees and timelines there before you begin.
