Replace Your Headlight Bulbs

Headlights don't usually die in a dramatic flash — they fade. A halogen bulb loses output slowly for years, and your eyes adjust to the dimming so smoothly you never notice how little of the road you can actually see at night, until a dark rural curve reminds you. Worse, a fully burnt-out bulb is both a real safety hazard and a fix-it ticket waiting to happen. The good news: on most cars, swapping a standard bulb is a 10–15-minute job in the driveway — twist the old one out, twist the new one in. There are only two rules that really matter: replace them in pairs, and never touch the glass with your bare fingers. Get those right and you'll be back to seeing the whole road again.

🗓️ Typically every ~3 years ⏱️ ~10–15 min on many cars 🔦 Restores full night vision 💸 Costs ~$10–25 per bulb

Why it matters

Headlights are the one part of the car you rely on most when you can see least — and they let you down so gradually you almost never catch it happening. By the time a bulb actually fails, it's been doing half its job for a long while, and a dead one is a problem the law cares about too. Here's what the slow fade and the burnt-out bulb really cost you:

A dim or dead light is a real hazard

When one headlight is weak or out, you see less of the road ahead and you're far harder for other drivers to spot — especially head-on, where two lights read as a car and one reads as a motorcycle or nothing. It's a genuine night-driving risk and, in most places, a fix-it ticket.

A safety risk & a ticket

Bulbs fade long before they die

A halogen bulb dims steadily over its life, so a bulb that still "works" may be throwing out only a fraction of the light it did when it was new. Your eyes adapt to the loss night by night, which is exactly why you don't notice — the road just quietly gets darker.

Half the light, none of the warning

Replace in pairs to stay matched

The two bulbs aged together, so when one goes the other is usually right behind it. Replacing both at once keeps the beams matched in brightness and color — no jarring one-bright-one-yellow look — and saves you a second trip out to the cold driveway in a few weeks.

Mismatched beams & a repeat job

Skin oil on the glass kills bulbs early

Touch a halogen or HID bulb's glass with bare fingers and the oil you leave behind cooks into a hot spot when the bulb runs, weakening the glass and shortening its life — sometimes dramatically. How you handle the new bulb matters as much as the part itself.

A brand-new bulb that fails early

How often should you replace them?

There's no single mileage interval — bulb life depends on the type and how hard you run them. Standard halogens often last around three years of normal driving, but heavy night use burns them faster, and long-life LEDs can outlast the car. The one rule that overrides everything: if a bulb is out or visibly dim, replace it now — and in pairs. Answer four quick questions:

1. What kind of bulbs does your car use?
Halogens are cheap but the shortest-lived; HID/xenon last longer but cost more and carry high voltage; factory LEDs can run the life of the car. Not sure? The old bulb or owner's manual lists the type (e.g., H11, 9005, D2S).
2. How much night driving do you do?
Every hour the lights are on is wear on the filament, so a long commute on unlit rural roads or highways burns bulbs through faster than the same miles run in daylight.
3. How do the headlights look right now?
This is the override question. A bulb that's out or visibly dim doesn't wait for a schedule — it's a replace-now job, and you do both sides so the pair stays matched.
4. How easy is it to reach the bulbs?
On many cars you twist the bulb out from behind in minutes; on others the engine bay is cramped or the whole assembly must come out — which doesn't change the schedule, but does decide whether it's a driveway job or a shop visit.

    How to replace your headlight bulbs

    On most cars the whole job is: confirm the right bulb, open the hood and find the back of the housing, unplug and unclip the old bulb, twist the new one in without touching the glass, reconnect, and test both beams. Read the safety notes first — the glass rule and the cool-down rule are the two that bite people — then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Work smart — heat, high voltage, and the glass rule

    • Let the bulb cool before you touch it. Halogens run extremely hot and stay hot for a while after the lights are off. Turn everything off and give it several minutes, or you'll get a nasty burn reaching into the housing.
    • Never touch the glass of a halogen or HID bulb with bare fingers. The oils from your skin create a hot spot that weakens the glass and shortens the bulb's life. Handle the new bulb by its plastic or metal base only — with clean gloves or a clean cloth — and if you slip, wipe the glass with rubbing alcohol before fitting it.
    • HID/xenon ballasts carry high voltage. Xenon systems run thousands of volts through the igniter. Turn the car and lights off (and disconnect the battery if you're unsure) before unplugging anything, and consider leaving HID work to a shop.
    • Replace in pairs for even output. A new bulb next to an aged one looks lopsided and one-sided to oncoming drivers. Do both sides while you're in there so the beams stay matched in brightness and color.
    • Check the aim after you're done. A bulb that's seated slightly off — or a housing you bumped — can throw the beam too high and blind oncoming drivers. Park a few feet from a wall or garage door and confirm both beams land level and even.
    • Know when it's a shop job. If the housing is sealed or the bumper/fender has to come off to reach the bulb, that's beyond a quick driveway swap. And be aware that dropping aftermarket "LED conversion" bulbs into a halogen housing can be illegal and can blind other drivers — check your local rules before you do.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Confirm the bulb & buy a pair

    Find your exact bulb code in the owner's manual, on the old bulb, or with a quick lookup by your year and model — common ones read like H11, 9005, or D2S. Buy a matched pair so both sides end up the same brightness and color.

    Open the hood & reach the back

    With the engine cool and lights off, find the back of the headlight housing under the hood. Unplug the wiring connector, then release the retaining clip, wire spring, or twist-ring that holds the bulb in its socket.

    Swap the bulb — by the base only

    Pull the old bulb straight out of the socket. Set the new one in holding it only by the base, with a glove or clean cloth, so your fingers never touch the glass. If you do touch it, wipe it with rubbing alcohol before fitting.

    Reseat the clip & reconnect firmly

    Lock the retaining clip or twist-ring back into place, then push the wiring connector on until it clicks fully home. A connector that's only half-seated is the most common reason a brand-new bulb still won't light.

    Test both beams & check the aim

    Turn on the low beams, then the highs, and confirm both sides light. Do the matching bulb on the other side, then park a few feet from a wall or garage door and check that both beams land level and even — not aimed up into oncoming eyes.

    Signs your headlights are overdue — and when to call a pro

    A good headlight is one you never think about: bright, white, evenly aimed, lighting the road well past your stopping distance. These are the signals it's time for fresh bulbs — and the handful of situations that aren't a quick driveway swap and are worth a pro:

    • One light out, or both gone dim and yellow. The obvious cases — a dead bulb is a replace-now job (and a ticket risk), and a pair that's lost its white, bright punch is past due. Do both sides so the pair stays matched.
    • You notice you can't see as far at night. If you're suddenly outrunning your headlights on dark roads or straining to read signs and curves, the bulbs have faded more than you realized — your eyes just adapted along the way.
    • A sealed or hard-to-reach assembly. If the housing is sealed, or the bumper or fender must come off to reach the bulb, that's beyond a quick swap. A shop has the right access and won't risk cracking a clip or fascia. That's a pro job.
    • HID/xenon or adaptive systems. Xenon ballasts carry high voltage, and adaptive/auto-leveling headlights often need recoding or recalibration after a swap. Unless you know the system, leave these to a shop. That's a pro job.
    • A brand-new bulb that dies again right away. If a fresh bulb fails almost immediately, suspect a wiring fault, a corroded connector, or a bad relay rather than the bulb. That's an electrical diagnosis, not another bulb. That's a pro job.
    • Condensation or water inside the housing. Fog or droplets sealed inside the headlight mean a failed seal or a cracked housing letting moisture in — which will keep killing bulbs and corroding contacts. The assembly needs resealing or replacing. That's a pro job.
    • Pro tip: if the bulbs are fine but the lenses look cloudy, foggy, or yellow, that's oxidized plastic, not a bulb problem. A ~$15–25 restoration kit (or a shop polish) brings the clarity back — fresh bulbs behind a hazy lens still can't reach the road.
    • While you're in there: headlight bulbs are one of a handful of quick visibility-and-safety swaps you can do yourself. The same driveway half-hour covers replacing wiper blades, and if you're comfortable under the hood, the car battery and cabin air filter are just as approachable.

    Common questions

    How often do headlight bulbs need replacing?

    There's no single mileage figure — it depends on the bulb type and how hard you run them. A standard halogen bulb typically lasts somewhere around three years of normal driving, give or take; heavy night use on dark rural roads or long highway commutes burns them through faster, while a car that rarely drives after dark can go longer. HID/xenon bulbs generally last longer than halogens, and factory LED units can run essentially the life of the car. The smarter rule than any interval: do a quick brightness check a couple of times a year — compare the two sides, and look at how far down the road they actually reach — and replace them the moment one dims or fails. And when you do, replace both sides at once.

    Why can't I touch the glass with my fingers?

    Because the natural oils on your skin leave a residue on the bulb's glass, and on a halogen or HID bulb that residue creates a hot spot when the bulb heats up. The glass runs very hot in use, and an oily patch heats unevenly — it can weaken or even crack the envelope and shorten the bulb's life, sometimes causing a brand-new bulb to fail far too early. So handle the new bulb only by its plastic or metal base, ideally wearing clean gloves or holding it in a clean cloth. If you do brush the glass by accident, don't panic — just wipe it down with a little rubbing alcohol on a clean cloth and let it dry before you fit it. (Factory LED units are less sensitive, but the habit is a good one to keep.)

    Do I have to replace both at once?

    You don't have to, but it's strongly recommended, and here's why. The two bulbs have lived the same life — same hours, same vibration, same age — so when one finally burns out, the other is usually not far behind. Replace only the dead one and you'll likely be back out in the cold driveway in a few weeks doing the second. There's also a visible mismatch: a fresh bulb next to an aged one looks noticeably brighter and whiter, which reads as lopsided to you and to oncoming drivers. Replacing them as a matched pair keeps both sides even in brightness and color, and saves you a second trip. Bulbs are cheap; your time and a balanced beam are worth more.

    How do I find the right bulb for my car?

    Three reliable ways. First, check your owner's manual — it lists the exact bulb type for low beam, high beam, fog, and so on. Second, pull the old bulb and read the markings on its base; you'll usually see a code like H11, 9005 (HB3), 9006 (HB4), H7, or D2S/D2R for xenon. Third, look it up by your year, make, and model — auto-parts retailers have a bulb-finder for exactly this. Be careful with one trap: low beam and high beam are often different bulb types on the same car, so confirm which one you're replacing. Once you have the code, buy a matched pair from a reputable brand, and decide between a standard, a brighter "performance" halogen, or a long-life version depending on whether you value maximum brightness or longest life.

    My new bulb still doesn't work — what's wrong?

    Start with the simplest and most common cause: the wiring connector isn't fully seated. Unplug it and push it firmly back on until it clicks — a half-connected plug is the number-one reason a fresh bulb stays dark. Next, make sure the bulb itself is seated correctly and the retaining clip is locked, because a bulb sitting slightly off won't make contact. Then check the obvious shared culprits: a blown fuse for the headlight circuit, or — if you bought a cheap bulb — a dud out of the box. If the connector, fuse, and bulb all check out and it still won't light, or if a new bulb fails again almost immediately, you're likely looking at a wiring fault, a corroded connector, or a bad relay. That's an electrical diagnosis rather than another bulb, and a shop can trace it quickly.

    Why are my headlights foggy or yellow — and can I fix them?

    If the lenses themselves look cloudy, hazy, or yellowed, that's almost never the bulb — it's the clear plastic lens oxidizing under years of sun and weather. New bulbs behind a foggy lens still won't reach the road, because the haze scatters and blocks the light on the way out. The good news is it's very fixable: a headlight restoration kit (typically around $15–25) walks you through sanding off the oxidized layer with progressively finer grits, then polishing and sealing the clear plastic back to near-new clarity in an hour or so. A shop can do it too. So diagnose the problem before you spend on bulbs: if the light source is fine but the lens is cloudy, restore the lens; if the bulbs are dim or dead, replace the bulbs in pairs. Sometimes you'll want to do both.
