Replace Your Watch Battery

A quartz watch keeps perfect time until the day a tiny coin cell runs out — usually every 2–3 years — and then it just stops, or starts losing minutes. The swap is a quick, cheap job: a $2–10 battery and a few minutes. The only real catch is keeping a water-resistant watch sealed, which is when a jeweler earns the extra few dollars.

Why it matters

A quartz (battery-powered) watch is the most common kind on a wrist, and it has exactly one wear part: a small coin cell that quietly runs out every couple of years. The swap is trivial — but the dead battery left inside is not. Here's what a timely change protects you from:

It just stops

When the cell dies, a quartz watch simply quits — or starts running slow and losing minutes a day. Usually every 2–3 years (sometimes longer). It tends to happen the morning you actually need to be on time.

Late because of a $3 part

A dead battery can leak

Left dead inside for months, a battery can leak and corrode the delicate movement — turning a $5 fix into a repair, or a write-off. Swapping it promptly is the cheap insurance.

Corrosion can total a movement

Accurate time, kept

A fresh cell keeps a good quartz watch within seconds a month — the whole point of owning one. A weak battery is the usual reason a "broken" watch is really just hungry.

"Broken" is often just dead

Battery vs. not-a-battery

This guide is for quartz (battery) watches. Automatic and mechanical watches have no battery — they're wound by motion or by hand and get serviced instead. Smartwatches simply recharge. Know which you own first.

Automatics aren't on this clock

How often should you replace it?

For a quartz watch, every 2–3 years is the honest default — but it depends on what kind of watch you actually own, what it's doing right now, and whether it's sealed against water. Answer four questions and we'll point you at the right move:

    How to replace a watch battery

    For a basic, non-sealed watch this is a ten-minute job. Read the four ideas below first — especially the one about water resistance — then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Care first — read before you pry

    • Don't leave a dead battery inside. A flat cell can leak and corrode the movement over months. If a watch has stopped, swap the battery promptly or pull it out until you can.
    • Water-resistant, diver, luxury, or in-warranty? Take it to a jeweler. Opening the case breaks the water seal; pros replace the gasket and pressure-test it afterward. A DIY swap on a sealed watch quietly ends its water resistance.
    • Work over a clean, soft cloth in a dust-free spot — case backs ping off and tiny parts roll. The cloth also protects the case from scratches.
    • Don't touch the movement with bare fingers. Skin oils and grit foul the works. Use plastic tweezers (metal can short the new battery) and handle the cell by its edge.
    • Match the battery exactly — the code is usually printed inside the case or on the old cell (e.g. a silver-oxide SR626SW). The wrong size or chemistry won't fit right or won't last.

    The matching replacement battery (the code's inside the case) Case-back tool — pry knife (snap-off) or wrench (screw-down) Plastic tweezers A soft, clean cloth Optional: finger cots or gloves, a loupe Water-resistant watch? Skip the kit — use a jeweler

    📐 Four ideas worth knowing

    First, know what you own

    Quartz watches take a battery (this guide). Automatics have a winding rotor and no battery — they're serviced, not swapped. Smartwatches just recharge. And check whether it's water-resistant: if so, lean toward a jeweler.

    Open the right way for the back

    A snap-off back pops open with a thin pry knife slipped into the small notch on the rim. A screw-down back has flats or notches and unscrews (counter-clockwise) with a case wrench. Don't force the wrong tool.

    Note the code, then lift it out

    Read and jot the battery code (it's on the old cell or stamped in the case), gently swing or unclip the retaining tab, and lift the old battery out with plastic tweezers. Don't pry against the gears.

    Fit it, close up, reset the time

    Drop the matching cell in the correct side up (usually + facing you), reseat the clip, and snap or screw the back shut firmly. If water resistance matters, replace the gasket and have it pressure-tested. Then set the time.

    🎉 Done — fresh battery, case closed, time set. Note the date and you'll know exactly when the next one's due.

    When to use a jeweler instead

    A plain watch is a fine DIY job. But a few situations are worth the few extra dollars at a watch shop or jeweler — mostly to protect a seal, a warranty, or a watch worth real money:

    • Water-resistant, diver, or swim-rated watches. Opening the case breaks the seal. A jeweler replaces the gasket and runs a pressure (water-resistance) test afterward, so it stays sealed — a DIY swap quietly does not.
    • Luxury or in-warranty watches. A clumsy pry can scratch the case or void the warranty. Let an authorized service handle anything valuable or still covered.
    • It still won't run on a fresh battery. If a new, correct cell doesn't bring it back to life, the problem is the movement — not the battery — and that's a repair, not a swap.
    • A screw-down or stuck case back you can't open safely. If the back won't budge without forcing it (and risking a slip into the movement or your hand), hand it to someone with the right tools.
    • Tiny, fiddly, or skeleton movements. Some watches pack the battery under clips and bridges that are easy to damage. If it looks intimidating, it probably is — let a pro do it.

    Common questions

    How long does a watch battery actually last?

    Typically 2–3 years for a standard quartz watch, though it varies a lot. A simple time-only watch sips power and can run longer — sometimes 4–5 years on a fresh silver-oxide cell — while a watch with extra functions (backlight, alarm, chronograph) draws more and runs the battery down faster. The honest plan is to expect 2–3 years and treat anything beyond that as a bonus.

    How do I tell the battery is dying before it stops?

    Two tells. First, it starts running slow — losing minutes over a day or two. Second, the classic trick: on many quartz watches with a sweeping seconds hand, a dying battery makes the second hand jump in 4-second hops instead of ticking every second. That "end-of-life indicator" is the watch telling you the cell is nearly flat — change it soon, before it stops and the dead battery sits inside.

    Can I replace a watch battery myself?

    For an ordinary, non-sealed watch — yes, easily. You need the right battery, a case-back tool (a pry knife for a snap-off back, a wrench for a screw-down one), and plastic tweezers, working over a clean cloth. The two things to respect: don't touch the movement with bare fingers, and match the battery code exactly. The big exception is water resistance — see below.

    Will my watch still be water-resistant after a DIY swap?

    No — not reliably. Water resistance depends on intact gaskets and a properly sealed case, and opening the back disturbs that. To keep a watch sealed you need a fresh gasket and a pressure (water-resistance) test, which is exactly what a jeweler provides. If your watch is a diver, swim-rated, or you ever get it wet, have the battery done professionally so the seal survives.

    What battery do I need?

    The exact code, which is usually printed inside the case back or stamped on the old cell — something like a silver-oxide SR626SW. Match it precisely: the number encodes the size and chemistry. If you can't read it, a watch counter can identify it from the old battery, or measure the cell. Avoid guessing or "close enough" sizes — the wrong cell won't seat correctly or won't last.

    What's the difference between a quartz and an automatic watch?

    A quartz watch runs on a battery that drives a quartz crystal — it ticks once a second and needs a new cell every 2–3 years (this guide). An automatic (or mechanical) watch has no battery at all: it's powered by a wound mainspring, either by hand or by a rotor that winds as your wrist moves, and its seconds hand sweeps smoothly. Automatics don't take batteries — they get a full service (cleaning and re-oiling) roughly every 5 years instead.

    My watch stopped — does that always mean the battery?

    For a quartz watch, a dead battery is by far the most likely cause, so that's the first and cheapest thing to rule out. Swap in a fresh, correctly-coded cell. If it springs back to life, you're done. If it still won't run, the issue is in the movement — and that's a repair for a watchmaker, not another battery.
