Test Your Backup Generator

A backup generator earns its keep exactly once: the night the power fails and it starts, runs, and carries your house through the dark. The trouble is that most people find out whether it will on that very night — and the answer, for an untested unit, is often no. The killers are boring and catchable: a dead starter battery, a tank of stale gas, a breaker someone tripped, a switch left in the wrong spot. A monthly test-run under load — not a lazy idle, but the real thing, actually powering your furnace, fridge, and well pump — proves it can do the job and surfaces those faults while you can still fix them. And before any of it: run it outside, well away from windows, because carbon monoxide is invisible and deadly. Here's how to test it right.

Why it matters

A generator is the rare machine whose whole purpose is to sit idle, ready, and then perform flawlessly on the worst night of the year. That's exactly why it fails: nothing about sitting there ready tells you it still works, and the moment you find out it doesn't is the moment you needed it. A monthly test — a real one, under load — is the only thing that closes that gap. Here's what the neglect costs:

Untested means unproven when it counts

A generator that sits untested is a promise nobody has checked. Outages are the single worst time to discover it won't start — you're already in the dark, the storm's outside, and the one machine meant to save the night just clicks and dies. A monthly test moves that discovery to a calm Saturday.

A no-start in the middle of the outage

The usual culprits are boring — and catchable

It's almost never dramatic. On standby units it's a dead starter battery; on portables it's gas gone stale; on both it's a tripped breaker or a transfer switch left in the wrong position. Every one of those is obvious in a monthly test and invisible until the outage otherwise.

A $40 battery you never knew was flat

Under load is the only real test

A unit can idle sweetly and still stall the instant it has to carry the furnace, fridge, and well pump — the exact loads you bought it for. A no-load run proves the engine turns over; it proves nothing about whether it can power your house. Only a real load does that.

A generator that idles fine but can't carry the house

Regular running keeps the engine healthy

An engine that never runs is quietly deteriorating: oil settles, seals dry out and start to leak, and condensation collects in the crankcase and exhaust. A monthly run circulates the oil, keeps the seals supple, and burns off that moisture — so the machine stays healthy between the storms it's saved for.

How often should you test it?

A monthly test-run under load is the sensible default for any backup generator — it's free, it's quick, and it's the only way to know. But how critical your loads are, what fuel you burn, and how old the unit is all matter. Answer four quick questions and we'll set your interval. (Note: many whole-home standby units already auto-exercise weekly on their own — helpful, but usually a no-load self-check, so it doesn't replace a real load test.)

1. What kind of backup do you have?
Standby units usually run their own weekly self-exercise and have a transfer switch built in; portables depend entirely on you remembering to start and test them, and on fresh fuel and a proper transfer switch or interlock.
2. How critical is the power when it's out?
If someone's oxygen concentrator, a sump or well pump, or a fridge full of medication rides on this machine — or your outages are frequent and long — the cost of a no-start is high enough that you never want to skip a month.
3. What does it run on?
Gasoline goes stale in months — ethanol absorbs water and gums up the carburetor — so gas units need frequent running and stabilizer. Natural gas and propane stay fresh indefinitely, which removes the single most common portable no-start cause.
4. How old is it — and when did it last run under load?
An older unit — or one whose last real test you can't recall — is exactly the one that surprises you. The battery, the fuel, and the seals all age, and a machine that hasn't carried a load in a year is an unknown until you prove it.

    The cost of skipping it

    The test itself costs nothing — you're just running a machine you already own. The parts it protects are cheap too: a replacement starter battery runs about $30–60, and fuel stabilizer is a few dollars a bottle. A professional standby service runs roughly $150–300 a year.     How to test your backup generator

    The whole point is a real run: pre-check the basics, start it up, let it carry actual circuits for about twenty minutes, watch and listen, then shut it down set up for next time. Read the safety notes first — carbon monoxide and backfeed are the two things that turn a routine test into a tragedy — then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Carbon monoxide and backfeed can kill — read this first

    • NEVER run any generator indoors, in a garage, in a shed, or near open windows or doors — even for a quick test. Carbon monoxide is invisible, odorless, and deadly, and an open garage door does not make it safe. Run it OUTSIDE, well away from the house, exhaust pointed away, with working CO alarms inside.
    • NEVER backfeed a portable generator into a wall outlet to power your home. A cord run from the generator into an outlet energizes your house wiring backward — it can electrocute utility line workers restoring power and start fires. Use a proper transfer switch or interlock installed by a licensed electrician. (Whole-home standby units already have one.)
    • Test UNDER LOAD, not at a lazy idle. Let a standby run its self-test while actually carrying real circuits, or plug real appliances into a portable through rated, heavy-gauge cords. A no-load run proves the engine turns; it does not prove the machine can carry your house.
    • Keep the starter battery charged. On standby units a dead battery is the number-one no-start. Keep the maintainer/charger working and the terminals clean, and replace a battery that's swollen or won't hold a charge before it strands you.
    • Keep fuel fresh, oil topped up, and the unit clear. Use stabilizer in gasoline and rotate it; check the oil before every test; and keep the enclosure clear of snow, leaves, and debris with the manufacturer's clearances so it can breathe and cool.
    • Let it cool before you touch it, and never refuel a hot or running generator. Spilled gas on a hot muffler ignites. Shut down, let it cool, then refuel. And remember a standby unit still needs its annual professional service — the monthly test doesn't replace it.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Pre-check the basics

    Before you hit start: confirm the oil level, make sure the fuel's fresh (with stabilizer for gas), check the starter battery and its charger, and make sure the area is clear of debris and the unit is OUTSIDE, well away from the house.

    Start it up & let it warm

    Start the unit — or let a standby begin its exercise cycle — and give it a minute or two to warm up and settle into a smooth, steady run before you ask it to do anything. Watch for a clean start and a green run light.

    Test under load ~20 minutes

    Now the real test: have it carry actual circuits — throw the transfer switch on a standby, or plug real appliances into a portable through rated cords — for about twenty minutes, so you know it can handle the demand, not just idle.

    Watch, listen & check output

    While it carries the load, pay attention: is it running smoothly with no surging or alarms? Is the output steady — lights not flickering, motors starting cleanly? Any leaks, burning smells, or signs of overheating? A healthy test is quiet and boring.

    Shut down, reset & log

    When the test's done, let it cool, then top off the fuel so it's ready for next time. Confirm a standby is set back to auto-start so it'll transfer on its own, and log the test date so the next month's run lands on schedule.

    Signs it needs service — and when to call a pro

    A healthy test is boring: it starts on the first try, runs smoothly, carries the load, and shuts down clean. These are the signals that your test just found a problem worth a professional's eyes — and the one that means stop everything and call 911:

    • Hard starting or repeated cranking. If it takes several tries, cranks slowly, or barely catches, something's wrong — most often the starter battery, stale fuel, or a fouled plug. Don't wait for it to fail completely; get ahead of it.
    • Surging, hunting, or stalling under load. An engine that revs up and down or dies when the load comes on has a fuel-delivery, governor, or capacity problem. It idles but can't work — exactly the failure a load test exists to catch.
    • Low, no, or unsteady output. Lights that flicker or dim, motors that won't start, or a meter reading low or wildly voltage points to an alternator, regulator, or capacitor issue — a job for a technician, not a homeowner.
    • Alarms or fault codes. Any warning light, error code, or fault message on the panel means the unit has flagged something itself. Note the code, check the manual, and call for service — don't just clear it and hope.
    • A swollen, leaking, or dead starter battery. A battery that's bulged, corroded, or won't hold a charge is the number-one no-start cause on standby units. Replace it — it's cheap insurance against a silent failure on the worst night.
    • A standby that won't auto-transfer. If the unit runs when you start it by hand but doesn't kick in and take over when the power drops, the transfer switch or controller needs a pro. That auto-transfer is the whole reason you bought a standby.
    • ⚠️ Any CO alarm sounding means evacuate and call 911. Carbon monoxide is invisible and deadly. If an alarm goes off, get everyone outside into fresh air immediately, then call 911 — don't stop to investigate or shut anything off first.
    • Pro tip: testing works best as part of a whole outage-readiness habit. Pair the monthly test with the full annual generator service (test the one you service), keep your emergency kit current so you're covered the moment the power drops, and make sure the sump pump — which often rides on backup power — actually works too. Together they mean the whole house is ready, not just one machine.

    Common questions

    How often should I test my backup generator?

    Once a month, under load, is the sensible default for any backup generator — and it's free, since you're just running a machine you already own. A monthly run keeps the starter battery charged, the seals from drying out, and the fuel from sitting stale, and it surfaces any no-start problem while you can still fix it calmly. Tighten to strictly-never-skip monthly if your loads are critical (medical equipment, a well pump, a house you can't let freeze), if you burn gasoline (which goes stale fast), or if the unit is older or you can't remember its last real test. Many whole-home standby units auto-exercise weekly on their own, which is helpful — but that self-test is usually a short no-load run, so it doesn't replace a real monthly load test. Pair the monthly test with a full professional service once a year.

    Why test it under load instead of just letting it idle?

    Because idling proves almost nothing. A generator can start, warm up, and purr along at no load while being completely incapable of carrying your house — the moment the furnace blower, the refrigerator compressor, and the well pump all draw current, an engine with a fuel-delivery problem, a weak governor, or simply not enough real capacity will surge, bog down, or stall. Those are exactly the loads you bought it for, and exactly what a no-load run never asks it to do. Testing under load means putting real demand on it: on a standby, let it run its exercise while actually carrying circuits through the transfer switch; on a portable, plug in real appliances through rated cords. Twenty minutes carrying a genuine load tells you what an hour of idling can't.

    Why won't my generator start when I need it?

    Almost always for one of a few boring, preventable reasons. On standby units, the number-one cause is a dead starter battery — it sits for months slowly self-discharging, and the maintainer fails silently, so it's flat the day the power drops. On gasoline portables, it's stale fuel: ethanol-blended gas absorbs water and gums up the carburetor within a few months, so the machine that sat since the last storm just won't catch. Beyond those, it's a tripped breaker, a fuel valve or choke in the wrong position, a transfer switch left in the wrong spot, low oil triggering a shutdown sensor, or a fouled spark plug. Every one of these is obvious in a monthly test and invisible until the outage otherwise — which is the entire argument for testing.

    Can I run it in the garage with the door open for a test?

    No — never, not even with the door open, not even for a few minutes. Generators produce carbon monoxide, which is invisible, odorless, and deadly, and it builds up in an enclosed or partly-enclosed space far faster than an open door can clear it. People die every year running generators in garages and near windows during outages, and a quick test is no safer than the real thing. Run it OUTSIDE, well away from the house, with the exhaust pointed away from any doors, windows, or vents, and keep working CO alarms inside your home. If a CO alarm ever sounds, get everyone outside into fresh air and call 911 immediately. There is no version of running a generator indoors that's acceptable.

    Can I plug a portable generator into an outlet to power my house?

    No — this is called backfeeding, and it's genuinely dangerous. Running a cord from the generator into a wall outlet energizes your home's wiring backward, which can push power out onto the utility lines and electrocute the line workers trying to restore your power — and it bypasses your breakers, creating a serious fire risk. It's illegal in most places for exactly these reasons. The right way to power your house from a portable is a transfer switch or an interlock kit, installed by a licensed electrician, which safely isolates your home from the grid before feeding your circuits. (Whole-home standby units already include an automatic transfer switch, which is why they're wired in by a pro.) If you don't have a transfer switch, power appliances individually with rated extension cords instead.

    Do standby generators still need testing if they self-exercise?

    Yes. The weekly self-exercise cycle built into most standby units is genuinely useful — it runs the engine to circulate oil, keep the seals moist, keep the battery topped up, and burn off condensation — but it's usually a short, no-load run. It confirms the engine starts and runs; it does not confirm the unit can actually carry your house, that the transfer switch will hand over the load when the grid drops, or that the battery still has the muscle for a real start under strain. So let it do its weekly exercise, but still do a monthly under-load test yourself: watch a start, let it carry real circuits, confirm it auto-transfers, and check the battery and fuel. And keep the annual professional service on the calendar — self-exercise doesn't change the oil, the plug, or the filter.
