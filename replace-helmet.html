Replace Helmet

A helmet is single-use safety gear on a slow clock. The foam inside is designed to crush exactly once — and even if you never crash, sun, sweat, and heat quietly take it apart. Here's when to replace yours, how to inspect it in five minutes, and what a new one costs.

🗓️ Replace every 3–5 years 💥 After any crash: immediately 💵 Solid replacement: $50–150 ⏱️ Inspection: 5 minutes

Why it matters

A helmet works by sacrificing itself: the EPS foam liner crushes to absorb the energy that would otherwise reach your skull. That design has a catch — the foam only gets one good crush, and everything around it ages whether you ride or not:

EPS foam crushes once

Expanded polystyrene absorbs impact by permanently collapsing its air cells — it doesn't bounce back like a sponge. Wherever it has crushed, that spot offers almost no protection on the next hit.

Single-use by design

UV, sweat & heat degrade it

Sunlight chalks and embrittles the shell, sweat salts work into pads and webbing, and hot-car or garage heat ages adhesives and foam. None of it shows from the outside — it just accumulates.

Decays even unused

Fit & retention systems wear

Straps stretch and fray, buckles crack, the dial-fit cradle loosens, and comfort pads compress flat. A helmet that shifts in a crash protects the wrong part of your head — or comes off entirely.

Loose helmet ≈ no helmet

The damage is invisible

Crushed foam hides under the shell; hairline cracks hide under stickers and paint. After an impact, a helmet can look factory-fresh and still be spent. That's why the rule is date- and event-based, not looks-based.

No visible warning

Protection tech moves on

A five-year-old helmet predates a lot: rotational-impact liners like MIPS are now standard at mid-range prices, coverage has extended, and independent impact ratings make good picks easy. Replacing is an upgrade, not just a reset.

Better safety, same price

How often should you replace it?

Manufacturers say every 3–5 years from first use — and any crash means immediately, no matter the age. Where you fall in that window depends on how the helmet has lived. Answer five questions for your number:

1. Has it ever been in a crash — or taken a real hit?
2. How long have you owned it?
3. Where does it live between rides?
4. How often do you wear it?
5. Any visible damage?

    What a replacement costs

    Helmets are unusual safety gear: in the U.S., every bike helmet sold must pass the same CPSC impact standard, so the $30 one and the $300 one clear the same bar. Price buys weight, venting, comfort, and aero — not a pass on the 5-year clock (typical U.S. estimates):

    $60–150

    $150–300+

    🪖 A decade of on-schedule helmets (2 × $100)≈ $200
    🏥 One ER visit for a head injury (exam + CT scan)≈ $1,500–3,000+
    Spread over five years, a good helmet costs about $1.50 a month — and study after study finds helmets cut the risk of serious head injury by roughly half or more. There is no cheaper insurance anywhere in your gear bag. Bonus: many brands (Giro, Bell, Specialized, POC, and others) run crash-replacement programs with a meaningful discount — register the new one when you buy it.

    How to inspect — and replace — your helmet

    The inspection takes five minutes in good light. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can finish after a trip to the bike shop.

    ⚠️ After any crash, replace it — the damage can be invisible

    • EPS foam crushes at the microscopic level first. A helmet that took a hit can look perfect while the liner under the shell is already collapsed where it matters.
    • One impact spends the protection. Wherever the foam compressed, the next hit lands on a layer that's already done its job. No inspection can certify a crashed helmet as safe.
    • This includes hits without your head in it — a hard bounce off a car roof onto the road deserves the same suspicion as a low-speed spill.
    • When in doubt, it's a $60 decision protecting the only head you get. Doubt should always lose.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Bright light or a flashlight Your eyes and thumbs A mirror (for the fit check) Scissors (to retire the old one) Somewhere to log the purchase date

    📐 Four checks worth knowing

    Shell & foam check

    In bright light, look over the shell for spider cracks and the foam for dents, gouges, or crumbling — pull the comfort pads to see the liner. Any separation where shell meets foam also fails it.

    Strap & buckle wear

    Run the webbing through your fingers: fraying, stiffness, or sun-bleached fading means it's aged. Click the buckle and yank — it should never pop open. Check the dial-fit cradle still holds tension.

    The two-finger fit check

    Worn level, the rim should sit about two finger-widths above your eyebrows. Straps form a V just under each ear, with room for only one finger between strap and chin. Shake your head — the helmet moves your scalp, not itself.

    Find the date stamp

    Every certified helmet carries a sticker inside with the month and year of manufacture, usually under the pads. That date plus 5 years is your hard ceiling; first-use date plus 3–5 is the working window.

    ♻️ What to do with the old one: most curbside recycling can't take helmets — the EPS foam is fused to the plastic shell and straps. Before it goes in the trash, cut the straps off (and ideally crack the shell) so nobody pulls a spent helmet out and rides in it. Never donate or hand down a retired helmet; a few manufacturers and specialty EPS programs do accept them, so it's worth a quick check.

    When in doubt, replace

    • Any crash or real impact — with your head in it or a hard bounce without. There is no inspection that clears a crashed helmet; this one isn't a judgment call.
    • You can't find the date sticker and can't remember buying it. A helmet of unknown age is past its window by definition.
    • It came to you secondhand. You can't verify its crash history, and crash damage is invisible — buy new, even on a budget.
    • The fit has gone loose and new pads or the dial can't make it snug again. Compressed padding and stretched webbing don't recover.
    • It lives in a hot car or hangs in the sun. Chalky, faded shell color is UV damage you can see; assume more you can't.
    • Anything is cracked, frayed, crumbling, or the buckle pops under a hard tug. One failed component fails the whole system.
    • A fully certified replacement starts around $25–40. Measured against what it protects, this is the cheapest "when in doubt" rule you'll ever follow.

    Common questions

    Does an unused helmet expire — even sitting in a closet?

    Slowly, yes. Here's the honest picture: lab studies on used helmets (including a large one by MEA Forensic) found that EPS foam itself holds its impact performance remarkably well for many years. But the helmet isn't just foam — glues, straps, pads, and the shell age regardless of use, and faster with heat and sunlight. Manufacturers' 3–5 year guidance is conservative, partly for liability — but since the failure modes are invisible and a replacement is cheap, it's the smart default. A boxed helmet stored cool and dark has more grace; one that's a decade past its manufacture date should simply be replaced.

    I dropped it off a shelf — is it ruined?

    Usually not. A short fall without your head inside carries far less energy than a crash — your head is what gives the impact its mass. Give it the full shell-and-foam inspection anyway. A harder case — bouncing off a car roof onto the road at speed, or a drop from height onto concrete — deserves real suspicion: inspect closely, and if you see any mark, dent, or crack, retire it. Manufacturers are intentionally cautious here, and following them costs little.

    Is a secondhand or hand-me-down helmet ever okay?

    No. Crash damage is invisible from the outside, so you can't verify a used helmet's history — and you often can't verify its age either. This is the one piece of cycling gear where buying used genuinely defeats the purpose. If budget is the issue, a new $30 CPSC-certified helmet protects better than any used $200 one with an unknown past.

    Is MIPS worth the extra money?

    Generally yes. MIPS (Multi-directional Impact Protection System) is a low-friction inner liner that lets the helmet rotate slightly around your head during an angled impact, reducing the rotational forces linked to concussion. It typically adds about $20 at mid-range prices, and helmets with MIPS or similar systems (SPIN, WaveCel) dominate the top of Virginia Tech's independent helmet ratings. Since you're replacing anyway, it's one of the cheapest meaningful safety upgrades available.

    Where exactly is the date stamp?

    Look inside the helmet for a white or silver sticker printed on the EPS foam — it's often hidden under a comfort pad, which peels back on hook-and-loop. It lists the month and year of manufacture, plus the certification (in the U.S., CPSC 16 CFR 1203 is mandatory for bike helmets; Snell and ASTM marks appear on some sport-specific models). If the sticker has worn off and you have no purchase record, treat the helmet as expired.

    Do expensive helmets protect better?

    Not in the certification sense — every bike helmet legally sold in the U.S. passes the same CPSC impact test, whether it costs $30 or $300. Extra money buys lower weight, better ventilation, aero shaping, nicer fit systems, and often MIPS. Interestingly, Virginia Tech's independent impact ratings regularly place mid-priced helmets at the very top. Buy for fit first: the safest helmet is the one positioned correctly on your head every single ride.

    Does the 5-year rule apply to ski, climbing, and motorcycle helmets too?

    The same logic applies, with the same caveat: one real impact ends any EPS-based helmet, and most makers recommend replacement at 3–5 years of use (climbing-helmet and motorcycle-helmet guidance commonly says about 5 years, or per the manufacturer). Multi-impact foams like EPP (in some skate-style helmets) tolerate minor knocks better but still age. Whatever the sport: check the date sticker, follow the maker's number, and let any crash overrule the calendar.
