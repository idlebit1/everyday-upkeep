Replace Your Cat's Litter

Scooping keeps the box usable day to day — but it never gets everything. Odor compounds soak into the bottom layer, bacteria and a fine dust of pulverized waste settle into even a freshly scooped box, and your cat smells all of it long before you do. The fix is the full reset on a schedule: empty it completely, wash and dry the box, and refill with fresh litter to the right depth. Clumping litter you scoop diligently can stretch to every two or three weeks; plain clay and non-clumping types need a full change about weekly. Do it on time and the box stays one your cat actually wants to use — skip it, and a fastidious cat will quietly find your rug instead.

Why it matters

A cat's sense of smell runs circles around yours, and cats are genuinely fastidious animals — by the time a scooped box reads "fine, I guess" to you, it can already be off-putting to the one who has to stand in it. Scooping pulls out the clumps; only a full dump-and-refill removes what's left behind. Here's what's at stake:

Scooping can't get everything

Even a box you scoop daily slowly builds up odor compounds soaked into the bottom layer, a film of bacteria on the litter and the plastic, and a fine dust of pulverized waste the scoop sifts right past. A periodic full change is the only thing that resets it.

Hidden buildup a scoop never reaches

A dirty box drives cats away

Cats are particular, and a box that's too dirty or smelly makes them simply stop using it — peeing or pooping on your floor, rug, or bed instead. Litter-box avoidance is the number-one behavioral reason cats end up surrendered to shelters.

Accidents on the floor, rug & bed

A clean box is a health win

A fresh box cuts the ammonia smell that stings the air, the tracked litter that spreads dust through the house, and the bacteria a neglected box quietly grows. Less odor and less mess is better for your cat and for everyone breathing the same room.

Ammonia, tracking & bacteria

Right type, right depth, on schedule

The litter your cat actually likes, filled to about two to three inches and changed on a regular schedule, keeps the box something they want to use — not something they tolerate until they don't. Get those three things right and most problems never start.

A box your cat quietly boycotts

How often should you change it?

Scoop every day; the full dump-and-refill is the schedule that matters here. For plain clay and other non-clumping litters, a complete change about weekly is the default — they can't trap odor, so the whole tray goes off fast. Clumping litter you scoop diligently can stretch to every two or three weeks. More cats, too few boxes, and any whiff of odor or sign of avoidance all pull you toward more often. Answer four quick questions:

1. What kind of litter do you use?
Non-clumping clay can't trap urine, so the whole tray saturates and needs a full change roughly weekly. Clumping litter, scooped every day, lets you remove the mess as it forms — so a full change can stretch further.
2. How many cats use this box?
Every extra cat multiplies the waste going into the litter and the odor that builds, so a shared box reaches "needs a full change" noticeably sooner than a single cat's would.
3. How many boxes do you have?
The rule of thumb is one box per cat plus one extra. Too few boxes means each one gets used harder and fouls faster — and a crowded box is itself a reason cats start going elsewhere.
4. How's the box right now?
A box you can smell from across the room, or one your cat is hesitating at or skipping, is already past due — those are the two signals that override any schedule and say "change it now."

    How to replace your cat's litter

    It's a short job once you have the rhythm: scoop and assess, empty the old litter completely into a bag, wash and fully dry the box, then refill with fresh litter to the right depth and put it back where your cat likes it. Read the safety notes first — the toxoplasmosis and never-flush points are not optional — then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Read first — toxoplasmosis, flushing, and your cat's preferences

    • Pregnant or immunocompromised? Don't scoop or change the litter if you can avoid it. Cat feces can carry toxoplasmosis, which is dangerous in pregnancy. If no one else can do it, wear disposable gloves and a face mask and wash your hands thoroughly with soap afterward. Everyone — pregnant or not — should glove up and wash up after handling litter.
    • Never flush cat litter or feces down the toilet. Even litter labeled "flushable" clogs pipes and septic systems, and the toxoplasma parasite survives standard water treatment and harms wildlife, sea otters, and other marine life. Always bag the old litter and put it in the trash.
    • Wash the empty box with mild soap and warm water, and dry it fully before refilling. Skip strong ammonia and citrus cleaners — cats hate the smell and may stop using the box, and ammonia mixing with leftover waste is unpleasant and unhealthy. A wet box also makes fresh litter clump to the bottom, so dry it completely.
    • Don't make abrupt litter changes or force a scented, dusty litter your cat dislikes. Cats can boycott a box over a sudden new texture or fragrance. If you're switching types, transition gradually by mixing the new litter into the old over a week or so.
    • Fill to the right depth — about two to three inches. Too little doesn't cover the waste or give your cat enough to dig and bury; too much just gets kicked over the side and tracked all over the house. Two to three inches is the sweet spot for most litters.
    • Follow the rule of one box per cat, plus one extra. Two cats means three boxes. Enough boxes in quiet, accessible spots keeps any single box from fouling too fast and gives a picky cat options.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Scoop daily, change on schedule

    Keep up the daily scooping no matter what — then do the full dump-and-refill on your litter type's schedule (roughly weekly for non-clumping, every couple of weeks for diligently scooped clumping). Move it up if it smells or your cat starts avoiding it.

    Empty it completely into a bag

    Pour all the old litter out into a sturdy trash bag — never down the toilet, since even "flushable" litter clogs pipes and the parasite it can carry harms marine life. Knot the bag and put it straight in the trash.

    Wash and fully dry the box

    Scrub the empty box with mild dish soap and warm water — never harsh ammonia or citrus cleaners, which cats hate and which react badly with leftover waste. Rinse it and dry it completely so fresh litter doesn't clump to a wet bottom.

    Refill to about 2–3 inches

    Pour in fresh litter — your cat's usual type — to a depth of about two to three inches (or whatever the litter recommends). Too little doesn't cover or let them dig; too much just gets kicked over the side and tracked through the house.

    Place it right and log the change

    Put the box back in a quiet, accessible spot — and keep to one box per cat plus one. Watch that your cat goes back to using it happily, and note the date so the next full change lands on schedule.

    Signs the litter's overdue — and when it's a vet visit

    A box your cat is happy with is one you barely think about. These are the signals it's overdue for a full change — and the handful that aren't about the litter at all and mean it's time to call your vet:

    • Persistent odor even right after scooping. If you can still smell the box after pulling the clumps, the odor is soaked into the litter and the box itself — scooping won't fix it. Do a full change, and if it comes back fast, look at the litter type or how many boxes you have.
    • Your cat is going outside the box. Peeing or pooping next to the box, on the floor, a rug, or the bed is the classic protest of a box that's too dirty, too crowded, or filled with a litter they dislike. Change the litter and rethink the type, depth, and number of boxes.
    • Litter tracking everywhere. A trail of litter across the floor often means the box is overfilled, the litter is too lightweight, or it's overdue — a fresh change at the right two-to-three-inch depth usually settles it.
    • Your cat hesitates or perches on the edge. A cat that pauses at the box, shakes its paws, or tries not to touch the litter is telling you it's not pleasant in there. That's a "change it now" sign, no matter what the calendar says.
    • Sudden avoidance, straining, or frequent tiny pees — see a vet. A cat that abruptly stops using a clean box, strains, cries in the box, or urinates often and in small amounts may have a urinary blockage, infection, or other medical problem. In male cats especially, a blockage is a life-threatening emergency. Litter habits are an early health signal, so don't write off a sudden change as just behavior — call your vet.
    • Pro note: a full litter change pairs naturally with a deep clean of the box itself every so often — see our guide on giving the box a proper litter box clean. And keep up the rest of cat care while you're at it — regular nail trims and the occasional bathing round out a tidy, healthy cat.

    Common questions

    How often should I completely change the litter, not just scoop?

    Scoop every single day — that's non-negotiable and keeps the box usable between changes. The full dump-and-refill is a separate job on its own schedule. For non-clumping or plain clay litter, do a complete change about once a week, since it can't trap urine and the whole tray saturates. For clumping litter that you scoop diligently every day, a full change every two to three weeks is usually fine, because you're already removing the waste as it forms. Those are starting points: more cats, too few boxes, or any odor or avoidance should pull you to change it more often. The simplest rule is that if you can smell the box after scooping, or your cat is hesitating at it, it's time for a full change regardless of the calendar.

    Does the schedule differ by litter type — clumping vs. non-clumping vs. crystal?

    Yes, quite a bit. Non-clumping clay can't isolate urine, so liquid spreads through the whole box and it needs a full change roughly weekly. Clumping litter lets you scoop out solid clumps daily, so the litter that remains stays usable longer — diligently scooped, it can stretch a full change to every two or three weeks. Crystal or silica litters absorb moisture and control odor well, so they often go longer between full changes (you stir them and scoop solids), but you still replace them entirely on the package's schedule, usually every few weeks per cat. Natural litters (pine, corn, wheat, paper) vary widely — follow the specific product's guidance. Whatever the type, multiple cats and any odor mean changing more often than the baseline.

    Can I flush cat litter down the toilet?

    No — don't flush litter or cat feces, even litter labeled "flushable." Clumping litter swells and sets like cement in your pipes, and septic systems aren't built to break down litter or waste, so you risk expensive clogs and backups. Beyond plumbing, there's a public-health and environmental reason: cat feces can carry Toxoplasma gondii, a parasite that survives standard municipal water treatment and has been linked to illness and death in wildlife, including sea otters and other marine animals. The right disposal is simple — scoop or pour the waste and used litter into a sturdy bag, knot it, and put it in the household trash.

    Is it safe to change the litter during pregnancy?

    This is the one health note to take seriously. Cat feces can carry toxoplasmosis, an infection that's usually mild in healthy adults but can cause serious harm to a developing baby. If you're pregnant — or immunocompromised — the safest move is to have someone else scoop and change the litter for the duration. If that's genuinely not possible, wear disposable gloves and a face mask, change the litter daily (the parasite takes a day or more after being shed to become infectious), and wash your hands thoroughly with soap and warm water afterward. Keeping the box scooped daily and well away from food-prep areas also lowers the risk. When in doubt, talk to your doctor — they may suggest specific precautions or testing.

    How deep should the litter be?

    About two to three inches for most clay and clumping litters. That's enough for your cat to dig and bury comfortably and enough for clumping litter to form solid clumps that don't stick to the bottom — but not so much that it gets kicked over the side and tracked across the house. Too shallow and the waste isn't covered, urine pools on the plastic, and clumps break apart; too deep and you waste litter and create a tracking mess. If you use a crystal or lightweight litter, follow the package, since some are designed for a thinner layer. Whatever the depth, top up between changes so it stays in that two-to-three-inch range as litter gets scooped out.

    Why is my cat not using the litter box?

    The most common reason is cleanliness — cats are fastidious, and a box that's too dirty, too smelly, overfilled, or holding a litter they dislike will simply get boycotted in favor of your floor, rug, or bed. Start there: do a full change, make sure you have one box per cat plus one, keep them in quiet accessible spots, fill to the right depth, and avoid sudden switches to scented or dusty litter. But avoidance isn't always behavioral. A cat that suddenly stops using a clean box, strains, cries, or pees frequently in small amounts may have a urinary infection, blockage, bladder inflammation, or other medical issue — and in male cats a blockage is a life-threatening emergency. If a clean, well-set-up box doesn't fix it quickly, treat the change in litter habits as the early health signal it is and call your vet.
