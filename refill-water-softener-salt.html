Refill Your Water Softener Salt

A water softener only softens because it has salt. It uses a brine — salt dissolved in water — to recharge the resin beads that pull the hardness minerals out of your water, and when the salt runs out, that recharge quietly stops. Nothing beeps. But within a cycle or two the hard water is back: spots on the dishes, scale creeping into the water heater, dry skin, and stiff, dull laundry. The fix is almost embarrassingly simple — glance in the brine tank once a month and pour in a bag before it empties — but two things trip people up: a salt bridge that hides an empty tank behind a crust, and pouring in the wrong salt. Get those right and soft water just keeps flowing.

🗓️ Check monthly, refill ~every 3 months ⏱️ ~2–5 min per top-up 🚿 Keeps soft water flowing 💸 Costs ~$6–12 a bag

Why it matters

A softener is the one appliance that fails without making a sound. It doesn't stop when the salt runs out — it just stops working, and the water keeps flowing looking exactly the same until the hardness quietly comes back. Here's what's actually riding on that bag of salt:

No salt = no softening

The softener can't regenerate the resin without salt, so the moment the tank runs dry, hard water starts coming straight through. That shows up fast as limescale on fixtures, spotty dishes, dry skin and hair, and stiff, dull laundry.

Hard water back within a cycle

Scale creeps back fast

The second softening stops, dissolved minerals start plating out again — building on the inside of your pipes, coating the elements in your water heater, and crusting up faucets and appliances just like they did before you ever installed the softener.

Scale in pipes & water heater

A salt bridge fools you

Salt can crust into a hard arch across the tank, leaving an air gap underneath. The tank looks full when you peek in, but none of that salt is reaching the water — so the softener is effectively running empty while you assume it's fine.

Full-looking tank, no soft water

It's a cheap two-minute check

Against everything it protects, keeping the tank topped up is almost free and takes a couple of minutes. Glance in, break up any crust, add a bag if it's low — and soft water keeps flowing while your plumbing and appliances stay scale-free.

Skipped only because it's silent

How often should you refill?

The rule of thumb is the same for everyone: check the brine tank once a month, and top it up before it drops below the halfway line — which for most homes lands around every three months a bag. But a big household running lots of laundry and showers, very hard water, or an older thirsty softener all burn through salt faster and want more frequent refills. Answer four quick questions:

1. How big is the household and how much water do you use?
Every gallon of soft water spends a little salt on the next regeneration, so more people, more showers, and more loads of laundry mean the tank draws down noticeably faster.
2. How hard is your incoming water?
The harder the water, the more minerals the softener has to strip out — and the more salt it burns through each regeneration to recharge the resin. Very hard water eats salt fast.
3. How efficient is your softener?
Older timer softeners regenerate on a fixed schedule whether they need to or not, wasting salt; newer metered units regenerate only after a set volume of water, so they sip it instead.
4. Where's the tank right now — and any bridge history?
If you've found the tank low or hit a salt bridge before, it pays to check more often and keep it fuller — a bridge can hide an empty tank for weeks before you notice hard water returning.

    How to refill your water softener salt

    The whole job is: open the tank, check the level, probe for a salt bridge, add the right salt to keep it at least half full without overfilling, confirm the settings and brine look right, and note the date. Read the techniques first (especially the salt bridge check and choosing the right salt), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Keep it half full, watch for bridges, use the right salt

    • Check about monthly and keep the tank at least HALF full. Never let it run completely empty — softening stops, hard water gets through, and a dry tank can let the resin foul. Topping up before it drops below halfway is the whole game.
    • Watch for a salt bridge. That's a hard crust that forms an air gap so salt looks full but isn't dissolving into brine. Break it up gently with a broom handle — push down carefully to crack it; don't stab hard enough to crack the tank itself.
    • Scoop out "mushing." If a sludgy, packed salt layer builds at the bottom of the tank instead of dissolving cleanly, scoop it out rather than piling more salt on top of it.
    • Use the RIGHT salt for your unit. Use clean pellets, or whatever crystal/type your manual specifies. Avoid cheap rock salt full of impurities if your unit calls for clean salt, and don't mix salt types randomly.
    • Don't overfill. Leave headroom near the top and don't fill above your manual's water-line guidance — an overpacked tank is more prone to bridging.
    • Lift 40-lb bags with your LEGS, not your back. Bend at the knees, keep the bag close, and set it on the tank rim to pour rather than hoisting it overhead. Keep the tank lid on between refills.
    • Know when it's a service issue. If you're suddenly using far more salt than usual, the tank level never draws down, or the water's hard despite plenty of salt, the unit may need service — a stuck valve or a regeneration problem, not more salt.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Check the level monthly

    Once a month, take the lid off the brine tank and just look. If the salt is sitting below about the halfway mark, plan to refill — you want to top up before it ever empties, not after.

    Check for a salt bridge

    Press a broom handle down through the salt. If you feel a hard crust with a hollow gap beneath it, that's a bridge — the tank looks full but isn't feeding brine. Break it up gently; don't jab hard enough to crack the tank.

    Add the right salt

    Pour in clean pellets — or whatever salt your manual specifies — to bring the tank up to about half to two-thirds full. Leave headroom at the top: overfilling makes bridges more likely, and the wrong salt can leave residue behind.

    Check the settings & brine

    While the lid's off, glance at the control head — the hardness and regeneration settings should look right for your water. There should also be some brine (water) in the bottom of the tank, as your manual expects; a bone-dry tank can point to a problem.

    Close up & note the date

    Put the lid back on to keep dust and humidity out, and jot down the date. A quick note is what turns this from "I think I filled it a while ago" into a real schedule — so you refill before it runs empty next time.

    Signs something's off — and when to call a pro

    Most salt problems are things you can fix yourself with a broom handle and a fresh bag. But a few symptoms mean the salt isn't the real issue — the unit itself needs attention. Here's how to tell them apart:

    • Hard-water symptoms are back despite salt in the tank. Scale on fixtures, spotty dishes, dry skin, dull laundry — if these return while the tank looks full, suspect a salt bridge first. Break up any crust and confirm the salt is actually dissolving into brine.
    • A salt bridge or heavy mushing you keep hitting. An occasional bridge is normal; hitting one every month, or finding the bottom packed with sludgy "mush," often means the salt type is wrong for your unit or the tank is being overfilled or run in a very humid space. Adjust the salt and fill level first.
    • The salt level never seems to go down. If the tank has stayed full for a long time, the softener may not be regenerating at all — a stuck valve, a bad timer or meter, or a control-head fault. That's a service call, not a salt problem.
    • The tank is overflowing or full of standing water. Too much water in the brine tank (or an overflow) points to a valve or float that's stuck, a plugged drain line, or a control problem. Shut off if it's overflowing and call a softener service or plumber.
    • You're suddenly burning through far more salt than usual. A sharp jump in salt use, with no change in your water habits, can mean the unit is regenerating too often or leaking brine — worth having looked at before it wastes both salt and water.
    • Pro tip: check the salt and settings first — the vast majority of "my softener stopped working" calls are just an empty tank or a hidden bridge. If soft water doesn't come back after you've confirmed salt, broken any bridge, and the settings look right, then call a plumber or softener service for a stuck valve or regeneration fault.

    Common questions

    How often should I add salt to my water softener?

    Make it a habit to check the brine tank about once a month, and add salt whenever it drops below roughly half full. For most households that works out to topping up every couple of months or so — but there's no fixed calendar, because it depends entirely on how much soft water you use and how hard your water is. A big family running lots of laundry and showers, or very hard water, can go through a bag every few weeks; a small household with only moderately hard water might stretch a bag much longer. The point of the monthly check is that you never have to guess: you just look, and refill before it empties. Softened water is also easier on your water heater's anode rod and other fixtures, so keeping salt in the tank pays off well beyond the shower.

    How full should I keep the brine tank?

    Keep it at least half full, and no more than about two-thirds to three-quarters full — leave real headroom at the top. Half full is the floor because that's your safety margin against ever running dry between checks. But you don't want to pack it to the brim either: an overfull tank is more likely to form a salt bridge, and it can crowd the space the brine needs. Also don't fill above whatever water-line guidance your manual gives. If in doubt, a good target is to bring it up to about the two-thirds mark each time you refill, then check again in a month. Never let it get down to empty — softening stops the moment the salt runs out, and a dry tank can foul the resin.

    What's a salt bridge and how do I fix it?

    A salt bridge is a hard crust of salt that forms across the tank — usually part way down — leaving a hollow air gap underneath it. It's sneaky because the tank looks full when you peek in, but the crust is holding all that salt up out of the water, so no brine is being made and the softener is effectively running empty. To check for one, press a broom handle (or similar blunt stick) straight down into the salt: if you feel a hard shelf with empty space below it instead of loose salt all the way down, that's a bridge. Break it up by pushing down firmly but gently in a few spots to crack the crust — do not stab so hard that you risk cracking the plastic tank. Bridges are more common with the wrong salt, an overfilled tank, or a humid location, so fixing those causes helps prevent the next one.

    What kind of salt should I use — pellets or crystals?

    Use whatever type your softener's manual specifies — most modern units want clean salt pellets, though some call for solar crystals or a specific product. The key word is clean: cheaper rock salt carries more insoluble impurities that settle out and can muck up the tank and valve over time, so avoid it unless your unit specifically allows it. Pick one type and stick with it rather than mixing salts randomly, since different salts dissolve differently and mixing them can encourage bridging or mushing. If your area has a rust problem, there are salts with rust-fighting additives. When in doubt, the manual (or the sticker on the softener) is the authority — it'll tell you exactly what to buy.

    What happens if the softener runs out of salt?

    The softener stops softening. With no salt to make brine, it can't recharge the resin during regeneration, so hard water passes straight through — and because nothing warns you, you usually find out from the symptoms: spots on the dishes, scale building on fixtures, dry skin and hair, and stiff, dull laundry. Scale also starts creeping back into your pipes, water heater, and appliances the whole time it's off. Running truly dry can even let the resin bed foul, which is harder to recover from than just adding salt. The good news is that catching it early is easy: add salt, and most units bounce right back at the next regeneration (you can often trigger a manual regen to speed it up). Keeping the tank at least half full is how you avoid the whole situation. Pairing a topped-up softener with a fresh refrigerator water filter and a periodic water-heater flush keeps the water clean and the appliances happy end to end.

    Why is my water hard even though there's salt in the tank?

    The two usual culprits are a salt bridge and a service issue. Start with the bridge: even if the tank looks full, a crust may be holding the salt above the water so no brine is being made — press a broom handle down to check, and break up any hard shelf you feel. If there's no bridge, the salt is dissolving normally, and the level does draw down over time but the water's still hard, then the problem isn't salt at all. The unit may not be regenerating properly — a stuck valve, a control-head or timer fault, a wrong hardness setting, or an exhausted resin bed. Confirm the salt and settings first, and if soft water still doesn't return, call a plumber or softener service to diagnose the regeneration fault.
