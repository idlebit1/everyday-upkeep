Clean Your Winter Coat Before Storing It

At the end of the season, a winter coat looks fine — so it gets hung up or boxed away exactly as it is. But a whole winter's worth of body oils, sweat, food spots, and salt spray is sitting in that wool, invisible, and over a warm storage summer those traces oxidize into permanent yellow-brown stains and call every moth in the closet to dinner. The fix is one end-of-season clean before it's packed away — and the crucial nuance is that not every coat wants the same one. Wool, cashmere, and structured tailored coats usually need dry cleaning; many down and synthetic puffers are machine-washable, and dry-cleaning solvents can actually flatten down. So the care label decides the method. Clean it the right way, store it breathing, and it comes out fresh and ready when the first cold snap hits.

Why it matters

A coat at the end of winter looks ready to pack away — that's exactly the trap. The damage is invisible going in and obvious coming out: a whole season of body oils, sweat, food, and road salt has soaked into the fabric, and a warm, dark summer in storage is when it does its worst. Clean the coat first and it comes out fresh next fall; skip it and you find out the hard way:

Invisible grime sets in storage

Sweat, skin oils, and food spots you can't even see oxidize over months in a closet into yellow and brown marks — and by the time the coat comes out, they're permanent. What went away looking spotless comes back scarred at the collar and cuffs.

Set-in yellow & brown stains

Moths feed on dirty wool

Clothes moths eat wool and cashmere, and they're drawn to the sweat, oils, and food traces on fabric that looks clean. A wool coat stored dirty is a buffet in a dark box — and it can come out next winter riddled with little holes.

Holes eaten through good wool

Fresh and ready next winter

Cleaning at season's end means the coat goes away done — so the first cold snap, you pull out something fresh and crisp instead of stained and musty. No scrambling to find a dry cleaner the week you actually need to be warm.

Stained & musty in the first frost

The wrong method ruins it

Dry-cleaning a washable down coat can strip the loft so it never puffs back; machine-washing a structured wool coat can shrink it and wreck its tailored shape. Matching the method to the fabric — what the care label is for — is what keeps the coat itself safe.

How often should you clean it?

Once a year — at the end of the season, right before it's stored — is the sensible default for a winter coat. But daily city commuting, visible stains, or a wardrobe of wool all push toward a mid-season clean too, while a coat you wear lightly can stretch further. And the material answer decides the method as much as the schedule. Answer four quick questions:

1. How much do you actually wear it?
A coat worn every day on a city commute absorbs far more sweat, exhaust grime, and salt spray than one that comes out a handful of times — and heavy wear means it earns a clean (sometimes two) before it ever gets packed away.
2. What's the coat made of?
This is the answer that decides the method. Wool, cashmere, and structured coats usually want dry cleaning; down and synthetic puffers are often machine-washable (and solvents can harm down); leather and shearling want a specialist. The care label has the final word.
3. What kind of shape is it in?
Visible stains, white salt rings, or a sweaty smell mean it's overdue now, not at season's end. But remember — even a coat that looks clean carries invisible body oils that set as stains and draw moths over a storage summer.
4. What happens to it next?
A coat about to spend a warm summer in storage must go away clean — that's when invisible grime sets and moths move in. A coat staying in rotation can be cleaned more on an as-needed basis when it actually looks or smells like it needs it.

    How to clean your winter coat before storing it

    The order is the whole secret: read the label and assess the coat, point out or pre-treat the stains, clean it by the right method for the fabric, dry and restore it fully, then store it breathing. Read the rules first — especially "the care label outranks this guide" and "match the method to the fabric" — then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Match the method to the fabric — the care label decides

    • Check the care label first — it outranks this guide. It tells you dry-clean-only versus machine-washable, and that single line decides everything. When the label and this guide disagree, follow the label.
    • Many down and synthetic puffers are machine-washable. And dry-cleaning solvents can strip the natural oils from down and flatten its loft, so for washable puffers, washing at home is usually better than the dry cleaner — not just cheaper.
    • Wool, cashmere, structured, and most leather/shearling coats should be cleaned professionally. Don't machine-wash a tailored wool coat — it can shrink, felt, and lose the shape that makes it look good. Take these to a dry cleaner (leather and shearling to a specialist).
    • Point out every stain to the cleaner — and say what caused it. Coffee, grease, salt, and ink all come out differently. Old, heat-set stains are much harder, so flag them and describe them; the more the cleaner knows, the better the result.
    • If home-washing down, do it right. Use a dedicated down or tech wash (never regular detergent or fabric softener, which clog the down), a front-loading machine, and a long, low-heat dry with a few dryer balls or clean tennis balls to break up clumps and re-loft the fill.
    • Never store a coat dirty or damp. Air out dry-cleaned coats and remove the plastic film before storing — the plastic traps moisture and chemicals and can yellow the fabric. Use a breathable garment bag and a cedar block instead, and don't hang heavy knit coats (they stretch — fold those).

    🧰 What you'll need

    The coat's care label (find it first) A sturdy, broad-shouldered hanger A down or tech wash (for washable puffers) Dryer balls or clean tennis balls A gentle spot/stain treatment A soft brush or lint roller A breathable cotton garment bag A cedar block or lavender sachet

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Read the label & assess

    Find the care label and note dry-clean-only versus washable — it decides the method. Then look the coat over: check the pockets, cuffs, collar, and hem for stains and white salt marks, so you know what you're dealing with before you start.

    Pre-treat & point out stains

    Gently spot-treat a stain if the fabric allows — or, better for anything precious, flag every mark for the dry cleaner and tell them what caused it. Collar oil and salt rings especially: old, heat-set stains are far harder, so don't iron over them first.

    Clean by fabric

    Dry-clean wool, cashmere, and tailored coats. Machine-wash down and synthetic puffers per the label, with a down or tech wash — never regular detergent. Send leather, shearling, and anything specialty to a specialist who handles that material.

    Dry & restore

    Air-dry wool flat or on a broad hanger. Tumble down on low heat with dryer balls to break up clumps and rebuild the loft so it puffs back to full. Either way, make absolutely sure the coat is bone-dry before it goes into storage.

    Store it right

    Hang it on a sturdy, broad-shouldered hanger (or fold heavy knits flat) inside a breathable garment bag with a cedar block or lavender — plastic removed. Stash it somewhere cool, dark, and dry, off the floor and away from damp basements and hot attics.

    Signs it needs cleaning — and when to call a pro

    A coat that's stored clean comes out next winter fresh, soft, and ready to wear. These are the signs it's overdue for a clean — and the pieces valuable or delicate enough to hand to a professional rather than risk yourself:

    • Visible stains, spots, or grubby cuffs and collar. Coffee, grease, makeup, food — anything you can see is well past due, and the longer it sits the harder it sets. Don't store it stained; clean it (or flag the stains for the cleaner) before it goes away.
    • White salt rings along the hem and cuffs. Road salt and de-icer leave crusty white marks that can stiffen and, over time, damage fibers. They need cleaning out before storage, not packing away to set in over the summer.
    • A musty, sweaty, or smoky smell. If the coat smells like the season it just had, that odor is body oils and absorbed grime — exactly what moths chase and what sets as stains. A clean refreshes it and removes the buffet before it's stored.
    • Moth holes or tiny larvae casings. Small clustered holes in wool, or grain-like casings in the folds, mean an active infestation — treat and clean it immediately. Washing or freezing kills eggs and larvae, and the whole storage spot needs attention before anything else goes back.
    • Any wool, cashmere, leather, down, or structured coat you're unsure how to clean. When the care label is ambiguous or the coat is valuable, don't guess. A professional cleaner — and a leather or down specialist for those materials — is the safe call and usually cheaper than a mistake.
    • Down or puffer coats you'd rather not wash yourself. Home-washing down works, but it's fussy — the right wash, a front-loader, and a long low-heat tumble with dryer balls. If you're not set up for that, a cleaner experienced with down (not every dry cleaner is) will do it without flattening the loft.
    • Pro tip: while you're caring for the off-season closet, give your leather pieces and boots their own treatment — conditioning leather keeps it from drying and cracking, and re-waterproofing boots means they store ready for next winter. And do it as part of storing your seasonal clothes so the whole swap happens at once.

    Common questions

    How often should I clean my winter coat?

    For most people, once a year is right — and the best time is at the end of the season, just before you pack it away. That single end-of-season clean removes the winter's worth of body oils, sweat, food spots, and salt that would otherwise set as permanent stains and attract moths over a warm storage summer. There are two adjustments. If you wear the coat hard — daily city commuting, lots of use — or it picks up visible stains or odor partway through winter, a mid-season clean is worth it too. And if you wear it only a handful of times and it stays genuinely clean, you can sometimes stretch further. But the end-of-season-before-storage clean is the one that matters most: it's the difference between a coat that comes out fresh next fall and one that comes out stained and moth-bitten.

    Does every winter coat need dry cleaning?

    No — and this is the most common mistake. The care label decides, not a blanket rule. Wool, cashmere, and structured or tailored coats usually do need dry cleaning (or professional cleaning), because machine-washing can shrink, felt, and distort the shape that makes them look good. But many down and synthetic puffer coats are machine-washable — the label will say so — and for those, dry cleaning is not just unnecessary, it can be harmful: the solvents strip the natural oils from down and flatten its loft. Leather and shearling want a leather specialist, not a standard dry cleaner. So always read the label first: it tells you dry-clean-only versus washable, and that single line should override anything you assume about the coat.

    Can I machine-wash a down or puffer coat?

    Often, yes — check the label, but many down and synthetic puffers are made to be washed, and washing is usually gentler on down than dry-cleaning solvents. Do it right: use a dedicated down wash or tech wash, never regular detergent (which strips down's natural oils) or fabric softener (which clogs the fill). Use a front-loading machine if you can — top-loaders with a center agitator can tear baffles — on a gentle, cool cycle, and run an extra rinse to get all the soap out. The drying is the most important part: tumble on low heat for a long time with a few dryer balls or clean tennis balls, pulling it out every so often to break up the wet clumps by hand. That's what redistributes the down and rebuilds the loft so the coat puffs back to full instead of coming out flat and lumpy. Make sure it's completely, bone-dry before storing — any trapped moisture grows mildew and smell.

    Why clean it before storing it for the summer?

    Because the damage happens during storage, and it's invisible going in. A coat that looks clean at the end of winter is still carrying body oils, sweat, deodorant residue, and food and drink traces. Over the months it sits in a warm, dark closet, two things happen. First, those oils oxidize and set into permanent yellow and brown stains — the classic scarred collar and cuffs — that no later cleaning fully removes. Second, clothes moths are drawn precisely to that sweat and food residue on natural fibers; an unwashed wool coat in a dark box is a buffet with an invitation pinned to it, and it can come out riddled with holes. Cleaning it first removes both the future stains and the moth bait, so the coat comes out next fall exactly as good as it went in.

    How do I get out collar oil and salt stains?

    For collar and cuff oil — the grey, slightly greasy buildup from skin and hair — the safest approach on a wool or tailored coat is to point it out to the dry cleaner and tell them it's body oil, so they can use the right solvent. If the label allows spot-treatment, a little gentle stain remover or a drop of clear dish soap worked in and blotted (never scrubbed hard) can help, but test a hidden spot first. For white salt rings along the hem and cuffs, a dab of plain white vinegar diluted in water often dissolves the salt — blot it with a damp cloth, then let it dry — but again, on wool or anything structured, it's safer to let the cleaner handle it. The one universal rule: don't apply heat (no ironing, no hot water) over a stain before it's treated, because heat sets it and turns an easy stain into a permanent one.

    How should I store a winter coat for the off-season?

    Clean and bone-dry, breathing, in a cool dark spot. First, never store it dirty or damp — that's what causes the stains and mildew in the first place. If it came from the dry cleaner, take it out of the plastic film and let it air for a few hours: that plastic traps residual chemicals and moisture and can yellow the fabric over months. Then hang it on a sturdy, broad-shouldered hanger inside a breathable cotton or canvas garment bag — not sealed plastic, which traps moisture and crushes loft. The exception is heavy knit coats and chunky wool: hanging stretches those out of shape, so fold them flat instead. Tuck in a cedar block or a lavender sachet to deter moths (skip mothballs — toxic and they reek for years), and store the whole thing somewhere cool, dark, and dry, off the floor — a closet shelf or under-bed box, not a damp basement or a baking attic.
