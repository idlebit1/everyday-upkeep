Wash Your Sleeping Bag

A sleeping bag keeps you warm by trapping air in its loft — the thousands of tiny pockets in the down or synthetic fill that puff up between you and the cold. But every night you sleep in it, body oils, sweat, and trail dirt soak into that fill and slowly mat it down, and a flattened bag is a colder bag. So it needs an occasional gentle wash. The trap is that over-washing — or the wrong method — ruins it faster than dirt does: an agitator washer shreds the baffles, dry-cleaning strips down's natural oils, and storing it crammed in its stuff sack permanently kills the loft. Done right, about once a year, the loft springs back and the bag keeps you warm for a decade or more.

🗓️ Wash about once a year 🌀 Front-loader or hand-wash 🧴 Down/tech wash only 💸 Costs ~$0–15

Why it matters

A sleeping bag is one of the few pieces of gear that gets less functional the dirtier it is — and one of the few you can ruin by cleaning it wrong. The warmth comes entirely from loft, and loft is fragile. Here's what dirt does to it, and why the method matters as much as the schedule:

Oils & sweat flatten the loft

Body oils, sweat, and skin cells soak into the fill night after night and clump it together, collapsing the air pockets that trap your warmth. A dirty bag is a measurably colder bag — the loft is the whole point, and grime is what kills it.

A bag that won't keep you warm

Over-washing does more harm

Harsh detergents strip the natural oils that give down its spring, and every wash stresses the fill a little. Wash too often and you break the bag down faster than dirt ever would — when it comes to a sleeping bag, too much cleaning is a real mistake.

Fill broken down before its time

The wrong machine tears it apart

A top-loading agitator washer twists and yanks the bag hard enough to rip the internal baffles and shred the fill, and dry-cleaning solvents destroy down. The method matters as much as the frequency — a gentle front-loader or a tub is the only safe route.

Torn baffles, leaking fill

Compressed storage kills it

Leaving a bag stuffed tight in its compression sack between trips permanently crushes the loft — the fill loses its ability to spring back at all. Dry it fully and store it loose in a big sack, and it stays warm for years; cram it small, and it won't.

A flat bag that never recovers

How often should you wash it?

For a regular camper, about once a year — or roughly every 20–30 nights of use — is the sensible default. Occasional users can comfortably stretch to every two or three years, especially if they keep the bag clean with a liner and spot-clean as they go. The one rule that overrides everything: don't over-wash, especially down. Answer four quick questions:

1. How much do you use it?
Nights in the bag are what load it with oils and grime. A guide or thru-hiker rolls past 30 nights fast; a few weekend trips a year is far gentler on the loft.
2. How dirty does it get inside?
Crawling in sweaty and dirty puts oils straight into the fill; a sleeping-bag liner and a quick rinse-off at the end of the day take almost all of that load off the bag.
3. What's it filled with?
Down is more sensitive — over-washing strips its natural oils — so the bias is toward washing it less, not more. Synthetic is hardier, but the same gentle method still applies.
4. How's the loft right now?
The bag's loft and smell are the real signal. If it won't puff up or has a musty odor, it's overdue now, whatever the calendar says; if it's still lofty and fresh, leave it be.

    The cost of getting it wrong

    Washing a sleeping bag is cheap; ruining one is not.     How to wash your sleeping bag

    The whole job is gentleness: a no-agitator wash with the right soap, a thorough rinse, careful handling of the heavy wet bag, a long low-heat dry with balls to rebuild the loft, and loose storage. Read the protect-the-bag rules first (especially "never dry-clean" and "no agitator"), then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Protect the bag — the rules that keep it from being ruined

    • Never dry-clean down. The solvents strip the natural oils that give down its loft, leaving the fill limp and lifeless. Dry-cleaning is one of the surest ways to wreck a down bag — wash it at home or send it to a down specialist instead.
    • No top-loading agitator washers. The central agitator twists and yanks hard enough to tear internal baffles and shred the fill. Use a front-loader (or a commercial one at the laundromat) on a gentle cycle, or hand-wash in a bathtub.
    • Use a down- or technical wash, never regular detergent or fabric softener. Regular detergent and softener coat the fibers, killing water resistance and loft — and on a treated shell, fabric softener ruins the DWR. A purpose-made down or tech wash cleans without stripping or coating.
    • Rinse thoroughly — run an extra rinse. Leftover soap weighs the fill down and keeps it from re-lofting. Run a second rinse cycle (or rinse and press in the tub repeatedly) until the water runs completely clear and no suds remain.
    • Support the whole weight when you move it wet. Wet down is surprisingly heavy, and lifting a soaked bag by one end can tear the baffles inside. Gather and lift the entire bag at once, cradling its weight, when you move it from machine to dryer.
    • Dry on LOW heat, long, with a few balls — and get it bone-dry. A long low-heat tumble with a couple of dryer or clean tennis balls breaks up the clumps and rebuilds loft; down can take hours. Damp down molds and mildews, so make sure it's fully dry inside before you store it.
    • Store it loose, never compressed. Keep it in a big cotton or mesh sack hung or laid flat — never stuffed in its compression sack between trips, which permanently crushes the loft.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Spot-clean first & check the tag

    Often the only dirty parts are the collar and hood, where face and hair oils build up. Dab those spots and any stains with a little wash and a damp cloth — and read the sewn-in care tag, which can override anything here.

    Wash gently, zipper closed

    Run it in a front-loader on a cold or warm gentle cycle (or knead it by hand in a tub) with a down or tech wash — never fabric softener. Zip the bag closed and fasten the Velcro so nothing snags or stretches in the wash.

    Rinse twice, press & lift carefully

    Run an extra rinse (or rinse repeatedly in the tub) until no suds remain, then press — never wring — the water out. Lift the whole soaked bag at once, supporting its full weight, since wet down is heavy and twisting can tear baffles.

    Dry low & long with balls

    Tumble it on low heat with a few dryer balls or clean tennis balls — they thump the clumps apart and rebuild the loft. Down can take several hours and a few cycles; never use high heat, which can scorch the shell and melt synthetic fill.

    Confirm dry, then store loose

    Make sure it's bone-dry and lofting fully before it goes away — any dampness deep in the fill grows mildew. Then store it loose in a big cotton or mesh sack, never crammed in the small compression sack, so the loft survives the off-season.

    Signs it's overdue — and when to call a pro

    A healthy bag puffs up thick the moment you shake it out and smells like nothing in particular. These are the signs it needs a wash — and the handful of problems that are worth handing to a down specialist rather than risking yourself:

    • Loft that won't come back, even after drying. If the fill stays clumped and the bag won't puff up the way it used to, oils have matted it — a proper gentle wash and a long tumble with balls is exactly what re-lofts it.
    • A persistent musty or mildew smell. A bag that smells stale or moldy has trapped moisture and grime in the fill. Wash it, dry it thoroughly, and store it loose — and if the mildew smell survives a wash, it may have grown into the fill and need a specialist.
    • A greasy, darkened collar and hood. The neck and face area darkens with oils first. If spot-cleaning no longer keeps up and the discoloration is spreading, it's time for a full wash.
    • Torn baffles or down leaking out. Feathers escaping or fill bunching to one side means the internal baffles have failed — often from an agitator wash or rough handling. Small tears can be patched; serious baffle damage is a repair job, not a wash.
    • A bag that feels colder than its rating. If you're cold at temperatures the bag used to handle, lost loft from grime or compression is usually the cause. A wash and re-loft often restores much of the warmth a neglected bag has lost.
    • A very expensive or expedition bag you'd rather not risk. High-fill-power down bags and pricey expedition gear are worth sending to a professional down-cleaning service. They have the large machines, the right wash, and the drying setup to do it safely — usually $40–80, far less than replacing the bag.
    • Pro tip: the cheapest "wash" is the one you avoid. A sleeping-bag liner, spot-cleaning the collar, and airing the bag out after every trip keep oils out of the fill and stretch the years between full washes — which is the kindest thing you can do for down. The same gentle down-washing care applies to your duvet or comforter and pillows; while you're at it, pair this with cleaning the yoga mat in your gear kit.

    Common questions

    How often should I wash my sleeping bag?

    Less often than you'd think. For a regular camper, about once a year — or roughly every 20 to 30 nights of use — is a good rhythm. Occasional users who take a few trips a year can comfortably stretch to every two or three years. The better guide than any calendar is the bag itself: wash it when the loft won't fully spring back, when the collar is visibly oily, or when it smells musty. The single most important rule is not to over-wash, especially with down — every wash stresses the fill, and harsh handling does more damage than a little dirt. Keep it clean between washes with a liner, spot-cleaning, and airing it out after trips, and you'll wash it far less.

    Can I machine wash it — and what kind of machine?

    Yes, with the right machine. Use a front-loading washer — the kind with no central agitator — on a gentle or delicate cycle in cold or warm water. The big commercial front-loaders at a laundromat are ideal because they give the bag room to move without being twisted. Never use a top-loading washer with a central agitator: it grabs, twists, and yanks the bag hard enough to tear the internal baffles and shred the fill. If a front-loader isn't available, hand-wash the bag in a clean bathtub, gently kneading it with cool water and down or tech wash, then pressing the water out. Zip the bag closed and fasten any Velcro first so nothing snags during the wash.

    Down vs. synthetic — does the method differ?

    The method is the same; the sensitivity differs. Both want a no-agitator wash, a purpose-made wash (down wash for down, a tech wash works for either), a thorough rinse, and a long low-heat tumble with balls. Down is the more delicate of the two: over-washing and harsh detergents strip the natural oils that give it loft, so lean toward washing it less, dry it especially patiently (it can take hours and several cycles to fully dry and re-loft), and never dry-clean it. Synthetic fill is hardier and dries faster, but it can be flattened by high heat and crushed by long compression just the same — so still dry it on low and store it loose. When in doubt about which you have, treat it like down; that's the gentler path and it never hurts synthetic.

    What detergent should I use — and why not fabric softener?

    Use a down-specific wash for down bags, or a technical (tech) wash for synthetics and treated shells; both clean the fill without stripping or coating it. Avoid regular laundry detergent — it's too harsh and leaves residues that weigh down the fill and reduce loft. And never use fabric softener: it works by coating fibers with a film, which is exactly the wrong thing for a sleeping bag. That film mats the fill, kills loft, and destroys the shell's durable water-repellent (DWR) finish, so the bag soaks up moisture instead of shedding it. Whatever wash you choose, rinse thoroughly afterward — run an extra rinse cycle — because leftover soap of any kind keeps the fill from springing back.

    How do I dry it, and why the tennis balls?

    Dry it in a large dryer on low heat, and plan for it to take a while — down can need several hours and a few cycles to dry completely. The balls are the trick: drop in a few wool dryer balls or a couple of clean tennis balls, and as the bag tumbles they thump into the clumped, wet fill and break it apart, redistributing it and rebuilding the loft you'd otherwise lose. Low heat matters — high heat can scorch the shell fabric and actually melt synthetic fill. Stop periodically to feel for damp spots and gently pull apart any remaining clumps by hand. Don't rush it: a bag that goes into storage even slightly damp will mildew from the inside, so keep drying until it's bone-dry and fully lofted throughout.

    How should I store my sleeping bag between trips?

    Loose and dry, never compressed. The compression sack is for the trail only — leaving the bag crammed in it between trips permanently crushes the loft, and a bag that can't loft can't keep you warm. Once the bag is completely dry, store it loosely in a large cotton or mesh sack (most bags come with one), or hang it, or simply lay it flat under a bed where the fill can stay relaxed and full. Keep it somewhere cool and dry, out of damp basements and hot attics. Air it out fully after every trip before it goes away, since even a little trapped moisture invites mildew. Treat the loft gently in storage and a good bag will keep its warmth for ten years or more.
