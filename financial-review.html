Do Your Annual Financial Review

Once a year, sit down and step back from the day-to-day to look at the whole picture: your net worth, where the money actually went, what you owe, whether the emergency fund still holds, whether you're really on track for retirement and the goals you set last year, and whether your insurance and beneficiaries still match your life. It's the strategic counterpart to a weekly money habit — a calm, structured afternoon that turns vague money anxiety into a written plan you can act on. Done well, it catches the small leaks before they compound and the big things you'd otherwise forget to update.

🗓️ Typically once a year ⏱️ ~2–4 hours, or split over a weekend 📈 Turns drift into a plan 💸 Costs $0 to DIY

Why it matters

Day-to-day money management — paying bills, watching the checking balance, the occasional weekly skim — keeps the lights on, but it's all close-up. None of it ever steps far enough back to ask the big question: am I actually on track? That's what a yearly review is for. Here's what the once-a-year step-back catches that the daily view never does:

It catches the big picture

Managing money week to week is close-up work — it misses whether you're truly on track for retirement and the big goals years out. Only a yearly step-back puts the whole trajectory in one view, so "on track" becomes something you can actually see instead of hope.

Years of quiet drift off course

Small leaks compound

A forgotten subscription, a little lifestyle creep, an emergency fund that quietly slipped below a safe level, or cash sitting idle in a near-zero-interest account — each one is small in a month, but over a year they add up to real money lost without a single deliberate decision.

Quiet leaks all year long

Life changes ripple outward

A raise, a move, a marriage, a new baby, a new job — every big change quietly rewrites your budget, your insurance needs, your beneficiaries, and your tax picture. They're easy to forget to update, and an out-of-date beneficiary or coverage gap is exactly the kind of thing only a yearly look catches.

Stale beneficiaries & coverage gaps

Anxiety becomes a plan

Vague money dread thrives in the unknown. A yearly review replaces "I think we're probably fine" with concrete numbers and one to three written goals — a calm, specific plan you can actually act on. Knowing where you stand is, by itself, a remarkable amount of the relief.

Money worry with nowhere to land

How often should you review?

A thorough sit-down once a year, plus lighter check-ins through the year, is the sensible default for most people. But complex finances, a big life change this year, or a sense that you're behind on your goals all push toward adding quarterly reviews — while a simple, steady, on-track situation is genuinely fine once a year. Answer four quick questions:

1. How complex are your finances?
More moving parts — multiple accounts, an investment portfolio, rental property, or a business — means more that can drift between looks, so it's worth checking in more than once a year.
2. Any big life changes this year?
A major life event ripples into your budget, insurance, beneficiaries, and taxes all at once — a quarterly check-in for a while makes sure nothing important slips through the cracks.
3. How do you manage your money?
If you're the only one watching the whole picture, more frequent self-reviews keep you on top of it. If a planner already reviews things with you periodically, your own deep-dive can be lighter.
4. How do you feel about your goals?
Feeling behind or uncertain is the strongest reason to look more often — frequent, small reviews turn a worrying gap into a series of concrete, manageable steps you can see working.

    What it costs — and the cost of skipping it

    The review itself is free: you can do the whole thing with a spreadsheet or a budgeting app and an afternoon. A one-time or annual session with a fee-only financial planner runs roughly $150–400 an hour or a flat project fee — well worth it for big decisions or genuinely complex situations. Set either of those against the real cost: drifting off track on retirement for years, or carrying high-interest debt you never sat down to examine.

    How to do your annual financial review

    The flow is simple: gather everything and tally a net worth, look hard at cash flow and the budget, check your debt and savings rate, review your protection and your future, then set goals and do a quick tax-and-credit pass. Read the techniques first (especially the "handle with care" notes below), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Handle with care — this is general information, not advice

    • This guide is general information, not personalized advice. Nothing here is financial, tax, or legal advice for your situation. For real decisions, consult a fee-only financial planner, a CPA, or an estate attorney who can look at your actual circumstances.
    • Be wary of anyone pushing products or "guaranteed" returns. An "advisor" who steers you to commission-based products or pitches guaranteed high returns is showing you a conflict of interest at best and a scam at worst. A fee-only fiduciary, paid by you rather than by commissions, doesn't have that incentive.
    • Keep statements and documents secure, and watch for fraud. When you pull your credit reports, use only the official free sources — beware look-alike sites that harvest your details. Store financial documents somewhere safe, and shred what you don't need.
    • Don't make big, emotional moves over one bad day. The review is for steady, deliberate decisions — not for reacting to a single scary market headline. If a choice feels urgent and fear-driven, that's the moment to slow down, not speed up.
    • Lock down your logins. Protect bank, brokerage, and tax accounts with strong, unique passwords and two-factor authentication. The annual review is a good moment to confirm 2FA is on everywhere it matters.
    • Write things down somewhere you'll find them. A review you can't remember the conclusions of doesn't help next year. Keep your numbers, goals, and the date in one place you'll actually return to.

    📐 Five steps worth knowing before you start

    Gather the picture & tally net worth

    Pull together every account, debt, and statement, and dig out last year's goals. Then tally a simple net worth — everything you own minus everything you owe — and compare it to last year. One number, year over year, tells you which direction you're actually heading.

    Review cash flow & the budget

    Compare income against spending and see where the money really went. Hunt for subscription creep and lifestyle creep — the costs that drifted up without a decision — and confirm your emergency fund still covers a few months of expenses. This is the engine of everything else.

    Check debt & savings rate

    List your debts by interest rate and make a paydown plan that hits the priciest first. Then confirm you're contributing to retirement, that you're capturing any employer match (that's free money), and that the money is landing in the right accounts for your situation.

    Review protection & the future

    Check that your insurance — health, auto, home or renters, life, disability — still fits your life, and confirm the beneficiaries on every account and policy are current. Glance at a will and estate basics, and make sure your investment mix still suits how far off your goals are.

    Set goals & do a tax/credit check

    Write one to three concrete goals for the year — specific enough to act on. Review your tax withholding and any sensible end-of-year moves, pull your free credit reports from the official source to check for errors or fraud, and put the next check-in on the calendar.

    Signs it's time — and when to bring in a pro

    Some of this you can absolutely handle yourself with an afternoon and a spreadsheet. But certain situations are worth paying a fee-only planner, a CPA, or an estate attorney to look at properly — these are the signals to get help (and a reminder that this guide is general information, not advice for your specific situation):

    • A major life event. Marriage, divorce, a new child, an inheritance, buying a home, retiring, or starting or selling a business each reshapes your whole financial picture at once. These are the moments a professional earns their fee — bring in a fee-only planner before, not after, the big decisions.
    • You feel persistently behind or overwhelmed. If the review keeps surfacing the same worry and you can't see a path forward, a planner can turn the knot into a concrete, step-by-step plan — and the structure alone is often a relief.
    • Complex investments or taxes. Equity compensation, a portfolio you're unsure about, self-employment, rental income, or a tax situation with a lot of moving parts is where a CPA or a fee-only advisor pays for themselves in mistakes avoided.
    • Estate planning. A will, powers of attorney, guardianship for kids, trusts, or making sure assets pass the way you intend is genuinely legal work — talk to an estate attorney rather than guessing.
    • Choose the right kind of help. Look for a fee-only fiduciary — paid by you, not by commissions, and obligated to act in your interest. Be cautious of anyone whose "advice" mostly steers you toward products they earn a commission on, or who promises guaranteed returns.
    • Pro tip: a yearly deep-dive pairs with a weekly money review for the day-to-day, an annual insurance review to keep coverage current, a digital cleanup to cancel the unused subscriptions you find, and a home inventory so your coverage actually matches what you own. The annual review is where all of those connect.

    Common questions

    How often should I review my finances?

    For most people, a thorough sit-down once a year — looking at the whole picture — plus lighter check-ins through the year is the right rhythm. Many people pair the deep annual review with a quick weekly money review for the day-to-day. Move toward quarterly reviews if your finances are complex (multiple accounts, investments, a business, property), if you've had a big life change this year, or if you feel behind on your goals — more frequent looks turn a worrying gap into a series of small, manageable steps. A simple, steady, on-track situation is genuinely fine reviewed once a year.

    What should an annual financial review cover?

    Think of it as a sweep across the whole picture: your net worth (and how it changed from last year), your cash flow and budget (where the money actually went, plus any subscription or lifestyle creep), your emergency fund, your debt (listed by interest rate, with a paydown plan), your retirement and investment contributions (including capturing any employer match), your insurance coverage across health, auto, home or renters, life, and disability, your beneficiaries and estate basics, your credit and taxes, and finally one to three concrete goals for the year ahead. The point is breadth — catching the things the day-to-day view never surfaces.

    How do I calculate my net worth?

    Net worth is simply everything you own minus everything you owe. Add up your assets — cash and checking, savings, retirement and brokerage accounts, the value of your home and car, and anything else of real value. Then add up your liabilities — your mortgage, student loans, car loans, credit-card balances, and any other debt. Subtract the second number from the first, and that's your net worth. It can be negative, especially early on, and that's fine; the number that matters is the direction it moves year over year. Tracking that one figure annually is one of the clearest signals of whether your overall plan is working.

    Do I need a financial advisor, and what kind?

    Not necessarily — plenty of people manage a straightforward situation well on their own with a spreadsheet or an app. It's worth paying for help around big or complex decisions: a major life event, equity compensation, self-employment, a portfolio you're unsure about, or estate planning. When you do hire someone, look for a fee-only fiduciary — paid directly by you rather than through commissions, and obligated to act in your interest. Be cautious of anyone who mainly steers you toward commission-based products or promises guaranteed returns; those are red flags for a conflict of interest or worse. A one-time or annual session, often $150–400 an hour or a flat project fee, can be plenty. This is general information, not advice for your specific situation.

    How much should be in my emergency fund?

    A common rule of thumb is a few months of essential expenses — often cited as three to six months — kept somewhere safe and easy to reach, like a high-yield savings account. The right size depends on your situation: a single income, a variable or commission-based job, self-employment, or dependents all argue for the higher end, while two stable incomes and few obligations can sit toward the lower end. The annual review is a good moment to recompute it, because your "few months of expenses" changes as your life and spending change. Confirming the fund still covers what it's meant to is one of the most reassuring lines on the whole checklist.

    What's the difference between this and a weekly money check-in?

    They're complementary, working at different altitudes. A weekly money review is close-up and tactical: skim the accounts, catch a fraudulent charge or a sneaky subscription, glance at the week's bills, and keep the day-to-day on the rails. The annual review is the strategic counterpart — you step back from all that to ask whether the whole thing is working: are you on track for retirement, is your net worth moving the right way, does your insurance still fit, are your goals current. The weekly habit keeps you from drifting day to day; the yearly review makes sure the direction you're heading is the one you actually want. You want both.
