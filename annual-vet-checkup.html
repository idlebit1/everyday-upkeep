Annual Vet Checkup

Your pet ages five to seven years for every one of yours — and is hard-wired to act fine right up until they can't. One wellness exam a year (two for seniors) is how problems get caught while they're still small, cheap, and fixable.

🗓️ Typically once a year 🐾 Seniors: every 6 months ⏱️ 30–60 minutes per visit 💸 Catching it late costs $1,500+

Why it matters

A pet that "seems fine" is not the same as a pet that is fine. The annual exam exists because animals are built to hide weakness — and because almost everything a vet can find early is cheaper and kinder to treat early. Costs below are typical U.S. estimates:

Pets hide illness

Dogs and cats are wired to mask pain and weakness — by the time a cat skips meals or a dog stops jumping on the couch, a problem has usually been brewing for months. The exam is how you see behind the act.

"Seems fine" ≠ fine

Early detection is cheap detection

Kidney disease, heart murmurs, thyroid trouble, diabetes, lumps that matter — all of them are dramatically easier to manage when found at a wellness visit instead of in an emergency room at 2 a.m.

ER visit: $1,500 – $5,000+

Boosters that can't lapse

Core vaccines — rabies for everyone, distemper-parvo for dogs, FVRCP for cats — run on 1- or 3-year cycles, and rabies is required by law. The annual visit is what keeps the schedule from quietly expiring.

Lapsed rabies = restart + fines

Dental disease is the default

Most dogs and cats have some periodontal disease by age three. It's painful, it seeds bacteria toward the heart and kidneys — and the annual exam is usually the only time anyone looks under the lip.

Extractions: $1,000 – $3,000

Parasite screening

The yearly fecal test and (for dogs) heartworm test catch the parasites you can't see — worms that also spread to people, and heartworm, which is far cheaper to prevent than to treat.

Heartworm treatment: $1,000 – $3,000

How often should you go?

"Once a year" is the floor for healthy adults — but puppies, kittens, and seniors run on different clocks, and lifestyle changes what the visit should cover. Answer four questions for a schedule to bring to your vet:

1. Who's due for a checkup?
2. What life stage?
3. What's their world like?
4. Any known conditions?

    How to prepare (and what to expect)

    A vet can examine an unprepared pet — but a prepared owner is what turns a 30-minute appointment into a genuinely useful one. Work through the checklist below; your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ The day-of details people get wrong

    • Ask about fasting when you book. If bloodwork is planned, many vets want 8–12 hours without food (water is always fine). Never fast a puppy, kitten, or diabetic pet unless the clinic explicitly says so.
    • Ask before skipping morning meds. Some should be given as usual, some held until after the blood draw — the clinic will tell you which.
    • Cats: shut the cat in the night before. Cats have a sixth sense for vet day; an outdoor cat who vanishes at 7 a.m. costs you the appointment.
    • Lobby rules: dogs on a fixed (not retractable) leash, cats in a closed carrier — for everyone's safety, including the nervous animals around you.
    • Fearful or reactive pet? Say so when booking. Clinics can schedule quiet slots, room you immediately, or prescribe a pre-visit calming med (common and effective for cats).

    📐 What the visit actually covers

    The nose-to-tail exam

    Eyes, ears, and teeth; heart and lungs with the stethoscope; hands-on belly check of the organs; skin, coat, and lumps; joints and gait; weight against last year's number. Five minutes that screen a dozen diseases.

    The life-stage clock

    Puppies and kittens visit every 3–4 weeks for the vaccine series until ~16 weeks. Adults settle into yearly. Seniors go semiannual — at their pace of aging, six months is years of human time.

    The carrier head start

    Most "my cat hates the vet" is really "my cat hates the carrier ambush." Leave it out for a week with a soft blanket and daily treats inside, and vet day starts calm instead of feral.

    When not to wait for the annual

    The yearly exam is for the problems you can't see. These are the ones you can — and none of them should sit on the calendar until next spring:

    • Not eating for more than a day — and for cats this is urgent: as little as 2–3 days without food can trigger life-threatening liver trouble (hepatic lipidosis).
    • A male cat straining in the litter box with little or no urine — this is an emergency. A urinary blockage is fatal within days; go now, not tomorrow.
    • Drinking or urinating much more than usual. The classic early flag for diabetes, kidney disease, and thyroid trouble — easy to dismiss, very worth a visit.
    • Unexplained weight loss, even with a normal appetite. In cats especially, "getting slim in old age" is usually a disease, not aging.
    • Labored or noisy breathing, or open-mouth breathing in a cat — same-day vet, every time.
    • Vomiting or diarrhea that lasts more than a day or two, contains blood, or comes with lethargy.
    • A new lump that's growing, bleeding, or bothers the pet. Most lumps are benign — but "most" is a finding, not a guess.
    • Limping, stiffness, or reluctance to jump that doesn't resolve in a couple of days — pain that finally shows is pain that's been there a while.
    • Sudden behavior change: hiding, irritability, confusion, or house-soiling in a previously tidy pet. Behavior is often the only symptom an animal will show you.

    Common questions

    My indoor cat never goes outside. Does she really need a yearly exam?

    Yes — arguably more than anyone, because indoor cats are the most under-vetted pets in the country and the best in the world at hiding illness. Indoor life doesn't prevent dental disease, obesity, kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, or diabetes, and the exam's weight-and-teeth trend line is how those get caught early. Rabies vaccination is also legally required in most places regardless of lifestyle — bats and escapes happen.

    Can I do titer tests instead of re-vaccinating?

    Sometimes. Titers measure existing antibodies, and many vets accept strong titers in place of boosters for core vaccines like canine distemper and parvovirus. Two caveats: a titer often costs as much as or more than the vaccine, and rabies is the exception — the law in most jurisdictions requires the vaccine on schedule, titer or not. If you want to minimize injections, ask your vet about titers and about 3-year vaccine formulations.

    Is senior bloodwork really worth it if my pet seems healthy?

    This is where lab work earns its keep. Kidney values, for example, often don't move until a large share of function is already gone — but trends across yearly panels flag the slide much earlier, and early diet changes meaningfully extend good years. Same story for thyroid disease in older cats and liver values in dogs. Even a normal panel is valuable: it's the baseline that makes next year's numbers mean something.

    Will the vet check the microchip?

    Usually only if you ask — so ask. The scan takes ten seconds and confirms the chip hasn't failed or migrated. While you're at it, log into the chip registry and check that your phone number and address are current; an out-of-date registration is the most common reason chipped pets don't make it home.

    My pet is terrified of the vet. Any way to make this easier?

    Lots. Tell the clinic when you book — many will room you straight away or schedule a quiet slot. Ask about pre-visit calming medication (very common for cats, and it works). Do "happy visits": drop in occasionally just for treats and a hello, no exam. For cats, the carrier acclimation in the diagram above does most of the heavy lifting. Some areas also have Fear Free–certified practices and house-call vets who do the whole exam on your sofa.

    What's the difference between pet insurance and a wellness plan — do I need either?

    Insurance covers the surprises (a broken leg, a sudden illness) and typically does not cover this checkup. Wellness plans are basically a subscription for routine care — exams, vaccines, fecal tests — spread into monthly payments. Neither is mandatory: a healthy adult pet's routine care is affordable out of pocket, while insurance is mostly about protecting yourself from the $4,000 emergency. Run the math for your pet's age and breed, and read what's excluded.
