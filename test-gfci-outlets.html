Test Your GFCI Outlets

It's the unremarkable outlet by the bathroom sink with two little buttons marked TEST and RESET — and it is quietly standing between your family and a fatal shock. A GFCI senses the instant electricity starts leaking through a wet hand or a dropped hairdryer and cuts the power in about 1/40th of a second, faster than your heart can register it. The catch is the cruel part: a GFCI wears out and dies silently. A dead one still powers the outlet, so nothing looks wrong — right up until the day you need the protection and it isn't there. The only way to know it still works is to press TEST and RESET on a schedule. It takes thirty seconds an outlet, and it is the cheapest life insurance in the house.

Why it matters

A GFCI is one of the few pieces of upkeep where the whole point is invisible. You never see it working, and — terrifyingly — you can't tell when it has stopped working either. That's exactly why a thirty-second test on a schedule earns its place. Here's what these outlets actually do, and why a dead one is so dangerous:

It can stop a fatal shock

A GFCI constantly compares the current flowing out against the current coming back. The moment even a few milliamps start leaking somewhere they shouldn't — through a wet hand, a dropped hairdryer, a frayed cord — it cuts the power in about one-fortieth of a second, before the current can stop your heart.

Electrocution risk

It fails silently

The electronics inside a GFCI wear out — from heat, surges, age, and corrosion — and when they do, most simply stop protecting while still passing power straight through. Your hairdryer turns on, the lamp lights up, everything looks normal. Nothing tells you the safety net is gone. The only way to know is to test it.

No warning when it dies

It's code-required — and shared

Code calls for GFCI protection in kitchens, bathrooms, garages, basements, laundry rooms, and outdoors — anywhere water and electricity meet. And one GFCI outlet often protects several ordinary outlets wired downstream of it, so a single dead GFCI can quietly strip protection from a whole string of receptacles.

Protects a whole circuit

It catches hidden faults

Testing does more than confirm the GFCI trips. It flags outlets that are already tripped (and you never noticed), miswired ones — the classic "reverse line-load" where the protection silently doesn't reach the receptacle — and plain outlets downstream that have lost their protection. These are problems you'd never spot any other way.

Silent miswiring

How often should you test them?

Manufacturers technically recommend testing every GFCI once a month. In practice, most households do fine on a twice-a-year rhythm — but if your outlets live outdoors, your home is older, or you're near the coast, monthly really is the right call. Answer four quick questions and we'll set your interval:

1. How old is the home — and does it have GFCIs?
Older homes often lack GFCI protection in places code now requires it, and any GFCIs that were added are themselves older and closer to wearing out — both reasons to look more closely and more often.
2. How many wet or outdoor outlets do you have?
Every wet-area and outdoor outlet is a place a GFCI is doing real work. The more of them you rely on, the more it matters that each one is verified — and the more likely at least one has quietly failed.
3. What's the environment like?
Moisture and salt air corrode the contacts and electronics inside a GFCI, so coastal and humid homes see them degrade and fail noticeably faster than dry, indoor ones.
4. Any history of trouble?
Frequent trips can signal a worn GFCI or a real fault, and not knowing which outlets a GFCI protects means you can't be sure your wet areas are covered — both worth testing more often until it's sorted.

    How to test your GFCI outlets

    The test itself is two button-presses, but doing it right means finding every GFCI first (including the ordinary outlets they protect downstream), testing each one, and knowing exactly what to do when one fails. Read the safety box and the techniques first — this is a real electrical-safety task — then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ This is a safety task — work carefully

    • If TEST does nothing — the outlet stays powered — STOP using it and call a licensed electrician. A GFCI that won't trip is no longer protecting you. Don't keep plugging things into it; its job is gone and it needs replacing.
    • If RESET won't latch or power won't come back, treat the outlet as out of service. Either the GFCI is bad, there's a real ground fault on the circuit, or it's miswired. Don't force it — get an electrician to find out why.
    • Keep your hands dry and never test in or near standing water. Don't test with wet hands or while standing on a wet floor. Dry off first; the whole point of this device is that water and electricity are dangerous together.
    • Never poke metal — a screwdriver, key, or wire — into an outlet to "test" it. Use the buttons, a plug-in lamp, or a proper plug-in receptacle tester. Nothing metal should ever go into the slots.
    • Unplug sensitive electronics before testing. The outlet briefly cuts power when it trips, which can disrupt or corrupt computers, routers, and fridges. Unplug or move them to another circuit first.
    • If you smell burning, see scorch marks, or feel a warm or buzzing outlet — stop. Don't test it. Switch off the breaker for that circuit and call a licensed electrician; that's a fire risk, not a routine test.
    • An older home with no GFCI protection in its wet areas should be upgraded by an electrician. You can't test protection that was never installed. If your kitchen, bath, or outdoor outlets have no TEST/RESET buttons and aren't protected at the panel, get them brought up to code.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Find every GFCI first

    Walk the house for outlets with TEST and RESET buttons — kitchen, baths, garage, basement, laundry, outdoors. Remember one GFCI often protects several plain outlets downstream, and some protection lives on GFCI breakers in the panel instead.

    Press TEST, then RESET

    Plug a lamp into the GFCI. Press TEST: the power should cut, the lamp should go dark, and the RESET button should pop out with a click. Press RESET back in: power returns and the lamp lights. If either step fails, the outlet needs attention.

    Use a plug-in tester too

    A $10 receptacle tester reads an outlet's wiring from its indicator lights — catching reversed or open wiring on the ordinary outlets a GFCI protects. Many have their own button that trips the GFCI, letting you confirm protection right at a downstream receptacle.

    Test GFCI/AFCI breakers too

    Some protection lives in the panel, not the wall. A GFCI or AFCI breaker has its own TEST button — press it and the breaker should trip to the middle position. To reset, flip it fully OFF and then back ON; just nudging it on won't re-arm it.

    Know what a failure means

    If TEST won't trip the outlet, or RESET won't hold and restore power, the protection is gone. That GFCI needs replacing — a confident DIYer with the power off can swap it, but if you're unsure, or it keeps failing, call a licensed electrician.

    Signs something's wrong — and when to call a pro

    A healthy GFCI is silent and forgettable: it trips on TEST, resets cleanly, and holds power. These are the signals that something is wrong — and the cases that aren't a DIY job at all and belong to a licensed electrician:

    • An outlet that won't reset, or trips constantly. If RESET won't hold or the GFCI trips again as soon as you plug something in, there's either a real ground fault on the circuit or a failing device — not something to keep fighting. Get it diagnosed.
    • TEST does nothing. If pressing TEST leaves the power on, the protection is gone even though the outlet still works. Replace the GFCI; if you're not comfortable doing it safely with the power off, that's an electrician's job.
    • No GFCI protection at all in your wet areas. Kitchen, bath, garage, basement, laundry, and outdoor outlets with no TEST/RESET buttons and no protection at the panel need to be brought up to code by an electrician — you can't test what was never installed.
    • Scorch marks, a warm or buzzing outlet, or a burning smell. Stop using it, switch off the breaker for that circuit, and call an electrician right away. That's a fire risk, not a maintenance item — never a "test it and see."
    • Aluminum wiring or an old panel. Homes with aluminum branch wiring or an outdated or recalled panel have failure modes a simple outlet swap won't fix. Have an electrician evaluate the whole system before relying on any single outlet.
    • You're not sure which outlets a GFCI protects. If you can't trace what's downstream of a GFCI, you can't be sure your wet areas are covered. A plug-in tester helps, but an electrician can map and label the circuit properly.
    • Pro tip: when an electrician replaces or maps a GFCI, ask them to label each one with the outlets it protects. A two-minute label turns every future test from a scavenger hunt into a thirty-second check.

    Common questions

    How often should I really test — monthly or every six months?

    Manufacturers and many safety bodies technically recommend testing every GFCI once a month, and there's nothing wrong with that — it takes seconds. In practice, most households keep up better with a twice-a-year rhythm (a great pairing with your spring and fall smoke- and CO-detector tests), and that's a sensible default for a modern home with reliable outlets in average conditions. Tighten toward monthly if any of three things is true: the outlets are outdoors or in damp spots, your home is older with aging GFCIs, or you live somewhere humid or coastal where salt air corrodes the contacts faster. The honest answer is that any regular schedule you'll actually follow beats a strict one you abandon — so pick the interval the quiz above gives you and put it on a calendar.

    What if pressing TEST does nothing?

    If you press TEST and the power stays on — the lamp keeps glowing, RESET doesn't pop out — the GFCI has failed and is no longer protecting you, even though the outlet still works. This is exactly the silent failure that makes testing worth doing. Stop using that outlet for anything in a wet area and replace the GFCI. A confident DIYer can swap a GFCI receptacle for about $15–30, but only with the circuit's breaker switched fully off and verified dead first, and with the wires landed on the correct LINE and LOAD terminals — getting that backward defeats the protection. If you're not completely sure of any of that, or it's a breaker rather than a receptacle, call a licensed electrician. A failed GFCI is cheap to fix and dangerous to ignore.

    What if RESET won't stay in, or power won't come back?

    First, make sure you're pressing RESET firmly until it clicks and stays in — a light tap often won't latch. If it still won't hold, or power doesn't return, don't force it: the GFCI is doing its job by refusing to restore power to a circuit it thinks is faulted. The cause could be a genuine ground fault somewhere downstream (a damaged cord, a wet appliance, moisture in an outdoor box), a worn-out GFCI, or a wiring problem. Unplug everything on the circuit and try resetting once with nothing connected; if it holds, plug things back in one at a time to find the culprit. If it won't reset even with nothing plugged in, or it keeps tripping, that's electrician territory — there may be a real fault you don't want to override.

    GFCI vs. AFCI — what's the difference?

    They protect against two different dangers. A GFCI (ground-fault circuit interrupter) watches for current leaking to ground — the situation that electrocutes a person, like a hairdryer hitting bathwater — and cuts power in a fraction of a second. An AFCI (arc-fault circuit interrupter) watches for the dangerous electrical arcing that starts fires — a frayed wire in a wall, a loose connection sparking behind an outlet — and shuts off before it can ignite. GFCIs are required in wet areas; AFCIs are required in living spaces like bedrooms and living rooms. Many modern outlets and breakers are now dual-function (DF), doing both jobs at once. Both types usually have a TEST button and should be tested on the same schedule — and the same rule applies: if it won't trip on TEST, it's not protecting you.

    My outlet works fine — do I still need to test it?

    Yes — and this is the single most important thing to understand about a GFCI. "Works fine" only tells you it's delivering power; it tells you nothing about whether it's still protecting you. The protective electronics can fail completely while the outlet keeps passing current straight through, so a dead GFCI looks and behaves exactly like a healthy one until the instant you need it to save your life. There is no warning light, no buzz, no symptom. Pressing TEST is the only way to confirm the safety function is intact. An outlet that powers your devices but won't trip on TEST is the most dangerous kind, because it lulls you into trusting protection that isn't there.

    An outlet suddenly has no power — what do I check first?

    Before you assume the outlet is broken or call anyone, check for a tripped GFCI. Because one GFCI commonly protects several ordinary outlets downstream, a GFCI that tripped somewhere else — often in a bathroom, garage, or outside — can kill power to a plain outlet in another room entirely, and people chase the wrong outlet for ages. Look around the house (and outdoors) for any GFCI whose RESET button has popped out, and press it back in. That frequently restores the dead outlet instantly. If you can't find a tripped GFCI, check your breaker panel for a tripped breaker and reset it fully off-then-on. If both look fine and the outlet's still dead — or a GFCI trips again the moment you reset it — there's a fault worth an electrician's look.
