Fertilize Your Lawn

Grass is a crop you happen to walk on, and like any crop it gets hungry. Feed it at the right moments and it crowds out weeds, shrugs off drought, and stays green on its own. Feed it at the wrong ones — or not at all — and you'll pay for it in weeds and reseeding. Here's the schedule, by grass type, month by month.

🗓️ Typically 2–4 feedings a year ⏱️ 30–60 min per feeding 💸 DIY runs $50–100/yr 🍂 For northern lawns, fall is the big one

Why it matters

An underfed lawn doesn't just look tired — it gets thin, and thin turf is how every expensive lawn problem starts. Here's what regular feeding prevents (typical U.S. cost ranges):

Weeds move in

Weed seeds need open ground and light to sprout. Thin, hungry turf hands them both — a dense, fed lawn is the best weed control there is.

$100 – $400/yr weed control

Pale, washed-out color

That yellow-green look is usually a nitrogen shortage, not a watering problem. No amount of extra sprinkler time fixes an empty tank.

Curb appeal, for free

Shallow roots, brown summers

Well-fed grass — especially fall-fed northern grass — banks energy in deep roots. Starved lawns root shallow and go brown at the first dry week.

Extra watering all summer

Slow recovery from wear

Kids, dogs, the mail path, last weekend's party — a fed lawn regrows over wear in days. A hungry one keeps the bald spots until you reseed.

$50 – $150 per reseeding

Moss & bare patches

Moss isn't an invader that beats healthy grass — it's what grows where grass gave up. Left long enough, the fix is a full renovation, not a bag of fertilizer.

$500 – $2,000 renovation

What's your feeding schedule?

"Every 3 months during the growing season" is the rule of thumb, but the right months depend entirely on what kind of grass you have — northern and southern lawns feed on nearly opposite schedules. Three questions:

1. What kind of grass do you have?
Hint: northern U.S. lawns are usually cool-season; southern lawns are usually warm-season. The transition zone in between can be either.
2. How much lawn do you want to be doing?
3. Do you bag or mulch your clippings?

    The cost of skipping it

    Fertilizer is the cheap part of lawn care — the expensive part is what fills the vacuum when you stop. All figures are typical U.S. estimates:

    $300 – $600/yr
    Lawn service program — 5–7 visits with fertilizer and weed control included. Zero thinking required.
    $500 – $2,000
    Renovating a weed-infested lawn — kill-off, regrade, reseed or sod — plus $100–400/yr of weed control on the way down.

And unlike most upkeep, this one's failure mode is slow: by the time the lawn looks bad, you're two seasons behind.

    How to fertilize your lawn

    A feeding takes 30–60 minutes on an average lawn, and most of that is walking. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ More is NOT better — this is the one rule

    • Over-applying burns the lawn — too much nitrogen pulls moisture out of the grass and leaves brown streaks that take weeks to recover. When in doubt, apply less.
    • Whatever lands on pavement washes into storm drains and waterways, where it feeds algae blooms instead of grass. Sweep granules off driveways and sidewalks back onto the lawn — every time.
    • Water it in afterward (or time it before a light rain) so granules reach the soil instead of sitting on the blades.
    • Check local fertilizer ordinances and blackout dates — several states restrict phosphorus or ban winter application entirely.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Broadcast spreader Lawn fertilizer (right one for the season) Gloves Hose or sprinkler — or a rain forecast Broom (for the pavement sweep)

    📐 Three techniques worth knowing

    Read the three numbers

    N-P-K, by percent weight. A 24-0-6 bag is 24% nitrogen — the number that does most of the visible work. Zero phosphorus is normal (and often required by law) unless a soil test says otherwise.

    Perimeter first, then stripes

    One pass around the edge outlines the lawn (and lets you shut off near beds and pavement). Then walk steady stripes, overlapping wheel tracks slightly so there are no missed lanes.

    Water it in — lightly

    About a quarter inch of water rinses granules off the blades (where they burn) into the soil (where they work). A light rain in the forecast does the job for free; a downpour washes it all away.

    When to bring in a pro instead

    • Your soil pH is way off. If a soil test shows pH below ~5.5 or above ~7.5, fertilizer can't do its job — the lawn needs lime or sulfur applied at calculated rates first, which is worth professional help.
    • Grub damage. Turf that peels back like carpet, plus skunks or birds tearing it up, means grubs — a diagnosis-and-treatment problem, not a feeding problem.
    • Full renovation. If more than half the lawn is weeds or bare, kill-off and reseeding/sodding is a project with real equipment and real timing. Pros do it in a week; DIY mistakes cost a season.
    • You just don't want to think about it. That's a legitimate answer — lawn programs ($300–600/yr typical) exist exactly for this, and a good one includes the soil testing and weed control too.

    Common questions

    Organic or synthetic fertilizer?

    Both feed the lawn; they just work differently. Synthetics are cheaper, faster, and precise — and easier to burn with. Organics (compost-based, corn gluten, etc.) release slowly, feed soil life, and are nearly impossible to over-apply, but they cost more per pound of nitrogen and the green-up is gradual. A perfectly good middle path: synthetic slow-release for the main feedings, and mulched clippings doing the organic work year-round.

    How soon after seeding can I fertilize?

    At seeding time, use a starter fertilizer (higher phosphorus, if your state allows it) — it's made for exactly this. After that, wait until the new grass has been mowed three or four times before a regular feeding, and skip weed-and-feed products entirely for the first season — the herbicide that kills weed seedlings doesn't know the difference between them and your grass seedlings.

    Should I just use a fertilizer + weed killer combo?

    Sparingly. "Weed and feed" puts herbicide on the entire lawn whether weeds are there or not, and the timing that's right for feeding is often wrong for the herbicide. You'll get better results — and use far less chemical — by feeding on schedule and spot-spraying the actual weeds. The exception: a crabgrass pre-emergent combo in early spring can make sense for cool-season lawns with a crabgrass history.

    When is it safe for kids and pets to go back on the lawn?

    For plain granular fertilizer: water it in, let the grass dry, and you're fine — typically 24 hours, or whatever the label says (the label wins). Combos with herbicide or insecticide need more caution: keep everyone off until it's been watered in and fully dried, usually 24–48 hours. Sweep paws-level surfaces like patios, since stray granules are the real ingestion risk.

    Why does everyone say fall is the most important feeding for northern lawns?

    Because of what the grass does with it. In September, cool-season grass is past summer stress and growing hard, but day length is dropping — so it sends the nitrogen into roots, energy reserves, and new shoots instead of into blades you have to mow. That stored energy is what powers spring green-up. A heavy spring feeding gives you the opposite: lush tops, lazy roots, and a worse summer. If you only fertilize once a year, make it September.
