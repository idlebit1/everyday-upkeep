The Spring Yard Cleanup

After a long winter the yard looks rough — matted leaves, dead grass, flopped-over perennials, and beds full of debris. Spring cleanup is the reset that wakes it all back up: clear the winter mess, gently rake the lawn, cut back what died down, edge and mulch the beds, take the first high mow, and walk the yard for damage. Get it right and the whole growing season starts clean. The one rule: don't rush it — wait until the soil has dried and the hard frosts have passed.

🗓️ Once a year, the big spring reset 🌱 Wait until the soil dries & frost passes ⏱️ A weekend or two, by yard size 💸 DIY runs about $20–60

Why it matters

Spring cleanup isn't about looks — it's the one chore that decides how the whole growing season goes. Skip it or rush it and you spend the next five months playing catch-up. Here's what a proper spring reset prevents (typical U.S. cost ranges):

Winter debris smothers the lawn

Matted leaves and dead grass left from fall pack into a wet, light-blocking layer — exactly what gives you bare patches, gray snow mold, and a happy home for pests as the lawn tries to green up.

$50 – $150 per reseeding

It lets bulbs and beds breathe

Clearing debris and cutting back last year's growth lets bulbs and perennials push up into the light — and gives you the season's first real look at what survived the winter and what didn't.

A faster, fuller spring

Early weeding beats a summer of catch-up

Edge, weed, and refresh mulch now and you cut weeds off before they seed. Wait until they're up and you'll fight them all season — a couple of spring hours saves a dozen summer ones.

Months of weeding saved

Spring is when winter damage shows

Storm-broken limbs, frost-heaved plants, vole runs in the lawn, washed-out mulch, winter-clogged gutters, and drainage problems all surface now. A walk-around catches them early — before a cracked limb or eroding bed gets worse.

$150 – $500+ if ignored

It sets up the whole growing season

Spring cleanup is the first domino. A clean, raked, assessed lawn is ready to feed and mow; cleared, mulched beds are ready to plant. Skip it and everything after lands on a yard that's still half asleep.

A head start, free

But rushing onto wet soil does harm

Go in too early and you undo the good: walking and raking soggy soil compacts it, tears tender new grass, and clearing too soon exposes overwintering pollinators and crowns to a late frost. Timing is half the job.

Compaction is costly

What's your spring plan?

It's a once-a-year push, but when it should happen and how many sessions it takes depends on your climate, your trees, and how much lawn versus garden you're working. Four quick questions — and the calendar below shifts to match:

1. What's your climate like?
This sets your window. The colder your zone, the later you start — you're waiting on the soil to dry and the hard frosts to finish, not on a date.
2. How big and how treed is your yard?
A big, leaf-littered lot takes more than one session; a small open yard can often be a single Saturday.
3. What shape is the lawn in?
A thatchy, mossy, matted lawn needs a real rake-out — and probably a dethatch, aerate, or overseed. A healthy one just needs a light spring grooming.
4. How much garden are you prepping?

    How to do a spring cleanup

    Most yards are a weekend; a big treed lot is two. Work through the checklist in order — assess first, then clear, then beds, then the lawn finish. Your progress saves automatically, so you can split it across sessions.

    ⚠️ Read this before you set foot on the lawn

    • Don't rake or work soggy soil — wait for it to dry. Working wet soil compacts it and tears the new grass right out by the roots. Use the squeeze test: if a handful forms a muddy ball, stay off it.
    • Service and safely start the cold mower and trimmer. After a winter in the shed they need fresh fuel (or a charge), clean air, and a sharp blade — a dull blade tears grass and a clogged machine is a fire and kickback risk. Start it on a flat, clear surface.
    • Wear gloves and lift with your legs. Beds hide thorns, broken glass, and surprises; loaded yard-waste bags get heavy fast — fill them loosely and squat to lift.
    • Spring is tick season. You're in tall, matted growth and leaf litter — wear long sleeves and pants, treat clothing if you like, and do a tick check afterward.
    • Wear eye protection for trimming and chipping. Cutting back ornamental grasses and running a chipper or trimmer flings sharp bits and grit straight at your face.
    • Use a stable ladder for any limb pruning — and never overreach. Keep three points of contact, plant the feet on firm ground, and stay well clear of power lines. If a broken limb is high or near wires, that's a pro's call.

    📐 Five techniques worth knowing

    Wait for the soil & frost — the squeeze test

    Before you set foot on the lawn, squeeze a handful of soil. A sticky, muddy ball means it's too wet — working it now compacts the soil and tears new grass. Wait until it crumbles, and until the hard frosts have passed. Patience here protects the whole season.

    Gently rake out matted leaves & thatch

    Rake off the matted leaves, dead grass, and built-up thatch so light and air reach the crowns — but go easy. Scalping down into the green crowns sets the lawn back. A light spring grooming is enough for most lawns; save the hard dethatching for a real thatch layer.

    Cut back perennials & ornamental grasses

    Shear last year's dead ornamental-grass clumps and spent perennial stems down to a few inches before the new growth gets tall enough to cut into. Pull dead annuals, clear the bed of debris, and you've made room for everything pushing up underneath.

    Edge, weed & refresh the mulch

    Cut a crisp edge, pull the early weeds, then top the beds with 2–3 inches of fresh mulch (don't pile it on stems). Spring is also pre-emergent timing — if you use one, lay it down before weeds sprout, and skip it where you plan to sow seed.

    First mow, set high — then assess

    Take the season's first mow with the deck set high (never scalp a waking lawn), then walk it and flag the trouble: bare, thin, or compacted patches that want overseeding or aerating. Spring is a prime window to fix them while you're already out there.

    Signs something's off — and when to call a pro

    • A lawn matted and grey with snow mold. Flattened, webby, crusty circles when the snow melts mean leaves or grass sat wet under the snow all winter. Gently rake the patches open to dry them; if the grass under them is dead, plan to overseed the bare spots once the soil warms.
    • Heavy storm-downed limbs. A cracked or hanging limb — especially one that's big, high, or anywhere near power lines — is a pro's job with the right gear. Don't take a chainsaw up a ladder.
    • Big bare or compacted areas. Wide thin, mossy, or rock-hard patches usually need more than a rake: a core aeration, a real dethatching, and an overseed. A rental machine or a one-time service pays for itself versus a failing lawn.
    • Drainage or grading problems. Standing water, soggy low spots, washed-out mulch, or erosion channels are a sign the grade is working against you — a landscaper's call before it undermines beds or the foundation.
    • A back that's had enough. Raking, cutting back, and hauling bags is real work after a sedentary winter. If a big yard leaves you sore for days, splitting it across weekends — or hiring the heavy clearing — is the smart move.

    Common questions

    When should I start my spring cleanup?

    Not on a date — on conditions. Wait until the soil has dried enough to crumble (the squeeze test: a muddy ball means wait) and the hard frosts have passed. That's usually a few weeks after the snow goes, earlier down south and later up north. There's also the "wait for the bees" debate: leaf litter and hollow stems shelter overwintering pollinators, so many gardeners hold off on the deep bed cleanup until daytime temps are reliably in the 50s. A fair compromise is to do the lawn and the safety items early, and leave bed debris a little longer.

    Can I rake the lawn when it's still wet?

    No — that's the single biggest spring mistake. Walking and raking soggy soil compacts it, squeezing out the air roots need, and a vigorous rake on saturated ground tears the tender new grass right out by the roots. Wait until the lawn is dry enough to walk without leaving footprints and the soil crumbles in your hand. A day or two of patience saves you a season of thin, struggling turf.

    Do I need to dethatch or aerate every spring?

    No. Most lawns just need a light spring raking to pull off the winter mat — that's not the same as dethatching. You only need to dethatch when the thatch layer (the spongy brown stuff between green and soil) is more than about half an inch thick. And core aeration is for compacted soil and is usually better timed for the growing season — cool-season lawns often prefer fall. So: rake every spring, dethatch or aerate only when the lawn actually shows it needs it.

    When do I cut back perennials and ornamental grasses?

    In early spring, before the new growth gets tall enough that you'd cut into it. Shear ornamental grasses down to a few inches and cut spent perennial stems back to the emerging basal growth. Many gardeners deliberately leave them standing through winter for the birds and pollinators, then cut back in spring — so spring is the natural time. If new green is already pushing up, cut carefully and high so you don't nick it.

    When's the first mow — and how short should it be?

    Once the grass is actively growing and dry enough to walk on, take the first mow with the deck set high — never scalp a lawn that's just waking up. Cutting too short stresses the roots and lets in weeds. Stick to the one-third rule (never remove more than a third of the blade in one pass), and make sure the blade is sharp after the winter so it cuts cleanly instead of tearing.

    What do I do with all the debris — compost or bag?

    Both, depending on what it is. Disease-free leaves, dead grass, and soft trimmings are excellent compost or, shredded, free bed mulch — keep them. Bag woody prunings and anything diseased for municipal yard-waste pickup rather than composting it (you don't want to spread problems back into the garden). Shred bulky material first so it packs smaller and breaks down faster, and check your town's yard-waste pickup dates and bag rules before you fill a dozen bags.
