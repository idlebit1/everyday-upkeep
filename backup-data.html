Back Up Phone & Laptop

Every photo, document, and message you care about lives on hardware that will eventually fail, get lost, or get locked up. The fix isn't discipline — it's automation. Set up backups once, then spend ten minutes a month verifying they actually work.

🛠️ Setup: 1–2 hours, once 🗓️ Then 10 min/month to verify 💵 Costs ~$70–120/yr 💸 Recovery without one: $300–2,000+

Why it matters

Data loss isn't a freak accident — it's the predictable end state of every device you own. These are the ways it happens, and what getting your files back costs when you have no backup (typical U.S. figures):

Drive failure

Every drive dies — the only question is when. Spinning hard drives often fail within 3–5 years; SSDs fail too, usually with zero warning and no clicking-noise grace period.

100% of drives, eventually

Phone loss & theft

Phones get left in taxis, dropped in lakes, and stolen off café tables. If your photos only exist on the phone, they leave with it.

No backup = gone for good

Ransomware

Malware that encrypts everything and demands payment. A backup that's connected to the computer gets encrypted too — which is why one copy stays offline.

Ransoms run $500 – $2,000+

Accidental deletion

The wrong folder emptied, an overzealous "free up space" tap, a sync conflict that picks the wrong version. Human error deletes more files than hardware does.

The #1 cause of data loss

The irreplaceables

A crashed laptop is annoying. Losing the only photos of your kids growing up, your wedding, or someone who's gone — that's the loss people never stop regretting.

No price. No do-over.

Recovery is a gamble

Professional data recovery from a dead drive typically runs $300–$2,000+ — and even then there's no guarantee they'll get anything back. A backup makes the whole industry irrelevant to you.

$300 – $2,000+, no guarantee

What's your backup gap?

The gold standard is the 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies of your data, on 2 different kinds of media, with 1 copy offsite. Answer three questions and we'll show you exactly where you stand — and what to fix first:

1. Do your phone photos sync to the cloud automatically (iCloud Photos, Google Photos)?
2. Does your computer back up automatically (Time Machine, File History, or a cloud backup service)?
3. Do you have an offline copy — a drive that stays disconnected from your machine?

    How to set it up — once

    This is a "set it up once, verify monthly" task. The setup phase takes an hour or two on a weekend. After that, your recurring job isn't backing up — it's spending ten minutes a month confirming the machines are still doing it for you. Progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ The one rule that matters: backups you have to remember don't happen

    • Automate everything. Any plan that depends on you remembering to plug in a drive "every so often" fails within two months. Humans are the unreliable component — design around us.
    • Once it's automated, your monthly job changes: you're not backing up, you're verifying.
    • And verify means restore: a backup you've never restored a file from is a hope, not a backup. Plenty of people discover their backup was silently broken for a year — at the worst possible moment.

    📐 Three ideas worth understanding

    The 3-2-1 rule

    3 copies of anything you care about (the original counts as one), on 2 different kinds of media, with 1 copy somewhere else — so no single disaster can take them all.

    Automated layers

    Layer 1: devices sync to the cloud continuously, on their own. Layer 2: once a month you add a copy to a drive that lives unplugged — your ransomware-proof copy.

    The monthly verify loop

    Check that each backup ran recently, restore one random file to prove it works, refresh the offline drive, unplug it. That loop is the whole monthly job.

    When to get professional help

    • A drive that's clicking, grinding, or not spinning up: stop powering it on. Every additional power-up can grind the platters and destroy what's left. If the data matters, send it to a data-recovery lab as-is.
    • Data recovery services (DriveSavers, Ontrack, and local labs) handle dead drives, water damage, and corrupted media. Typical cost: $300–$2,000+ depending on damage — and success is never guaranteed, so treat it as a last resort, not a plan.
    • A deleted file you need right now: stop using that drive immediately (new writes overwrite deleted data) and have a tech run recovery software — or restore from your backup, which is the entire point of this page.
    • Business data, or a home NAS/server setup: worth an IT pro's hour. Multi-drive arrays, versioned offsite replication, and tested restore procedures are a different league from personal backups.
    • Ransomware on a work machine: disconnect it from the network and call IT or a security professional before touching anything else.

    Common questions

    Isn't cloud sync (iCloud, Google Drive, Dropbox) already a backup?

    Only partially. Sync's job is to make every device match — so when a file is deleted or corrupted on one device, that change faithfully propagates everywhere, including the cloud. Most services keep deleted files for only 30–60 days. Sync protects you from a lost device; it does not protect you from deletion, ransomware, or your own mistakes. That's what the true backup layers (computer backup + offline drive) are for.

    Why does one copy need to be offline?

    Ransomware encrypts everything the infected computer can reach — including connected external drives and mounted cloud folders. A drive that lives in a drawer, unplugged, simply isn't reachable. It's the one copy malware can't touch, which is why the monthly routine is "connect, update, disconnect" rather than leaving it plugged in.

    How long do hard drives and SSDs actually last?

    Spinning hard drives typically last 3–5 years, with failure rates climbing steeply after that; some die in year one. SSDs often last 5–10 years but fail differently — usually all at once, with no warning clicks. Phones and laptops also get lost, dropped, and stolen on their own schedule. Plan as if any single device could be gone tomorrow, because that's the actual deal.

    Do I need to back up apps, music, and movies?

    No. Anything you can re-download — apps, streaming libraries, purchased media, the operating system — isn't worth backing up. Focus on the irreplaceables: photos and videos you took, documents you wrote, tax and financial records, message history, and anything created rather than purchased. That's usually a much smaller (and cheaper) set than your whole drive.

    I pay for iCloud / Google One — am I done?

    You're in better shape than most, but you have one company holding your only backup copy. Accounts get locked, hacked, and mistakenly flagged more often than people think. Adding the offline drive — one purchase, ten minutes a month — means no single company, password, or piece of hardware can take everything. That's the whole 3-2-1 idea.
