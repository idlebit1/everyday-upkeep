How Often to Dry Clean a Suit

Here's the counterintuitive part almost nobody's told: dry-cleaning your suit less is what makes it last. The solvents and the hot press that make a suit look crisp for a day are quietly hard on wool — they break down the fibers, dull the color, and flatten the fabric a little more each time — so a suit run through the cleaner after every wear wears out years ahead of schedule. The right rhythm is to clean it only every several wears, at the end of a season, or when it's genuinely stained or smelly, and to keep it sharp in between by airing it out, brushing it down, spot-cleaning small marks, and steaming out the wrinkles. Dress shirts are the exact opposite — they touch your skin and soak up sweat and oil, so they're laundered after every single wear. Learn the two rhythms and one good suit lasts a decade.

Why it matters

Most people treat a suit like a shirt — worn, then straight to the cleaner — and it's exactly backwards. A suit is a durable garment that dislikes frequent cleaning, kept fresh mostly by air and a brush; a shirt is a launder-every-time garment. Get the two rhythms right and one good suit outlasts three that were over-cleaned. Here's what's really going on:

Over-cleaning wears a suit out

The dry-cleaning solvents and the high-heat pressing that make a suit look sharp for a day are hard on wool: each pass breaks the fibers down a little, fades the color, and flattens the fabric's spring. Clean it after every wear and you're aging the suit years ahead of schedule.

A dull, worn suit years early

But it does need cleaning sometimes

Cleaning less isn't cleaning never. Sweat, body oils, and food spots left in the wool oxidize into permanent stains and — crucially — they're what moths come to eat. Airing and brushing keep a suit fresh for a while, but every several wears (or end of season) it needs a real clean.

Set stains & moth holes

Between-wear care does most of it

The everyday work of keeping a suit sharp doesn't involve solvents at all. Brushing lifts off dust and lint, airing it out for a day lets odors dissipate, spot-cleaning tackles small marks, and a steamer relaxes wrinkles and freshens the fabric — no cleaner required, and no wear on the wool.

Skip it and it needs cleaning sooner

Shirts are the opposite

The dress shirt under the jacket is the one part of the outfit against your skin all day, soaking up sweat and body oils. That's why shirts are laundered after every single wear — treating a shirt like the suit leaves it stained and smelly, and treating the suit like a shirt wears it out.

How often should you clean it?

There's no cleaning "on the calendar" for a suit — the honest unit is wears. A suit worn daily in a hot, sweaty environment needs cleaning far sooner than one pulled out for the occasional wedding. And if you're really asking about a dress shirt, the answer flips entirely. Four quick questions sort it out:

1. What are we talking about — the suit, or a dress shirt?
These are cleaned on opposite schedules: a suit sparingly, every several wears; a dress shirt after every single wear because it sits against your skin and collects sweat and oils.
2. How often do you wear a suit?
"Every several wears" arrives on the calendar much faster if you're in a suit every day — daily wearers reach a real clean in a month or so, while an occasional-wear suit can go many months between cleanings.
3. What conditions does it live in?
Heat and sweat, smoke, and grime load a suit far faster than an air-conditioned office where it barely gets warm — the harder the conditions, the sooner it needs cleaning.
4. What do you do between wears?
Airing, brushing, and steaming between wears do most of the freshening a suit needs, so they stretch the interval between real cleanings — skip them and the suit hits "genuinely needs cleaning" much sooner.

    How to care for a suit — the right rhythm

    The whole method is two rhythms: clean the suit rarely and deliberately (every several wears, at season's end, or when actually stained or smelly), and freshen it constantly in between with air, a brush, and steam. Read the technique cards first — especially "clean by wears" and "clean the jacket and trousers together" — then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Protect the wool — clean rarely, care constantly, store clean

    • Don't dry-clean a suit after every wear. The solvents and hot pressing wear out and dull wool, so clean it only every several wears, at the end of a season, or when it's genuinely stained or smelly — not on a schedule and not out of habit.
    • Clean the jacket and trousers together, always. Cleaning only one piece fades it a shade differently from the other, and before long they no longer match. Even if only the trousers look tired, both halves go to the cleaner at the same time.
    • Point out every stain to the cleaner — and never iron over one. Heat sets stains permanently, so identify each mark so it's pre-treated correctly. Don't run a hot iron directly over a stain, and skip aggressive home stain-removal on wool, which can leave a bald or shiny spot.
    • Hang it on a broad wooden or shaped hanger. Wire hangers dig in and distort the shoulders. Give the suit a proper hanger, and let it air out for a day before you re-wear it so moisture and odor dissipate.
    • Brush, spot-clean, and steam between wears. A clothes brush lifts dust and lint; spot-clean small marks promptly; and a garment steamer refreshes and de-wrinkles without solvents. If you must iron wool, use a pressing cloth and low heat — never a hot iron straight on the fabric.
    • Rotate your suits — don't wear the same one two days running. Wool needs a day or more to rest, recover its shape, and dry out from the previous wear. Rotating suits (or at least giving one a full day off) dramatically extends its life.
    • Store off-season suits clean, breathing, with cedar. Moths feast on dirty wool, so a suit going into storage must be cleaned first, then kept in a breathable garment bag with cedar — never sealed in plastic, which traps moisture and can yellow the fabric.
    • Launder dress shirts after each wear. Shirts sit against your skin and collect sweat and oils. Wash them every wear — but go easy on heavy starch and hot pressing, which wear shirts out over time too.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Clean by wears, not by habit

    Dry-clean a suit only every several wears, at the end of a season, or when it's genuinely stained or smelly — and send the jacket and trousers together every time so they age at the same rate and keep matching.

    Air & rest the wool

    Hang the suit on a broad wooden or shaped hanger and let it air out for a day before re-wearing — and rotate between suits so the wool has time to rest, dry out, and spring back into shape rather than being worn two days running.

    Brush & spot-clean

    Run a clothes brush over the suit in downward strokes to lift off dust and lint, and treat small marks promptly with a gentle wool spot-cleaner. Point out any stain to the cleaner rather than fighting it at home — and never iron over a stain, which sets it for good.

    Steam to refresh

    A garment steamer relaxes wrinkles and freshens the fabric between cleanings with no solvents and no wear on the wool. It's the daily fix for a slightly creased or stale-smelling suit — and if you must iron, keep it low and use a pressing cloth to protect the surface.

    Store right & shirts separately

    Off-season, store the suit clean in a breathable garment bag with cedar — never sealed plastic — so moths and moisture stay out. Meanwhile launder dress shirts after each wear, and log every cleaning so you know exactly where the suit is in its rhythm.

    Signs it's time to clean — and when to call a pro

    A well-kept suit hides its rhythm: it looks fresh, hangs clean, and smells of nothing in particular. These are the signals it's genuinely due for a clean — and the problems that are a tailor's or a cleaner's job, not something to handle at home:

    • A visible stain, or an odor that won't air out. If brushing and a day on the hanger don't lift the smell, or there's a mark you can see, it's time for a real clean — take it in and point out every stain so it's pre-treated before it sets.
    • Wrinkles a steamer can't relax. A suit that stays creased and tired-looking after steaming, especially at the end of a season, has earned a proper press and clean. A good cleaner can press it far crisper than you can at home.
    • It's headed into storage. Any suit going away for the off-season must be cleaned first — moths feast on dirty wool, and a suit stored with sweat and oils in it is an invitation. Clean, then bag it breathable with cedar.
    • Tears, a broken zipper, a loose lining, or a lost button. These aren't cleaning jobs — they're a tailor's. Trousers with a split seam or a jacket with a failing zipper should go to a tailor, who can also handle alterations if the fit has drifted.
    • The fit isn't quite right anymore. Taking in the waist, shortening sleeves, or nipping the jacket is tailor work that transforms how a suit wears. A clean-and-press pairs perfectly with a quick alteration.
    • Delicate or expensive fabrics. For fine wool, cashmere blends, silk, linen, or an expensive suit, trust a quality dry cleaner over a bargain shop — the right cleaner uses gentler processes and knows how to press without crushing the fabric.
    • Pro tip: a good dry cleaner can also do minor repairs and pressing in the same visit, so it's often worth building a relationship with one you trust rather than chasing the cheapest coupon each time.

    Common questions

    How often should I dry-clean a suit?

    Far less often than most people think — dry-clean a suit only every several wears, at the end of a season, or when it's genuinely stained or smelly, not after every wear. The exact interval is driven by wears, not the calendar: a suit worn daily in a hot, sweaty environment might need cleaning roughly monthly, one worn a couple of times a week every couple of months, and an occasional or special-occasion suit only a few times a year (or just once, at season's end). Airing, brushing, spot-cleaning, and steaming between wears keep it fresh in the meantime and let you stretch the interval considerably.

    Does dry-cleaning too often damage a suit?

    Yes — this is the crucial, counterintuitive point. The chemical solvents used in dry cleaning, together with the high-heat pressing that gives a suit its crisp finish, are genuinely hard on wool. Each cleaning breaks the fibers down a little, strips natural oils, fades the color, and flattens the fabric's spring and texture. A suit run through the cleaner after every wear will look dull, thin, and shiny in the high-wear spots years before its time, whereas the same suit cleaned only when it needs it and cared for in between can last a decade or more. Cleaning less isn't laziness — it's how you make the suit last.

    How do I keep a suit fresh between cleanings?

    With three no-solvent habits. First, brush it — a clothes brush in downward strokes lifts off dust, lint, and surface grime after each wear. Second, air it — hang it on a broad wooden hanger and let it breathe for a day before you re-wear it, which lets moisture and odor dissipate; rotating between suits so the wool rests helps enormously here. Third, steam it — a garment steamer relaxes wrinkles and freshens the fabric without the wear that ironing or dry-cleaning causes. Spot-clean small marks promptly with a gentle wool cleaner, and never iron over a stain. Do these and you'll be genuinely surprised how long a suit goes between real cleanings.

    Should I clean the jacket and trousers together?

    Always, yes. Dry-cleaning fades fabric very slightly each time, and if you clean the two pieces on different schedules they'll fade at different rates — before long the jacket and trousers no longer match, which is the classic tell of a suit that's been cared for carelessly. Even if only the trousers look tired (they usually take more wear), send both halves to the cleaner at the same time so they age as one garment. The same logic applies to a three-piece suit: clean the waistcoat with the rest.

    How often should I wash dress shirts?

    After every single wear — the opposite rhythm from the suit. A dress shirt sits directly against your skin all day and absorbs sweat and body oils, especially at the collar and cuffs. Left in, those oils oxidize into permanent yellow stains and start to smell, so shirts get laundered every time you wear them. One caveat that mirrors the suit lesson: go easy on heavy starch and very hot pressing, which crisp a shirt up but wear the fibers (particularly the collar folds) out faster over time. Wash after each wear, but don't over-process.

    How should I store a suit?

    Clean, hung, and breathing. Always clean a suit before storing it for the off-season, because moths are drawn to the sweat, oils, and food traces in dirty wool. Hang it on a broad wooden or shaped hanger so the shoulders keep their form, and enclose it in a breathable cotton or canvas garment bag with cedar blocks or a cedar sachet to deter moths. The one thing to avoid is sealing it in plastic — a dry-cleaner's plastic bag or an airtight container traps moisture, which can yellow the fabric and, ironically, create the humid conditions moths and mildew like. Breathable in, plastic off.

    People ruin good suits by cleaning them too often.

    The fix is genuinely counterintuitive: air it, brush it, and steam it between real cleanings, and only send it to the cleaner every several wears or at season's end. That's easy to get wrong when nothing reminds you where the suit is in its rhythm.
