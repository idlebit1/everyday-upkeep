Replace Your Spark Plugs

A spark plug fires a tiny arc of lightning into the cylinder thousands of times a minute, and that spark is what turns fuel into motion. Over tens of thousands of miles the electrodes wear down and the spark gets weak — so the engine starts to stumble, idle rough, and drink more fuel. Fresh plugs are a cheap part with an outsized payoff: a smooth start and a crisp, efficient-running engine, restored.

🗓️ Typically every 60,000 mi ⏱️ 30–90 min DIY 💵 $15–100 in plugs, DIY 💸 Shops charge $100–400+

Why it matters

Every time a cylinder fires, a spark plug has to jump a clean, hot spark across a precise gap. As the electrodes erode, that gap widens and the spark weakens — so combustion gets unreliable. That's where rough idle, hesitation, hard starts, and the dreaded check-engine light come from. Here's what worn plugs are quietly costing you:

Misfires & rough idle

A weak spark can fail to light the mixture, so a cylinder skips — the engine shudders at idle, stumbles under load, and you feel a tell-tale shake. Persistent misfires also dump raw fuel into the exhaust, which can cook an expensive catalytic converter.

Shaky · skipping cylinders

Worse fuel economy & power

Incomplete combustion wastes fuel and saps power — you press harder for the same result, and your MPG quietly slides. Tired plugs are a common, cheap-to-fix culprit behind a car that just feels gutless and thirsty.

Lost MPG · soft throttle

Hard starts

Cold mornings ask the most of your ignition. Worn plugs that fire fine when warm can leave you cranking and cranking on a cold start — and an engine that's slow to catch is one symptom away from not catching at all.

Slow, labored cranking

Cheap part, big payoff

For the price of a set of plugs and an hour of your time, a tired engine starts smoothly, idles steadily, pulls cleanly, and sips fuel like it should. Few maintenance jobs return this much drivability for this little money.

Smooth start · crisp pull

How often should you replace them?

There's no single number — the interval depends almost entirely on the type of plug in your engine: copper ~30,000 miles, platinum ~60,000, iridium ~100,000+. Symptoms can move that up. Answer three questions for your personal schedule — and always confirm against your owner's manual:

1. What kind of spark plugs does your engine use?
2. About how many miles since the last change?
3. Noticing any warning signs?

    What it costs — DIY vs. the shop

    Spark plugs themselves are cheap; what varies wildly is access. On an easy four-cylinder it's an hour in the driveway; on a V6 with plugs buried under the intake manifold, it's why shops can charge a few hundred dollars. Here's the math (typical U.S. figures, for planning purposes):

    How to replace your spark plugs

    On an accessible engine the rhythm is simple and repeats per cylinder: coil off, blow out the well, unscrew the old plug, gap-check and grease the new one, hand-thread it, torque it, coil back on. Read the techniques and the safety notes first — especially "engine fully cool" and "one cylinder at a time" — then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Safety & the rules that save your engine

    • The engine must be fully cool. Pulling a plug from a hot aluminum head can strip the threads — a very expensive repair. Let it sit several hours, ideally overnight, before you start.
    • Disconnect the battery's negative terminal as a safe extra step before you unplug coils, so nothing fires while you work.
    • Work one cylinder at a time — remove, replace, and reconnect a single plug before moving to the next. That makes it impossible to mix up the coil/wire firing order.
    • Never overtighten. Hand-thread the plug first, then torque to the manufacturer's spec with a torque wrench. Cranking on it cracks the insulator or strips the head.
    • Anti-seize: only if the plug maker allows it, and sparingly. Many modern plugs come with a special coating and should go in dry — a dab of anti-seize on those can cause over-torquing and seizing. Check the box.
    • Keep the wells clean. Blow dirt out of each plug well before you remove the plug, so nothing falls into the open cylinder.

    📐 Five things worth knowing

    Cool down, then go one at a time

    Start on a fully cool engine so the threads don't strip. Disconnect the battery negative, then work a single cylinder start-to-finish before touching the next — that keeps every coil matched to its own plug.

    Remove the coil (or plug wire)

    Unplug the coil's electrical connector, remove its hold-down bolt, and pull the coil straight up off the plug. On older engines you'll instead pull the plug wire by its boot — never by the wire itself.

    Blow out the well, then unscrew

    Blast loose dirt out of the deep plug well first so nothing drops into the cylinder. Then run the spark-plug socket and extension straight down and turn counterclockwise to back the old plug out.

    Gap, grease, hand-thread, torque

    Check the new plug's gap with a gauge (many come pre-gapped). Hand-thread it in first to avoid cross-threading the head, then snug and torque to spec. Smear dielectric grease inside the coil boot before it goes back on.

    Reinstall and repeat

    Press the coil (or wire) firmly back on, bolt it down, and reconnect the plug. Then move to the next cylinder and do exactly the same thing — one at a time — until every plug is fresh.

    Signs they're worn — and when to call a pro

    Worn plugs announce themselves through how the engine runs. These are the tells, plus the cases where this job is better left to a mechanic:

    • Rough idle and a shaking engine. A skipping cylinder makes the whole car shudder at a stop. It's one of the clearest signs a plug (or its coil) has given up.
    • A flashing or steady check-engine light. Misfire codes (the P0300 family) often trace back to plugs. A flashing light means an active misfire — ease off and address it soon, since it can damage the catalytic converter.
    • Hard starts, hesitation, and worse MPG. Slow cold-starts, a stumble when you accelerate, and a quiet slide in fuel economy together point at tired ignition.
    • It still misfires after new plugs. If a fresh set doesn't fix it, the cause may be the ignition coils, plug wires, a fuel-delivery problem, a vacuum leak, or low compression. That's diagnostic territory — let a pro chase it.
    • Plugs buried under the intake manifold or barely reachable. Some engines (many transverse V6s) require pulling intake plumbing to reach the rear bank. If the procedure reads like a teardown, the shop's lift and flat-rate are worth it.
    • A stripped or seized plug. If a plug won't budge, or it turns but won't bite going back in, stop. Forcing it can wreck the cylinder head's threads. A mechanic can extract it and repair the threads (a Heli-Coil) far more cheaply than a new head.

    Common questions

    How often should I replace spark plugs?

    It depends on the plug material. Copper/nickel plugs are the shortest-lived, around 30,000 miles. Platinum plugs typically last about 60,000 miles. Iridium (and double-platinum/long-life) plugs can run 100,000 miles or more. Those are general guides — your owner's manual lists the exact interval and the plug type your engine was designed for, and that always wins over a rule of thumb.

    What are the signs of bad spark plugs?

    The classics are a rough or shaky idle, engine misfires, a check-engine light (often a P0300-series misfire code), hard or slow starting, hesitation when you accelerate, and a drop in fuel economy. Any one of these can have other causes, but worn plugs are a common, cheap-to-rule-out culprit — and several of them at once strongly point at the ignition.

    Do I need to gap new spark plugs?

    Check — don't assume. Many modern plugs (especially iridium) come pre-gapped from the factory to the most common spec, and you should handle their fine electrodes as little as possible. But the right gap is set by your engine, not the plug, so confirm the plug's gap matches your manual's spec with a gap gauge before installing. If it's off, adjust gently — and never pry directly on an iridium or platinum center electrode, which is fragile.

    Should I use anti-seize on spark plugs?

    Only if the plug manufacturer says to — and then very sparingly. Many modern plugs have a special plating and are designed to be installed dry; adding anti-seize to those changes the friction and makes it easy to over-torque, which can crack the plug or strip the head. Read the box. If anti-seize is called for, use a tiny amount on the threads only (never near the electrode), and reduce the torque slightly per the maker's guidance.

    Can I replace spark plugs myself?

    On most accessible engines, yes — it's a very doable DIY job. The main special tool is a spark-plug socket (with a rubber insert that grips the plug), plus a ratchet, an extension, and a torque wrench so you don't overtighten. Work on a fully cool engine, do one cylinder at a time, hand-thread each plug to avoid cross-threading, and torque to spec. The job gets hard only when the plugs are hidden under the intake manifold — see "when to call a pro" above.

    Should I replace the ignition coils or plug wires too?

    Not automatically. Coils and wires are separate parts with their own lifespans, and they don't need replacing just because the plugs do. Replace them only if they're worn or failing — a misfire that persists after new plugs, visibly cracked wires, or a coil that tests bad. If a coil already failed, fresh plugs throughout still make sense, but you don't need to preemptively swap healthy coils. Inspect the boots while you're in there and reuse a little dielectric grease.
