Review Your Will & Estate Documents

A will isn't a "sign it once and forget it" document — and neither is the rest of an estate plan. Life keeps moving: a marriage, a divorce, a new child, the death of someone you named, a big change in what you own, a move to another state — and any one of them can quietly make your plan wrong without your lawyer or anyone else flagging it. An out-of-date plan can send your assets to the wrong people, hand a hard year to the family you meant to protect, or leave everyone in court just to make a decision for you. A calm review every couple of years — and after any major life event — keeps the whole thing current: the will, your beneficiaries, your advance directive, your power of attorney, guardianship for the kids, and where it's all stored.

Why it matters

An estate plan feels like a "done" thing once it's signed — which is exactly why it's so easy to let it quietly fall out of step with your life. But the documents only do their job if they still match who you'd choose and what you own today. Here's what a stale or missing plan actually costs the people you'd most want to protect:

No will means the state decides

Die without a valid, current will and the law of your state — through probate court — decides who gets what, on a rigid formula that may be nothing like your wishes. It's slower, costlier, and more public, and it can leave the people you actually meant to provide for out in the cold.

A court splits your estate, not you

Life events quietly break the plan

Marriage, divorce, a new child, the death of someone you named, new assets, or a move to a new state can each invalidate or misdirect parts of your plan. The classic disaster: an ex-spouse still listed on a beneficiary line years after the divorce — and the money goes straight to them.

An ex still on the beneficiary line

Beneficiary forms override your will

The named beneficiaries on your retirement accounts and life insurance pass directly to those people — they override whatever your will says. If a designation is stale, no will can fix it. These forms have to be checked and updated at the account itself, not just on paper in a drawer.

A current will can't undo them

No directive or POA means court

Without an advance directive and a power of attorney, if you're ever incapacitated your family may have to go to court — guardianship or conservatorship — just to make medical or financial decisions for you. Those documents name your trusted people in advance and keep that crisis out of a courtroom.

A guardianship case at the worst time

How often should you review it?

Every two to three years is the sensible default for most people — plus, always, after any major life event. But a complex estate, a big change since you last looked, or no documents at all push toward a sooner or first review, while a simple, steady, fully-current plan can comfortably stretch toward the longer end. Answer four quick questions:

1. How complex is your situation?
Minor children, real estate, a business, a blended family, or larger assets all add moving parts that can drift out of date — and they're exactly the situations where a careful, more frequent look pays off.
2. Any major life events since you last looked?
A major life event is the single biggest reason a plan goes wrong — it can invalidate or misdirect documents all at once. After one, review now rather than waiting for the calendar.
3. What's actually in place right now?
If you have no documents at all, the "review" is really a first plan — and it's the most urgent box on this whole page. Old or incomplete documents are worth a careful look soon, too.
4. How confident are you it's all current and findable?
A plan no one can locate is almost as much trouble as no plan at all. If you're unsure it's current — or where the originals even are — that's a strong reason to sit down with it sooner.

    How to review your will and estate documents

    The flow is straightforward: gather and locate everything, check that the people you named are still the right ones, check the beneficiaries (including the account forms that override your will), reflect any life or law changes, then update properly and tell the right people where things are. Read the techniques first — especially the "handle with care" notes below — then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Handle with care — this is general information, not legal advice

    • This guide is general information, not legal advice. Estate laws and document rules vary by state and by situation. For your actual plan — and especially for trusts, blended families, business interests, or a larger estate — consult a licensed estate attorney who can look at your real circumstances.
    • Beneficiary designations override your will. The named beneficiaries on retirement accounts, life insurance, and payable-on-death accounts pass directly to those people, no matter what the will says. Check and update them directly at each account — the will can't fix a stale form.
    • Keep the original signed documents safe. A fireproof box, a safe, or your attorney's office are all good. Make sure your executor and a trusted person know where the originals are and how to access them — a plan no one can find can fail just like no plan at all.
    • Verify your state's signing, witness, and notary rules. An improperly executed will can be ruled void. Requirements for witnesses and notarization differ by state, so confirm yours — or have an attorney handle the signing — before you rely on a document.
    • Be careful sharing documents digitally, and watch for scams. These papers hold sensitive personal and financial details. Share them only over secure channels with people you trust, and be wary of anyone pressuring you toward a particular product or "urgent" estate service.
    • Update — don't just re-sign — when life changes. A real change (a divorce, a new child, a death) usually calls for a proper amendment or a new will, not a fresh signature on the old one. Make the change with the right legal formalities so it actually holds up.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Gather & locate everything

    Pull together the will, any trust, your advance directive or living will, healthcare proxy, financial power of attorney, and a list of your accounts and assets. Then confirm the part everyone forgets: that you — and your executor — can actually find the originals.

    Check the people

    Look hard at everyone you named: the executor, the guardian for minor children, your healthcare agent, and your power of attorney. Are they each still the right person — and still willing, able, and living? People move, fall out, and pass away; the roles have to keep up.

    Check the beneficiaries

    Check who inherits — on the will and directly on the forms that override it: retirement accounts, life insurance, and payable-on-death accounts. These pass to the named person regardless of the will, so remove anyone outdated (the ex-spouse is the classic example).

    Reflect life & law changes

    Walk through what's actually changed: a marriage, a divorce, births, deaths, new property or a business, or a move to a new state (estate law is state-specific). Note any tax-law shifts too. Each of these can quietly mean your documents no longer say what you'd want.

    Update properly & communicate

    Make any changes with the right legal formalities — a codicil or a new will, properly witnessed and notarized for your state. Then store the originals safely, tell the key people where they are and how to access them, and set the date for your next review.

    Signs it's time — and when to see an estate attorney

    Some of a review you can handle yourself with an afternoon and a folder. But certain situations are genuinely legal work, where an estate attorney earns their fee — these are the signals to get professional help (and a reminder that this guide is general information, not legal advice for your specific situation):

    • A major life event. Marriage, divorce, a new child, the death of a beneficiary or executor, an inheritance, starting a business, or a move to a new state each reshapes your plan at once — review now, and bring in an attorney if the change is significant.
    • No will or directives at all. If you have nothing in place, that's the most urgent item here. Even a basic will, advance directive, and power of attorney spare your family the worst of probate and guardianship — get a plan started.
    • A blended family or minor children. Stepchildren, children from a prior relationship, or kids who'd need a named guardian make the "who gets what and who's in charge" questions genuinely complicated — exactly the situation an attorney is built for.
    • Significant or complex assets. Real estate, a business, investment accounts, or a larger estate raise tax and structuring questions that DIY tools don't handle well. Professional help here usually pays for itself in mistakes avoided.
    • Anything involving trusts or estate tax. Trusts and estate-tax planning are firmly legal-and-financial territory. Don't guess — an estate attorney (often with a CPA) should set these up and keep them current.
    • Pro tip: pair this with your annual financial review and insurance review — beneficiaries and coverage tie directly together — and keep a current home inventory for your estate records, so what you own is documented alongside who it goes to.

    Common questions

    How often should I review my will and estate documents?

    For most people, every two to three years is a sensible default — plus, always, after any major life event. Move toward a sooner review if your situation is complex (minor children, real estate, a business, a blended family, or significant assets), if something big has changed since you last looked, or if you're not even sure your documents are current or where the originals are. A simple, steady situation with a fully current plan can comfortably sit toward the longer end. The point is that an estate plan drifts out of date quietly, so a periodic check keeps it matching your actual life. This is general information, not legal advice — an estate attorney can advise on the right cadence for your situation.

    When must I update them — what life events count?

    Treat any major life event as a trigger to review right away rather than waiting for the calendar. The big ones: marriage or divorce, the birth or adoption of a child, the death of someone you named (a beneficiary, executor, guardian, or agent), a significant change in what you own — a home, a business, a large inheritance — and a move to a new state, since estate law is state-specific and documents valid in one state may need adjusting in another. Changes in your relationships or in tax law matter too. A divorce in particular is notorious for leaving an ex-spouse on documents and beneficiary forms, so it deserves a prompt, careful pass. After any of these, sit down with the plan; don't assume it updated itself.

    What documents make up an estate plan?

    A basic plan usually has a few pieces working together. A will says who gets your assets and, if you have minor children, names a guardian and an executor to carry it out. A financial power of attorney names someone to handle money and property matters if you can't. An advance directive (sometimes a living will plus a healthcare proxy or medical power of attorney) states your wishes for medical care and names someone to make health decisions if you're incapacitated. Some people also use a trust to manage how and when assets pass, often to avoid probate or handle more complex situations. Alongside the documents, keep a current list of your accounts and assets, and note where the originals are stored. Which pieces you actually need depends on your situation — an estate attorney can tell you.

    Do beneficiary designations override my will?

    Yes — and this surprises people. Accounts with a named beneficiary — retirement accounts like a 401(k) or IRA, life insurance, and payable-on-death or transfer-on-death accounts — pass directly to the person named on that form when you die, regardless of what your will says. The will simply doesn't control those assets. That's why a stale designation is so dangerous: if your will leaves everything to your current spouse but your old 401(k) still names an ex, the ex generally gets that account, and a perfectly current will can't undo it. So updating beneficiaries isn't something you do in the will at all — you have to go to each account and insurance policy directly and change the designation there. Reviewing those forms is one of the highest-value parts of the whole exercise.

    Do I need a lawyer, or can I DIY a will?

    For a genuinely simple situation — modest assets, a straightforward family, clear wishes — a reputable DIY or online will tool can produce a valid basic will for roughly $0–200, and that's far better than having nothing. But there are real cautions: the document must be executed correctly for your state (witness and notary rules vary, and an improperly signed will can be void), and DIY tools handle complexity poorly. Bring in a licensed estate attorney if you have a blended family, minor children needing a guardian, a business, significant or complex assets, or anything involving trusts or estate tax — there, professional help typically pays for itself in mistakes avoided. A simple attorney-drafted will and estate package commonly runs about $300–1,000, more for complex plans. This is general information, not legal advice; an attorney can tell you which path fits your situation.

    Where should I store the originals, and who should know?

    Keep the original signed documents somewhere safe from fire, water, and loss — a fireproof box or home safe, a safe deposit box, or with your attorney are all common choices. Be a little careful with a bank safe deposit box, since access can be complicated right after a death; some people keep the originals at home or with the attorney for that reason. Wherever they live, the part people forget is just as important: make sure your executor and at least one other trusted person know the documents exist, where the originals are, and how to access them. A plan no one can find — or can't get into when it's needed — can fail almost as badly as no plan at all. It also helps to keep a simple list of your accounts and assets with the documents, and to revisit storage whenever you update anything.
