Replace Your Mattress

You spend roughly a third of your life on it, and it wears out so slowly you adapt to the dip instead of noticing it. After about eight to ten years the foam and coils stop springing back, the support sags where you sleep, and you wake up stiff on a bed that's quietly become an allergen reservoir. The calendar is only half the answer — here's the signs-based test that tells you whether it's time, and how to pick the next one so it actually fits how you sleep.

🗓️ Typically every 7–10 years 🛏️ The sag test takes 30 seconds 💸 $500–$2,000+ for a quality bed 🌙 You spend ~1/3 of life on it

Why it matters

A mattress is the single piece of furniture your whole body rests on for a third of every day, and it fails the way every soft thing fails — slowly, where the load is heaviest, while you adjust to it instead of noticing. Here's what an expired mattress actually costs you:

Back, neck & shoulder pain

A sagging mattress stops holding your spine in line, so your hips dip and your back arches all night. The classic tell: a stiff lower back or sore shoulders in the morning that loosen up an hour after you're vertical.

Stiff, achy mornings

Years of allergens

A mattress quietly collects a decade of shed skin, sweat, and dust — and dust mites move in to feed on it. Unlike sheets, you can't wash the core. An old mattress carries an allergen load that fresh bedding can't touch.

3 a.m. congestion & flares

Springs & foam break down

Coils lose tension, foam takes a permanent set, and the comfort layers compress where your hips and shoulders press every night. Past roughly seven to ten years there's simply less material left to support you — and no rotating or topper rebuilds it.

Permanent sag & noisy coils

Quality sleep is worth it

You don't sleep through a worn-out mattress — you fight it, rolling toward the dip, shifting off the sore spot, surfacing a little each time. That's shallower sleep night after night, on the one purchase that touches a third of your life. It's worth getting right.

A third of your life, downgraded

Do you actually need a new one?

Replacing a mattress is driven by condition, not the calendar. Eight to ten years is the typical lifespan, but a well-cared-for latex bed can outlast it and a cheap one can sag in four. Forget the date for a moment and check the real signs — visible sag or body impressions deeper than about an inch, waking up stiff or sore, sleeping noticeably better in hotels, springs that poke or creak, or allergies that flare in bed. Answer four questions and we'll give you a verdict:

1. How old is the mattress?
2. How do you feel in the morning — and do you sleep better elsewhere?
3. Any visible sag, lumps, or noisy springs?
4. What type is it?

    How to assess and replace your mattress

    This is a deliberate decision, not a chore: run the test, decide honestly, then choose a replacement that fits how you sleep and protect it from night one. Check the care callout and techniques first, then work the checklist — your progress saves automatically.

    🌙 Make the next one last — and delay this whole job

    • Use a mattress protector from day one. A breathable, waterproof protector keeps sweat, spills, and allergens out of the core — it's the single biggest lifespan extender, and stains void most warranties anyway.
    • Rotate it regularly. Spinning it 180° head-to-foot every few months spreads the wear instead of denting one spot. (See our mattress rotation guide.)
    • Put it on a proper, supportive foundation. The right base for your mattress type, with slats no more than ~3 inches apart (or a solid platform), prevents the premature sag a warranty will blame on you.
    • Don't fold or sharply bend a foam mattress when moving it — the core can crack. And never machine-anything the mattress itself; only the protector and sheets get washed.
    • The label and warranty outrank this guide. Some mattresses are zoned or no-rotation; some have specific foundation requirements. Check yours.

    📐 Five steps worth knowing

    Run the age + sag + sleep check

    No single signal decides it. Weigh three together: how old it is, whether it sags or lumps, and how you feel in the morning (and on other beds). Two of the three pointing the same way is your answer.

    Press for sag, listen for springs

    Strip it bare and press your palm into the spot where you sleep. A body impression deeper than about an inch, foam that's slow to recover, or coils that poke and creak all mean the support layers are spent.

    Pick type & firmness for how you sleep

    Side sleepers want a softer surface to cushion hips and shoulders; back sleepers medium; stomach sleepers firm to keep the hips from sinking. Sleep hot? Favor innerspring, hybrid, or a cooling foam over dense memory foam.

    Buy with a trial & read the warranty

    It takes a few weeks to know if a bed suits you, so favor a generous sleep-trial and an easy return. Then read the warranty's fine print — especially the sag-depth threshold (usually 1–1.5 inches) that decides whether a future dip is covered.

    Protect & rotate from day one

    Zip a protector on before the first night — stains void warranties and the protector blocks the sweat that ages the core. Then start a rotation schedule immediately, so the new bed wears evenly from the start.

    When to see a doctor or file a claim

    A new mattress fixes a lot, but not everything — and sometimes the right move is a phone call rather than a purchase:

    • Persistent back pain on a good, newer mattress → see a doctor. If the bed is well under ten years old, isn't sagging, suits your sleep position, and you still wake up in pain (or hurt during the day too), the mattress probably isn't the culprit. Ongoing or worsening back pain — especially with numbness, tingling, or pain down a leg — is worth a doctor's visit, not another bed.
    • Significant sag while still under warranty → file a claim first. If the impression measures past your warranty's threshold (usually 1–1.5 inches under a straightedge), the mattress is stain-free, and it's been on an approved foundation, that's exactly what the warranty is for. Photograph the dip with a ruler and a yardstick laid across it, and call before you buy a replacement.
    • Allergies or asthma that flare in bed despite clean, protected bedding → talk to your doctor. A decade-old mattress can carry a real allergen load, and a new one plus a barrier protector often helps — but persistent nighttime symptoms are worth raising with a doctor or allergist, who may point to triggers a new bed won't solve.
    • You can't safely move the old one → hire haul-away. A waterlogged or king-size mattress is awkward and heavy; many retailers remove the old one free on delivery, and municipal bulky-waste or mattress-recycling programs will collect it for a modest fee. Don't wreck your back on the way to fixing your sleep.

    Common questions

    How long do mattresses last by type?

    Rough planning ranges with normal nightly use: innerspring about 6–8 years, pillow-top a bit shorter because the soft top compresses first, memory foam about 8–10, hybrid roughly 7–9, and quality latex the longest at 10–15+ years. These are guides, not guarantees — a protected, rotated budget bed can outlive a neglected premium one, and a heavier sleeper or a kid jumping on it shortens any of them. When the calendar and the condition disagree, trust the condition.

    What are the signs I need a new mattress?

    The big ones: a visible sag or a body impression deeper than about an inch that doesn't bounce back; waking up stiff or sore in a way that fades once you're up; sleeping noticeably better in hotels or other beds; springs that poke, creak, or feel uneven; allergies or asthma that flare in bed despite clean bedding; and simply being past eight years old. No single sign is decisive — but when two or three line up, it's time. Replacement is condition-driven, so let the test overrule the calendar in either direction.

    Should I flip my mattress to get more life out of it?

    Almost certainly not. Nearly every mattress made since the mid-2000s is one-sided — comfort layers on top of a support core — so flipping it puts you on the hard base while your weight crushes the comfort layers from the wrong side. What you should do instead is rotate it 180° head-to-foot every few months so it wears evenly. Flip only if the manufacturer explicitly says it's a true double-sided (two identical surfaces) model.

    How do I dispose of or recycle an old mattress?

    Best route first: when you buy a new one, ask the retailer to haul the old mattress away on delivery — it's often free or cheap. Otherwise, many areas have mattress-recycling programs (some states run them statewide) that break the bed down into steel, foam, and fiber for reuse; search your city plus "mattress recycling." Municipal bulky-waste pickup will take it for a small fee, and some charities or shelters accept a clean, undamaged mattress — but only donate if it's genuinely clean and pest-free; a stained or bed-bug-exposed mattress should never be passed on.

    Will a mattress topper buy me more time?

    Temporarily, and only for some problems. A good topper can refresh a mattress that's merely too firm or slightly worn on the surface — it adds a cushion layer for a fraction of a new bed's cost. What it can't do is fix a broken-down core: if the support layers sag, the coils have gone, or there's a real dip, a topper just drapes over the crater and you'll still roll into it. Use a topper to fine-tune comfort or stretch a few months while you shop, not to rescue a mattress the signs say is finished.

    My mattress is only a few years old but I wake up sore — is it the bed?

    Maybe, maybe not. First check the basics: is it sagging, is the foundation sound, and does the firmness actually match your sleep position (side sleepers on a too-firm bed and stomach sleepers on a too-soft one both wake up sore)? A protector and a rotation may even things out. But if a sound, well-matched, newer mattress still leaves you in pain — or you hurt during the day too — that points away from the bed and toward a conversation with a doctor.
