Clean Your Dryer Vent

Every load sheds lint, and the screen catches only part of it — the finest fibers ride the hot, damp air down the duct and pack into the bends, the blower, and the flap outside. Twice a year you pull the dryer out, brush that lint out of the line, and clear the exhaust path. It takes well under an hour, it's the single biggest thing you can do to lower your home's fire risk, and it's the difference between clothes that dry in one cycle and a dryer that runs hot, slow, and old before its time.

Why it matters

Lint is the byproduct of every dry cycle, and the screen in the door catches only the coarse part — the finest fibers ride the airflow into the duct and pack down where you can't see them. What that quiet buildup causes ranges from a higher power bill to a house fire:

It's the #1 cause of dryer fires

Lint is dry, fluffy, and astonishingly flammable, and a clogged vent traps the dryer's heat right next to it. U.S. fire departments respond to roughly 13,000–15,000 dryer fires a year — and "failure to clean" is the single leading cause.

$200M+ in property damage a year

Two cycles to dry one load

A blocked vent can't carry the moist air out, so clothes come out hot and still damp and you run the dryer a second time. You pay for that wasted cycle — in time and energy — every single load.

Wasted energy on every load

Your dryer wears out early

Restricted airflow makes the dryer run hotter than it was designed to, cooking the heating element, the thermostat, and the thermal fuse. Run it that way for years and a $500–$1,200 machine gives up early.

$150–$400 repair, or early replacement

Moisture and (on gas) CO

A vent that can't exhaust pushes humid air back into the room — hello mildew and peeling paint. On a gas dryer it can also push combustion gases, including carbon monoxide, back indoors instead of safely outside.

CO risk on gas dryers

How often should you clean it?

"Twice a year" is the sensible default for a typical household — but a long or rooftop vent, a ribbed foil duct, or a houseful of laundry all build lint faster, while a short rigid run with light use can stretch to annual. Answer four questions:

1. How long and twisty is the vent run?
Trace it from the back of the dryer to where it exits the house. Every elbow and every extra foot is another place lint settles — and the harder it is to push clean.
2. What's the duct made of?
Look behind the dryer: a smooth or ribbed metal tube is rigid/semi-rigid; thin shiny foil or white plastic that crushes in your hand is flexible — the kind that catches the most lint.
3. How much laundry runs through it?
More loads means more lint downstream. Towels, bedding, and a shedding pet's blankets shed especially heavily.
4. Gas or electric dryer?
A gas dryer burns fuel to make heat, so a blocked vent carries a carbon-monoxide risk an electric one doesn't — worth not stretching the schedule.

    How to clean your dryer vent

    The active work is maybe 30 minutes — pull the dryer out, brush the duct, clear both ends, button it back up. Read the techniques first (especially the metal-only rule and how to leave the dryer breathing room) then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Safety first — heat, gas, and very flammable lint

    • Cut the power before you reach behind it. Unplug an electric dryer. For a gas dryer, unplug it and shut off the gas valve behind it before you move or disconnect anything.
    • Gas dryers: respect the gas line. When you slide the unit out, don't kink or over-stretch the flexible gas connector. After you reconnect, check for a gas smell — if you ever smell gas, leave the house and call the utility from outside. Don't flip any switch.
    • Metal duct only — never plastic or vinyl. Flexible plastic/vinyl duct melts and feeds fire, and it's against code. Smooth rigid metal is best; ribbed semi-rigid metal is acceptable. If you find plastic back there, replacing it is the most important thing you'll do all day.
    • Never run the dryer without the lint screen in place, and never reconnect with a duct that's crushed, torn, or kinked.
    • Bag the lint as you go. It's extremely flammable — don't leave piles near the dryer, the water heater, or any pilot flame.
    • Rooftop or out-of-reach exterior vents mean ladder work. If getting to the outside hood safely isn't realistic for you, that half of the job is worth handing to a pro.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Vacuum with hose + long crevice/brush attachment Dryer vent brush kit (flexible rods + brush) Screwdriver or nut driver (for duct clamps) UL-181 metal foil tape (not cloth "duct tape") Work gloves + dust mask Flashlight A bag for the lint ~30–60 minutes

    📐 Four things worth knowing before you start

    Know your whole run

    The job has three zones: the dryer's exhaust port and screen housing, the duct in between (where lint settles at every bend), and the exterior hood outside. Clean all three — a clog can hide in any of them.

    Rigid metal beats flexible foil

    Smooth rigid metal lets lint slide straight out; ribbed foil and plastic catch it in every fold — and plastic/vinyl is a fire hazard outright. If yours is flexible, cleaning it is step one and replacing it with metal is step two.

    Brush the whole length

    A vent brush kit on flexible rods — turned by hand or chucked into a drill — reaches the middle of the run where a vacuum can't. Feed it through, vacuum the loosened lint, and work both the duct and the dryer's exhaust port.

    Test the exterior flap

    Outside, the hood's louvered flap should swing fully open on a strong, warm push of air when the dryer runs. A flap that barely lifts, or is matted with lint or a pest nest, is the clearest sign the vent is choked.

    Signs your vent is overdue — and when to call a pro

    A clean vent moves a strong stream of warm air outside in about half the time it takes to read this section. These are the signals it's clogged now — and the cases where the smart move is a professional:

    • Clothes take two cycles to dry, or come out hot and damp. The single strongest sign airflow is choked. Don't just keep re-running loads — clean the vent.
    • The dryer or the laundry room gets very hot, or there's a faint burning smell. Overheating from restricted airflow. Stop and clean before the next big load. A burning smell that persists after a thorough cleaning is an electrical fault — unplug it and call for service.
    • The exterior flap barely opens, or airflow outside is weak while it runs. The blockage is downstream of the dryer. If you can't clear it from both ends, a pro with powered equipment can.
    • Long or rooftop vent runs — over roughly 15–20 feet, several elbows, or an exit through the roof — are where DIY brushes struggle to reach the middle, and where ladder or roof work is a real safety call. A good case for the pros.
    • You find a bird or rodent nest, or the duct is crushed or torn inside the wall. That's a repair, not just a cleaning, and sometimes a job for someone who can open up the run.
    • Gas dryer plus a CO alarm that's sounded, or you suspect it isn't venting. Treat it as urgent: ventilate, get everyone out if anyone feels unwell, and have it checked before running the dryer again.
    • Pro tip: if your duct is flexible foil/plastic or you've never traced the full path, a one-time professional clean-and-inspect maps the run — and it's the natural moment to upgrade to rigid metal so future cleanings are easier and rarer.

    Common questions

    How is this different from cleaning the lint screen?

    They're two different jobs on two different schedules. The lint screen in the door should be cleared after every load — it catches the coarse lint. But even a spotless screen lets the finest fibers through, and over months those pack into the duct, the blower housing, and the exterior hood. That deeper buildup is what causes slow drying and fires, and clearing it is the twice-a-year vent clean this guide covers. The good news: doing the screen every single load is exactly what keeps the vent clean enough that the twice-a-year job stays quick.

    How do I know my vent is clogged?

    The classic tells: clothes that need a second cycle or come out hot and damp, a dryer or laundry room that runs unusually hot, a humid or musty laundry area, a faint burning smell, longer-than-usual cycle times, or visible lint around the door seal and behind the dryer. The quickest field test is outside: run the dryer and watch the exterior flap — it should swing well open on a strong, warm push of air. If it barely lifts or the airflow feels weak, the line is choked.

    Can I skip the duct and just clean the lint trap?

    No — and this is the most common (and most dangerous) shortcut. A clean lint screen still passes plenty of fine lint, and with nothing else catching it, that lint accumulates in the duct and exterior hood month after month. The screen protects drying performance load-to-load; the vent clean protects against the buildup that actually starts fires and overheats the machine. You need both.

    My duct is flexible foil — do I have to replace it?

    Strongly recommended. Flexible plastic or vinyl duct should be replaced immediately — it's a genuine fire hazard and a code violation; never reinstall it. Flexible foil is legal in many places but its accordion ridges trap far more lint than smooth pipe, so it clogs faster and needs cleaning more often. The best upgrade is smooth rigid metal duct for the run, with at most a short length of semi-rigid metal as the flexible transition behind the dryer — kept as short and un-crushed as possible.

    Do dryer sheets and fabric softener matter here?

    They do, in a sneaky way. Dryer sheets leave a thin waxy film on the lint screen that you can't see but that restricts airflow — which is why it's worth scrubbing the screen with soap, water, and a soft brush every month or two (rinse and dry it fully before it goes back). That film, plus the extra lint softeners can loosen, contributes to buildup downstream too. None of it changes the twice-a-year vent schedule, but a clean, film-free screen keeps the whole system breathing.

    How long is too long for a vent run?

    Dryer manuals set a maximum equivalent length — commonly around 25–35 feet of straight duct, with every elbow counting as several feet (often ~5 ft per 90° bend). A run near or over that limit clogs faster, dries more slowly, and may need a booster fan to vent properly. If yours is long, bendy, or you're not sure it's within spec, have a pro measure and assess it — fixing an undersized or over-long run does more than any cleaning can.

    Should I just hire a professional?

    For most homes, a DIY clean twice a year with a $15–$30 brush kit handles it completely. A pro makes sense when the run is long or exits through the roof, when you suspect a blockage you can't clear from either end, or for a first deep clean on a vent that's never been done. Professional cleaning typically runs $100–$200. Even if you hire out the occasional deep clean, cleaning the lint screen every load and eyeballing the exterior flap now and then stays your job.
