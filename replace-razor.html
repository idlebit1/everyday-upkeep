How Often to Replace Your Razor Blade

Here's the thing almost nobody tells you: that razor burn, those little nicks, the way the blade tugs instead of glides, the new crop of ingrown hairs — that's not "sensitive skin." It's a dull blade. A fresh edge slices each hair cleanly in one light pass; a worn one drags, skips, and saws, irritating your skin and leaving you to make extra passes that irritate it more. And blades dull in two ways: from cutting hair, yes, but also from sitting wet in the shower between shaves, where water quietly corrodes that fine edge and grows bacteria on it. So how long a blade lasts is partly up to you. Swap the cartridge every five to ten shaves — the moment it tugs or leaves you irritated — and rinse and dry it between shaves, and you'll get a closer, gentler shave for less money. Find your number, and a great shave becomes a habit instead of a gamble.

Why it matters

A razor blade is one of the few tools you press against your skin and then drag, so its edge has to be genuinely sharp to do its job kindly. The trouble is it dulls so gradually that you adapt to the drag without noticing — and start blaming your skin for what the blade is doing. Here's what's actually going on, and why a fresh edge and dry storage matter so much:

A dull blade drags instead of slices

A worn edge can't cut a hair cleanly, so it tugs and saws across the skin instead. That dragging is what actually causes razor burn, nicks, and that raw, stinging feeling — the irritation people blame on their skin is usually just a blade that's past it.

Razor burn, nicks & tugging

Blades dull from cutting and from sitting wet

Coarse hair wears an edge down, but so does water. A blade left wet in the shower oxidizes and corrodes along its fine edge between shaves — which is why a razor that's rinsed and dried after each use stays sharp far longer than one parked in a soggy caddy.

Wet storage corrodes the edge

An old, damp blade harbors bacteria

Trapped hair, dead skin, and shaving cream hold water against the blade, and a warm, wet razor is a tidy little home for bacteria. Dragging that across skin you're about to nick is exactly the wrong place for it — a fresh, clean edge gives germs nowhere to ride in.

Bacteria on a fresh cut

Stretching it too far means more passes

Pushed past its life, a blade leaves uneven stubble and missed patches, so you go back over the same skin again and again to get it smooth — and every extra pass is another round of irritation. A fresh blade clears it in one gentle, faster pass.

Missed patches & extra passes

How often should you swap it?

About every two weeks — roughly five to seven shaves out of a cartridge — is the all-purpose default, but your real number swings with how often you shave, how coarse your hair is, how much area you're covering, and how easily your skin reacts. Daily shaving, thick coarse hair, large areas like legs or a full beard, and sensitive or ingrown-prone skin all pull toward weekly (or a fresh edge every shave for safety-razor blades). Lighter, occasional shaving stretches a cartridge to a few weeks. Answer four quick questions:

1. How often do you shave?
Every shave wears the edge a little and exposes it to more water. Shaving daily uses up a blade's five-to-ten-shave life in a week or two; shaving occasionally stretches that same cartridge out over a month or more.
2. How coarse is the hair you're shaving?
Coarse, dense hair is harder on a blade — it dulls the edge faster and clogs the cartridge sooner, so thick beards and dense body hair burn through blades quicker than fine, light growth.
3. How much area are you shaving?
A blade only has so many strokes in it. Covering legs, a full beard, or a large body area in one shave racks up far more passes than a quick neckline or small touch-up, so it needs replacing sooner.
4. How does your skin react?
Sensitive or ingrown-prone skin shows the cost of a dull blade first, because a dragging edge is exactly what triggers burn and ingrowns — so swapping early, while the blade is still keen, pays off most for reactive skin.

    What it costs — and what skipping it costs

    Blades aren't free, and that's exactly why so many people stretch them too far. Brand-name cartridges run about $2–4 each, which adds up to real money over a year; disposables cost less; and double-edge (safety razor) blades are pennies — around 10 to 25 cents apiece. So the storage habit that makes a blade last twice as long isn't just about a closer shave; it's money back in your pocket. An electric shaver's foil and cutters are a once-a-year replacement. Set all of that against the daily misery of shaving with a dull blade. Pair this with the rest of your grooming routine — beard trims, haircuts, and nail trims.

    How to make a blade last — and know when to swap it

    The whole game is two habits: replace the blade the moment it tugs, skips, or irritates you, and treat it well in between so that moment comes later. Rinse it clean after every shave, store it bone-dry and blade-up out of the wet shower, and shave gently with lubricant so the sharp edge does the work. Read the techniques first (especially "store it dry" and "know the signs"), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ A sharp blade is the safe blade — handle it right

    • Replace it the moment it tugs, skips, or irritates. A dull blade causes more cuts and irritation than a sharp one, because it drags and you press harder to compensate. If a shave leaves you tugging, skipping patches, or red and stinging, the blade is done — don't push it one more shave.
    • Store it DRY, blade-up, out of the wet shower. Standing water is what dulls and corrodes the edge and grows bacteria on it. Rinse the blade thoroughly after every shave and stand it somewhere it can air-dry — on a dry shelf or a stand by the sink, not in a puddle in the shower caddy. Many people double their blade life just by drying it.
    • Never share a razor. A razor nicks skin and traps blood and bacteria, so sharing one can transmit infections and even blood-borne illness. Keep your own razor, and don't lend it out "just this once."
    • Don't dry a blade by wiping it on its edge. Running a towel or cloth against the cutting edge rolls and dulls it — the opposite of what you want. Just shake the water off and blot or pat it gently, or wipe along the blade, never into it.
    • Always shave with a lubricant. Gel, cream, or soap lets the blade glide instead of scrape. On sensitive skin or if you get ingrowns, shave with the grain (the direction the hair grows) rather than chasing a closer shave against it — that's where most irritation and ingrowns come from.
    • Rinse a fresh nick, and don't drag a clogged blade. If you nick yourself, rinse it with cool water and let it clot rather than shaving over it. And clear trapped hair and cream out of the cartridge as you go — a clogged blade skips, pulls, and holds water against the edge.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Know the signs it's done

    A blade tells you when it's finished: it tugs or skips instead of gliding, leaves a rough, irritated shave, shows visible gunk between the fins or rust on the edge, and makes you go over the same spot three times. Any one of those is your cue to swap it.

    Rinse it clean every time

    Run the cartridge under water after every stroke and again at the end of the shave, knocking out the trapped hair and cream. That gunk both makes the blade skip and holds water against the edge — clearing it keeps each pass clean and helps the blade last.

    Store it dry, out of the shower

    This is the habit that saves the most money. A blade left standing in water dulls and corrodes far faster than one that air-dries. Stand the razor blade-up on a dry shelf or stand by the sink — not in a soggy shower caddy — and it can last roughly twice as long.

    Swap the cartridge or blade

    Click the old cartridge off and a fresh one on — most handles release with a button or a pull. Loading a double-edge razor? Open the head, lay a new blade in, and handle it only by the short, non-cutting sides. Drop used DE blades in a blade-disposal box, never loose in the trash.

    Shave gently, then dry & store

    Lather up and let the sharp blade do the work — light pressure, short strokes, with the grain on sensitive skin. Afterward, rinse the blade, shake and blot it dry, and stand it up to air-dry. If you use an electric shaver, replace its foil and cutters about once a year to keep it shaving close.

    Signs it's time to replace it now

    A fresh, well-kept blade is almost invisible: it glides, clears the hair in one pass, and leaves your skin calm. These are the signals it's gone past its window and is doing more harm than good — swap it the moment you notice any of them:

    • It tugs, skips, or pulls hair instead of cutting. The clearest sign of all. A sharp blade slices cleanly; once it starts dragging, sawing, or yanking hairs, the edge is done and every stroke is irritating your skin.
    • Razor burn, more nicks, or new ingrown hairs. If a shave that used to be smooth now leaves you red and stinging, nicks you more often, or brings on ingrowns, suspect the blade before your skin — a dull edge is the usual culprit.
    • Visible rust, gunk, or a bent fin. Look at the cartridge: orange rust specks on the edge, packed hair and cream you can't rinse out, or a bent or splayed guard fin all mean it's past saving. Replace it rather than dragging it across your face.
    • A shave that needs three passes. When you have to go over the same patch again and again to get it smooth, the blade isn't cutting efficiently anymore — and each extra pass is extra irritation. A fresh edge clears it in one.
    • For an electric shaver, a worn or torn foil. If the foil looks worn shiny, has a tear or hole, or simply isn't shaving as close as it used to, it's time for new foils and cutters — about a once-a-year job for most electric shavers.
    • Pro tip: the single best upgrade most people can make isn't a fancier razor — it's swapping the blade sooner and storing it dry. Keep a small stock of fresh cartridges or blades on hand so a dull one is never an excuse, and let the price-per-shave of cheap double-edge blades free you from rationing.

    Common questions

    How often should I change my razor blade?

    The honest answer is by feel, not by the calendar — swap it the moment it starts to tug, skip, or leave you irritated, which for most people lands somewhere around every 5–10 shaves. In weeks, that works out to roughly weekly if you shave daily or shave coarse hair or a large area, every two weeks for the average shaver, and every three to four weeks (or more) if you shave occasionally or have fine, light hair. Coarse hair, big areas like legs or a full beard, and sensitive or ingrown-prone skin all push you to the shorter end. The two things that move that number most are how much you shave and how you store the blade between shaves — a blade that's rinsed and dried lasts far longer than one left wet.

    How many shaves does a blade last?

    A typical cartridge gives a good shave for about five to ten shaves before the edge starts to drag — call it 5–7 for most people, fewer for thick coarse hair or large areas, more for fine hair and small touch-ups. Disposables are usually good for a handful of shaves before you toss the whole razor. Double-edge (safety razor) blades are cheap enough that many people change them every few shaves, or even every shave, without thinking about cost. The number isn't fixed, though: the same blade lasts noticeably longer if you rinse it clean and store it dry, and burns out faster if it sits wet in the shower. When in doubt, trust the shave — a blade that's still gliding and cutting cleanly is still good; one that's tugging is done.

    How do I make my razor blades last longer?

    Dry it. The single biggest thing you can do is keep the blade out of standing water between shaves — water is what dulls and corrodes that fine edge and grows bacteria on it, and people routinely double their blade life just by drying it. So rinse the cartridge thoroughly after every shave to clear out trapped hair and cream, shake the water off, blot or pat it gently dry (never wipe against the cutting edge — that rolls it), and stand the razor blade-up somewhere it can air-dry, like a stand or shelf by the sink rather than the wet shower caddy. Some people swear by a final rinse in rubbing alcohol or a quick dunk in mineral oil to displace water; the essentials, though, are clean and dry. Shaving with plenty of lather and light pressure also spares the edge, since you're not forcing a clogged or scraping blade across your skin.

    Why does my razor irritate my skin or cause ingrown hairs?

    Nine times out of ten, it's a dull blade — not "sensitive skin." A worn edge can't slice a hair cleanly, so it drags, tugs, and saws across the skin, and you instinctively press harder and make more passes, all of which inflame the skin and cause razor burn. Dull blades also tend to cut hairs at a ragged angle or below the skin's surface, which is exactly what leads to ingrown hairs. The fixes stack up: swap to a fresh, sharp blade; always shave with a lubricating gel, cream, or soap; use light pressure and let the blade do the work; and on sensitive or ingrown-prone skin, shave with the grain rather than chasing a closer shave against it. Keep the blade clean and dry too, since a bacteria-laden old blade can turn a tiny nick into a breakout.

    Cartridge vs. safety (double-edge) razor — how often for each?

    It comes down to cost per blade. A cartridge costs $2–4, so you naturally want to get its full 5–10 shaves before swapping — replace it when it starts to tug, and store it dry to stretch that out. A double-edge (safety razor) blade costs pennies — around 10 to 25 cents — so there's no reason to ration it; many people load a fresh blade every few shaves, and some change it every single shave for a consistently keen edge. A safety razor has a learning curve (one blade, a fixed angle, lighter pressure), but it gives a very close, low-irritation shave once you've got it, and it's far cheaper over time. Disposables sit in between — cheap, but you throw the whole razor when the blade's done. Whichever you use, the rule is the same: a sharp blade is the safe, comfortable one.

    How do I store my razor (and why not in the shower)?

    Store it dry, blade-up, and out of the wet shower. The shower is the worst place for a razor precisely because it's the wettest: standing water and constant humidity sit on the edge, oxidizing and corroding it, and the warm damp grows bacteria in the trapped hair and cream. That's what dulls a blade long before its cutting has worn it out. Instead, after you shave, rinse the cartridge thoroughly, shake off the water, and stand the razor up so the head can drain and air-dry — on a dry shelf, a counter stand, or a hook outside the shower spray. Keeping it blade-up (rather than resting the head down in a puddle) helps it dry and protects the edge. Do that and a single blade routinely lasts about twice as long as one left sitting wet — a closer shave, fewer blades, and less money, all from where you set it down.
