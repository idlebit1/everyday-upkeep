Replace Your Electric Razor Head or Foil

Here's what almost nobody tells you about an electric shaver: the cutting parts wear out, and when they do, it's not the machine that feels tired — it's your face. The thin metal foils and the little cutter blocks underneath them (or the three spinning heads on a rotary shaver) start sharp and dull with every shave, so slowly that you never catch the moment it happens. One day the shaver that used to leave you smooth is tugging at hairs, missing patches, and leaving your neck red and stinging — and you blame your skin, or the shaver, when the fix is a $30 part. A worn foil can even thin out until it tears and nicks you. Replace the head about every 12 to 18 months — sooner if you shave daily or have coarse hair, and right away the moment the shave turns tugging or irritating — and keep it clean and oiled in between, and that close, comfortable shave comes right back.

Why it matters

An electric shaver has just a few working parts that touch your skin, and they're the ones that wear out: the thin metal foils and the cutter blocks that oscillate beneath them, or the round cutting heads on a rotary. They start out sharp and thin the metal a little with every shave — so gradually that you never notice the day it stopped being good. Here's what a worn head actually does, and why a fresh one is worth the swap:

Cutting parts dull with use

Every shave wears the foils a little thinner and takes a little edge off the cutters, so over months the shaver stops slicing hairs cleanly and starts to drag across them instead. It's not the motor giving out — it's the parts up top that do the actual cutting quietly wearing down.

Foils thin, cutters go dull

A worn head means a bad shave

Once the head is past it, the shave changes: hairs pull and tug instead of cutting, the result isn't as close, your skin comes up red and irritated, and you go over the same patch again and again. Every extra pass just adds more irritation on skin the dull head already annoyed.

Tugging, redness & extra passes

A torn foil is a safety issue

A foil is only a whisper of metal, and as it wears it can thin right through into a hole or a tear. A holed foil no longer protects your skin from the cutter racing underneath it — so it can nick and cut you. A worn or torn foil isn't something to shave over; replace it promptly.

A holed foil nicks skin

A fresh head restores the shave

Swap the worn parts for a new set and the shaver comes back to life — close, quick, and comfortable again, the way it was when it was new. And it saves you from the losing move: pressing harder to make a dull shaver cut closer only causes more razor burn, not a better shave.

Pressing harder just burns

How often should you replace it?

About every 12 to 18 months is the sensible default for a foil or rotary head — but that number swings with how hard you use it. Shaving daily and having coarse, dense hair both wear the cutting parts down faster, pulling you toward the 12-month end; lighter, less frequent shaving stretches a set closer to 18. The one rule that overrides the calendar: if the shave has started tugging, stopped being close, or left you irritated, the head is done now, whatever the date says. Answer four quick questions:

1. How often do you shave with it?
Every shave wears the foils and cutters a little. Shaving daily works through a head's life a good deal faster than shaving twice a week, so heavy users land nearer the 12-month end of the range.
2. How coarse is the hair you're shaving?
Coarse, dense hair is harder on the cutting parts — it wears the edge down faster and works the foils harder — so a thick beard burns through a head sooner than fine, light growth.
3. How's the shave right now?
This is the tiebreaker. A shave that tugs, isn't as close as it used to be, or leaves you red is the head telling you it's finished — replace it now, whatever the calendar says.
4. What kind of shaver is it?
Both wear out on a similar schedule; the difference is what you buy. A foil shaver takes a matched foil-and-cutter set, while a rotary takes a matched set of all three heads.

    How to replace your electric razor head or foil

    The whole job is short: know when to do it (by the shave, not just the date), buy the exact right part for your model, pop the old head off, seat the new parts as a matched set, then test and maintain it. The two things people get wrong are mixing old and new parts and buying the wrong-model foil — so read the techniques first (especially "replace them together" and "match the model"), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Replace the parts together, and mind the foil

    • Replace the foil AND the cutter TOGETHER on a foil shaver. They wear as a matched pair, so a new foil over old cutters (or vice versa) shaves poorly and wears out fast. On a rotary, replace all three heads as a matched set — never just one.
    • A thin, worn, or TORN foil can nick or cut your skin. The foil is the only thing between the racing cutter and your face. If it looks worn shiny, has a hole, or has torn, replace it promptly — don't keep shaving over it.
    • Don't press HARDER to make a dull shaver cut closer. Bearing down on a worn head doesn't get you a closer shave — it just crushes hairs and causes razor burn and irritation. If you're pressing to compensate, the head needs replacing, not more force.
    • Use the correct OEM (or reputable compatible) part for your exact model. Foils and heads are model-specific; the wrong one won't seat right, won't oscillate properly, and can damage the shaver. Match the exact model number printed on the shaver or in the manual.
    • Clean after each use and lubricate regularly. Rinse or brush out the trapped hair and skin after every shave, and lubricate the head regularly with shaver spray or oil (or run the cleaning station) — a clean, oiled head cuts better and lasts noticeably longer.
    • Power off, unplug, and let it dry before opening the head. Switch the shaver off and unplug it (or make sure it can't run) before you take the head apart, and let a wet-shave model dry so you're not fumbling slippery parts over a hard sink.
    • Recycle the old parts and any battery properly. Old foils and heads are small metal; a dead rechargeable battery in an old shaver should go to e-waste or battery recycling, not the household trash.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Judge by the shave, not the date

    The head tells you when it's done: it tugs at hairs, no longer shaves as close, leaves your skin red, makes you go over the same spot several times, or the foil is visibly worn or torn. Any one of those means replace it now — a rough date of 12–18 months is only the backstop.

    Buy the right part for your model

    Find the exact model number on the shaver or in the manual, and buy the part it calls for — a matched foil-and-cutter set for a foil shaver, or the three-head set for a rotary. Genuine OEM is safest; a reputable compatible can work, but a random wrong-model foil won't seat right.

    Remove the old head

    Press the release buttons or catch and lift the head or foil frame straight off — most pop off with a click. On a foil shaver, lift the old cutters out of their carriers underneath too; they're a set with the foil. Note how everything sits so the new parts go back the same way.

    Fit the new parts together

    Drop the new cutters into their carriers first, then click the new foil frame on — as a matched set, never new foil over old cutters. Make sure everything seats fully and faces the right way (foils and heads have a correct orientation). On a rotary, snap in all three new heads.

    Test & maintain

    Run the shaver and take a shave — it should feel close and smooth again. Then keep it that way: brush or rinse out the hair after each use, lubricate the head regularly with spray or oil, and log the date. A clean, oiled head cuts better and makes the next set last longer.

    Signs it's time to replace it now

    A fresh head is almost invisible: the shaver glides, clears the stubble in a pass or two, and leaves your skin calm. These are the signals the head has gone past its window — swap the foil-and-cutter set (or the rotary heads) the moment you notice any of them, and the one case where the head isn't the problem at all:

    • It tugs or pulls hairs instead of cutting them. The clearest sign of all. A good head slices; once it starts dragging, sawing, or yanking hairs, the foils and cutters are worn — and every stroke is irritating your skin.
    • The shave is no longer as close. If you're left with stubble you can feel just after shaving, or you can't get the smoothness you used to, the cutting parts have dulled. Pressing harder won't fix it — a new head will.
    • Redness or irritation after shaving. A worn head drags and crushes hairs rather than cutting them cleanly, and that's what leaves your neck and face red and stinging. If a shave that used to be comfortable now burns, suspect the head.
    • You need several passes to get it smooth. Going over the same patch three and four times means the head isn't cutting efficiently anymore — and each extra pass is more friction on already-irritated skin. A fresh head clears it in one or two.
    • A torn or visibly worn foil. Look at the foil in the light: if it's worn shiny thin, has a hole, or has torn, replace it immediately — a holed foil no longer protects your skin from the cutter and can nick you. Don't shave over it.
    • If a brand-new head still shaves poorly, the shaver may be done. When you've fitted a fresh, correct head and the shave is still weak, the motor or battery is likely worn — it's losing power. That's the point where a new shaver, not another head, is the right call.
    • Pro tip: the single best upgrade for most electric shavers isn't a new machine — it's a fresh head plus a real cleaning habit. Clean out the hair after each use and lubricate the head regularly (a cleaning station does both), and keep a spare set on hand so a worn head is never an excuse to suffer through a bad shave.

    Common questions

    How often should I replace my electric razor head or foil?

    The rough guide is about every 12 to 18 months for both foil and rotary shavers, but treat that as a backstop rather than a rule — the real signal is how the shave feels. Shaving daily and having coarse, dense hair both wear the cutting parts down faster, pulling you toward the 12-month end; lighter, occasional shaving with fine hair stretches a set closer to 18. Whatever the calendar says, though, if the shaver has started tugging at hairs, stopped shaving as close, or begun leaving your skin red and irritated, the head is done — replace it then. And keeping the head clean and lubricated between swaps genuinely extends how long each set lasts, so good maintenance can push you toward the longer end of that window.

    Do I have to replace the foil and cutter together?

    Yes — always replace them as a matched set. On a foil shaver, the foil and the cutter block underneath it wear together and are designed to work as a pair: the cutter oscillates against the inside of the foil, and after a year of shaving they've worn into each other. Put a new foil over old, dull cutters and the shave stays poor and the fresh foil wears out fast; put new cutters under an old, thin foil and you're one shave away from tearing it. Buy the combined foil-and-cutter replacement for your model and swap both at once. The same logic applies to a rotary shaver: replace all three cutting heads as a matched set, not one at a time, so they stay evenly matched and shave consistently.

    How do I know it's actually time — the shave or the calendar?

    The shave, first and foremost. The 12-to-18-month figure is only a rough backstop for people who never notice a slow decline; the head itself tells you clearly when it's finished. Watch for hairs that tug or pull instead of cutting, a shave that just isn't as close as it used to be, redness or irritation afterward on skin that used to be fine, needing three or four passes to get smooth, or a foil that looks worn thin or has torn. Any one of those means replace it now, whatever the date. The catch is that the change is gradual — you adapt to the drag and start blaming your skin — so if you can't remember when you last swapped the head and the shave feels like work, it's almost certainly overdue.

    Foil vs. rotary — what exactly needs replacing?

    They wear out on a similar schedule; what differs is the part you buy. A foil shaver has one or more thin, perforated metal foils with a cutter block oscillating just beneath each — you replace those as a combined foil-and-cutter set for your model. A rotary shaver has three round cutting heads, each a spinning cutter behind a slotted guard — you replace all three as a matched head set. In both cases the rule is the same: buy the exact part for your model number and swap the whole matched set together, never mixing a new piece with worn ones. Foil replacements are often a little cheaper and simpler to fit; rotary sets seat as three units. Either way it's a few-minute job with parts in the $25–50 range.

    Can I use a cheaper compatible head instead of the OEM part?

    Sometimes, with a bit of care. Genuine OEM (the shaver maker's own) foils and heads are made to the shaver's exact tolerances, so they seat perfectly, oscillate correctly, and give the shave the machine was designed for — the safe default. A reputable compatible replacement, from a well-reviewed third-party maker that clearly lists your model, can save money and often works fine. What you want to avoid is a cheap, unbranded foil that "looks about right": if it doesn't match your exact model it may not seat properly, may fit loosely, and can shave badly or even damage the shaver. Whichever you choose, match the exact model number, buy from a seller with real reviews, and if a compatible part shaves worse or seats loosely, switch back to OEM — this is a part that touches your face, so it's worth getting right.

    How do I make the head last longer?

    Clean it and lubricate it. Hair, dead skin, and shaving debris clog up between the foil and cutter (or in the rotary heads), which makes the shaver drag, run hot, and cut worse — so clean it after every use: brush the dry cuttings out, or rinse a washable head under water, and let it dry. Then lubricate the head regularly, every week or two, with a purpose-made shaver spray or a drop of shaver oil on the foil while it runs — that keeps the metal-on-metal cutting smooth and slows the wear. If your shaver came with a cleaning station, use it; it cleans and oils in one go. A clean, well-oiled head cuts closer, stays cooler on your skin, and reaches the longer end of that 12-to-18-month window instead of the shorter one — the same "look after it and it lasts" logic as storing a wet razor blade dry.
