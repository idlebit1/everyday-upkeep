Clean Your Patio Furniture

It sneaks up over a season: a green bloom of mildew creeping up a chair leg, a dusting of pollen, a sticky smear of tree sap, and that one cushion that smells faintly of a wet basement no matter how long it sits in the sun. The fix isn't a once-a-year scrub-and-replace — it's a quick wash a few times a season, matched to what the furniture's actually made of. Plastic shrugs off soapy water; aluminum needs drying so it won't pit; wood and wicker want a gentle hand and no pressure washer; and the cushions, which trap moisture and grow mold, need to dry fully on edge before anything stacks on top. Do it right and a $300 set looks new for years instead of one neglected summer turning it to junk.

🗓️ Typically every 3 months ⏱️ ~20–40 min a wipe-down 🪑 Method depends on material 💸 Costs ~$0–15

Why it matters

Outdoor furniture lives in the worst conditions of anything you own — sun, rain, pollen, sap dripping from the trees above it, and birds overhead — and it's the one set of furniture nobody thinks to clean until it already looks rough. A few minutes a few times a season keeps it ahead of all of it. Here's what the buildup does:

Mildew, pollen, sap & bird mess stain

Mildew, pollen, tree sap, and bird droppings build up and stain — and mildew doesn't stay put. It spreads from the frame onto the cushions, so a green bloom you ignore on a chair leg in June is sitting on the seat fabric by August.

Stains that set in & spread

Metal rusts, wood & wicker rot

Leave grime and trapped moisture sitting on a set and metal rusts, while wood and wicker rot or grey out. A film of dirt holds water against the surface — exactly what corrosion and rot need to get started.

Rust, rot & a greyed-out finish

Cushions hold moisture & grow mold

Dirty cushions soak up rain and dew and hold it, and damp foam grows mold from the inside out. It's a hygiene-and-smell problem you literally sit on — that musty wet-basement smell is mold you can't see yet.

Musty, moldy cushions

Clean & cover beats replacing it

A quick regular clean and a cover for the off-season beats replacing a faded, rusted, rotten set. Fifteen dollars of soap and a furniture cover is the cheapest insurance there is against a whole new patio set.

Replacing a $300–1,500 set

How often should you clean it?

A wipe-down every three months in season plus a deep clean when you open and close the patio is the sensible default. But furniture out in the open under trees in a humid climate needs monthly attention, while a sheltered set in dry air can stretch much longer. Answer four quick questions:

1. What's most of it made of?
Plastic and metal shrug off a hose-and-soap clean, so they tolerate going a bit longer. Wood, wicker, and especially cushions trap moisture and grow mildew, so they reward staying ahead of it.
2. Where does it sit?
Open sky means rain, sun, and bird mess; trees add sap and pollen; salt air corrodes metal fast. A covered or screened spot keeps the worst of all of it off the furniture.
3. What's the climate like?
Mildew and mold need moisture, so humid and rainy climates grow it fast — and a heavy pollen season coats everything yellow. Dry air is forgiving: less mildew, slower buildup.
4. How much do you use it?
A set you actually use gets food, drinks, and sunscreen on it and shows dirt sooner — but it also gets noticed and wiped more often. A set that just sits collects grime quietly until you finally look.

    How to clean your patio furniture

    The whole trick is matching the method to the material: plastic and metal take a real scrub, wood and wicker want a gentle hand and no pressure washer, and cushions need to dry fully on edge before anything stacks on top. Read the techniques first (especially "skip the pressure washer on wood and cushions" and "let everything dry fully"), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Work smart — cleaners, pressure, and drying

    • Test any cleaner on a hidden spot first. Dab it under a chair or on the back of a cushion and check before you do the whole piece, so a reaction or fading shows up somewhere it won't matter.
    • Go easy on bleach — and never mix it with ammonia. Diluted bleach can kill mildew on plastic, but keep it off bare wood and most fabrics (it weakens fibers and fades color). Never combine bleach with ammonia or other cleaners — the fumes are dangerous. Oxygen bleach is the safer choice for most jobs.
    • Be very careful with pressure washers — or skip them. They gouge soft wood, strip finishes, and blast water deep into wicker and cushion foam where it can't dry and grows mold. Use the lowest pressure and a wide tip, keep your distance, or just use a hose and brush instead.
    • Wear gloves and a mask for heavy mildew. Scrubbing moldy cushions or a mildewed frame kicks spores into the air. Gloves and a simple dust mask keep them off your skin and out of your lungs.
    • Let everything dry FULLY before covering or stacking. Trapped moisture is exactly what grows mold — so stand cushions on edge, leave frames in the sun, and don't cover, stack, or store anything until it's bone dry inside and out.
    • Use a stable ladder for umbrellas and pergolas. Reaching up to clean a canopy or shade structure from a chair or a wobbly stool is how an easy chore turns into a fall — use a proper step ladder on level ground.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Plastic & resin: soap and a soft brush

    Warm soapy water and a soft brush handle almost everything on plastic and resin. For set-in stains or mildew, step up to oxygen bleach (not chlorine, which can yellow or weaken it), rinse well, and let it dry.

    Metal & aluminum: dry it, stop the rust

    Wash with soapy water and — crucially — dry it so it can't rust. Spot-treat any rust with a wire brush or remover, then touch up bare spots with paint or a coat of paste wax so corrosion doesn't come straight back.

    Wood & teak: gentle, with the grain

    Scrub gently with the grain using mild soap — never a pressure washer, which gouges soft wood and raises the grain. Rinse, let it dry, and oil or seal as needed; for the same idea on decks, see resealing the deck or fence.

    Wicker & rattan: vacuum, then wipe

    Vacuum dust out of the weave first, then a damp wipe and a soft brush to get into the gaps where grime hides. Don't soak it — water trapped in the weave grows mildew — so wipe lightly and dry it quickly in the sun.

    Cushions & fabric: dry on edge

    Brush off debris, scrub with mild soap or hose them down, then stand them on edge to drip-dry fully — laid flat, the inside stays wet and molds. Check the tags first: many have removable, machine-washable covers.

    Signs it's beyond a cleaning — and when to replace or call a pro

    Most patio furniture just needs a wash on a schedule. But some damage is past the point a brush and soap can fix — these are the signs a piece needs refinishing, new cushions, or replacing outright:

    • A frame rusted through or structurally rotten. Surface rust cleans up; a leg that's rusted thin, a weld that's let go, or wood that's gone soft and punky is a safety problem — that chair can fail under you, and it's time to replace or have it repaired.
    • Mildew set deep into the cushions. If the mold is inside the foam and the musty smell won't wash out, the cushion is done — replace the covers and inserts. A moldy cushion you keep sitting on is a hygiene problem, not a stain.
    • A faded or cracked finish that needs refinishing. Powder-coated metal that's chalky and peeling, or painted wood that's cracking and flaking, needs stripping and refinishing for a real fix — a job worth doing once properly rather than re-cleaning a failing finish every season.
    • Teak or hardwood gone silvery grey. Grey is just weathered surface, not damage — but restoring it to honey-gold means a teak cleaner and brightener, then sanding and re-oiling. It's doable yourself, or a furniture-restoration pro can bring a good set back to new.
    • Cushion covers torn or zippers shot. Once the outdoor fabric is shredding or the seams are blown, cleaning won't save them — replacement covers (or having them re-sewn) are cheaper than a whole new set.
    • Pro tip: the cheapest upkeep of all is a cover and a dry storage spot for the off-season. Pair this clean with pressure washing the deck or patio underneath it and cleaning the grill, and you knock out the whole outdoor space in one afternoon.

    Common questions

    How do I clean patio furniture by material?

    Match the method to what it's made of. Plastic and resin take the most abuse — warm soapy water and a soft brush, with oxygen bleach (not chlorine) for set-in stains, then rinse. Metal and aluminum get washed and then dried so they don't rust; spot-treat any rust and touch up bare spots with paint or paste wax. Wood and teak want a gentle scrub with the grain, mild soap, no pressure washer, and oil or seal afterward. Wicker and rattan get vacuumed first, then a damp wipe and a soft brush into the gaps, dried quickly so water doesn't sit in the weave. Cushions and fabric get debris brushed off, a scrub or hose-down, then stand on edge to dry fully. The common thread: gentle cleaning, rinse, and — above all — dry everything completely before covering or stacking.

    How do I get mildew off outdoor cushions?

    First, take them somewhere with airflow and brush off the loose mildew (wear a mask — you're stirring up spores). Mix a mild solution: a squirt of dish soap in warm water, or a cup of white vinegar per gallon, or a fabric-safe oxygen-bleach cleaner. Scrub it into the fabric with a soft brush, let it sit a few minutes, then rinse thoroughly with a hose. Check the tags — many cushions have removable, machine-washable covers, which makes this far easier. The non-negotiable last step is drying: stand the cushion on its edge in the sun so both sides and the inside can dry completely. Laid flat, the underside stays damp and the mildew comes right back. If the mold is deep in the foam and the musty smell won't leave, the cushion is past saving — replace it.

    Can I pressure-wash patio furniture?

    Carefully, and not on everything. A pressure washer is genuinely risky on patio furniture: it gouges soft wood and raises the grain, strips paint and finishes off metal and wood, and blasts water deep into wicker weave and cushion foam where it can't dry and grows mold. On tough plastic or metal a pressure washer can speed things up if you use the lowest pressure, a wide fan tip, and keep your distance. On wood, teak, wicker, and cushions, skip it — a hose, soap, and a soft brush do a safer job. Honestly, for most furniture you don't need one at all; save the pressure washer for the deck and patio surface and clean the furniture by hand.

    How do I remove rust from metal furniture?

    Scrub the rust off with a wire brush, steel wool, or fine sandpaper until you're back to clean metal — a commercial rust remover or a paste of baking soda and water (or white vinegar for light rust) helps loosen it. Wipe it clean and let it dry fully. The step people skip is sealing it back up: bare metal will just rust again, so touch up the spot with a rust-inhibiting primer and matching paint, or for a quick protective layer, a coat of automotive or paste wax. Going forward, the best rust prevention is simple — wash the furniture, dry it so water doesn't sit on it, and cover or store it for the off-season, especially in salt air, which corrodes metal fast.

    How often should I clean it?

    A good default is a quick wipe-down every three months while it's in use, plus a thorough deep clean when you open the patio in spring and close it in fall. From there, adjust to your situation: furniture out in the open, under trees that drop sap and pollen, or in a humid, rainy climate needs more frequent attention — closer to monthly — because mildew grows fast and the buildup never lets up. A set on a covered, shaded, or screened porch in dry air can stretch much longer between cleans. Cushions are the exception that always rewards attention: wipe spills right away, and never let a damp cushion sit, because that's where mold starts. Take the quiz above for a schedule matched to your material, exposure, climate, and use.

    Should I cover or store furniture for winter — and what's the best cleaner for outdoor fabric?

    Yes — covering or storing for the off-season is the single biggest thing that keeps a patio set looking new, and it's cheap: covers run about $20–60, or store pieces in a shed or garage if you have the room. The one rule is to clean and fully dry everything first, because a cover over a damp chair or stacked moist cushions traps moisture and grows mold — the opposite of what you want. Bring cushions indoors or into a dry bin for winter rather than leaving them out. As for the best cleaner for outdoor fabric: a mild dish-soap-and-water solution or a cup of white vinegar per gallon handles routine dirt and mildew, and there are inexpensive purpose-made outdoor-fabric cleaners (and oxygen-bleach products) for tougher stains. Always test on a hidden spot first, rinse well, and dry on edge.
