Wash Your Bedding

You spend a third of your life pressed against these fabrics, shedding skin cells, sweat, and oils the whole time. Sheets need a weekly wash — but the rest of the stack (covers, pillows, duvets, protectors) runs on longer cycles most people never learn. Here's the whole schedule, and how to do it right.

🗓️ Sheets every week ⏱️ ~15 min hands-on per load 🌡️ 130°F water kills dust mites 💸 Protects an $800–$2,000 mattress

Why it matters

A bed warms to body temperature every night and collects moisture, skin cells, and oils — exactly what microscopic life loves. Washing on schedule is less about looking tidy and more about what builds up when you don't:

Dust mites & allergens

Mites live on the skin cells you shed and thrive in warm, humid bedding. Their droppings are a top trigger for year-round allergies and asthma — an unwashed pillow hosts thousands of them.

Worse sleep + allergy flares

Acne & skin irritation

A pillowcase reabsorbs oil, product residue, and bacteria night after night, then presses it back into your face for eight hours. Dermatologists flag dirty pillowcases as a real factor in breakouts.

Breakouts + itchy skin

Sweat & body oils degrade fabric

Adults lose up to half a liter of moisture a night. Oils and sweat left to sit oxidize into yellow stains, break down fibers and elastic, and give bedding that permanent "musty" smell no spray fixes.

Sheets die years early

Pet dander & outdoor grime

If pets share the bed, they bring dander (a major allergen), hair, and whatever they walked through that day. Every interval on the schedule shortens — and a protector stops it reaching the mattress.

Allergens + odors multiply

Shortened pillow & mattress life

Sweat and oil that soak past the pillowcase compress fill and stain mattresses — and a stained mattress usually voids its warranty. Clean covers and a protector are how a mattress reaches year ten.

$800–$2,000 replaced early

How often should you wash?

"Sheets weekly" is the baseline, but the right interval depends on allergies, who (and what) shares the bed, and how clean you are when you climb in. Answer three questions for your personalized schedule:

1. Do you have allergies, asthma, or eczema?
2. Do pets sleep on (or in) the bed?
3. Which sounds most like you at bedtime?

    How to wash your bedding

    Wash day is mostly waiting — the hands-on work is about 15 minutes per load. Work through the checklist; your progress saves automatically, so a multi-load day can span the afternoon.

    🧺 Three rules before anything touches the machine

    • Read the care label first. Most bedding is machine-washable, but memory-foam pillows never are, and some duvets are dry-clean or "professional wash" only. The label outranks this guide.
    • Pick your water temperature on purpose. Allergies or asthma? Wash sheets hot — 130°F (54°C)+ is what actually kills dust mites. Otherwise warm or cold is plenty, and it's gentler on fibers, colors, and your energy bill.
    • Don't overload the machine. Bedding cleans by moving through water. If a duvet has to be stuffed in, it just gets wet — not clean — and you can strain the motor. When in doubt, use a laundromat's large-capacity washer.

    📐 Three techniques worth knowing

    Leave room in the drum

    A duvet or pillow load should fill the drum about two-thirds, loosely. If you have to force the door shut, take it to a laundromat's big machine instead.

    Dry pillows with dryer balls

    Low heat plus two or three wool dryer balls keeps fill from clumping. Expect multiple cycles — fluff and break up clumps by hand between each.

    Run two sets in rotation

    Strip the bed, remake it immediately with the clean set, and wash the dirty one whenever it suits you. The bed is never bare, and weekly stops feeling like a chore.

    When to replace instead of wash

    • Pillows that fail the fold test. Fold the pillow in half and let go — if it doesn't spring back, the fill is dead and no wash revives it. Typical lifespan: 1–2 years for synthetic, 3+ for quality down.
    • Yellowed, lumpy duvets. If the fill stays clumped after a proper wash-and-dry, or the duvet smells musty even when clean, the insert is done — usually after 5+ years of nightly use.
    • Protectors with worn waterproofing. When the backing cracks, peels, or crinkles apart, it's no longer protecting anything. Most last about 1–2 years of regular washing — at $20–$60, replace without guilt.
    • Sheets that have gone thin. Pilling you can feel, see-through patches, or elastic that's given up. Weekly washing gives a good set roughly 2–3 years; that's the deal.
    • Anything that survived a mildew episode. A pillow or duvet that got put away damp and smells of mildew at the core is very hard to rescue — replace it and dry fully next time.

    Common questions

    How hot does the water need to be to kill dust mites?

    About 130°F (54°C). Many U.S. water heaters are set to 120°F for scald safety, so check yours — a "sanitize" cycle works too, and so does 15+ minutes in a hot dryer after a warm wash. If you can't wash hot, hot drying is your mite-killing step.

    Can I wash a down duvet at home?

    Usually, if the care label allows it and your washer is large enough — a front-loader (or any machine without a center agitator) with the duvet filling no more than two-thirds of the drum. Use a small amount of down-safe detergent, an extra rinse, and budget a few hours of low-heat drying with dryer balls. If it's a squeeze, the laundromat's commercial machine is worth the few dollars.

    How often should I wash blankets and throws?

    If a blanket is part of your nightly sleeping layers, treat it like a duvet cover: every 2–4 weeks. A decorative throw that mostly lives on the sofa can go seasonal — unless pets nap on it, in which case every week or two keeps dander and smell in check.

    How do I know a pillow or duvet is actually dry?

    Squeeze the center: any coolness, dampness, or clumping means keep drying. A pillow can feel dry outside while the core is still wet — and a damp core grows mildew within days, which ruins it. When in doubt, run one more low-heat cycle, then let it air out flat for a few hours before putting it back on the bed.

    Do I need to wash brand-new sheets before using them?

    Yes. New bedding ships with finishing chemicals (and sometimes excess dye) that can irritate skin and stop the fabric from breathing properly. One warm wash makes them softer, more absorbent, and actually yours.
