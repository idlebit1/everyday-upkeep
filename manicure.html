How Often to Get a Manicure

A manicure isn't really about the color — it's nail maintenance that happens to end in a nice finish. Every couple of weeks your nails want shaping so they don't split and snag, your cuticles want tending so hangnails don't start, and your hands want cream so the skin doesn't dry and crack. The polish is the part you see, but it's the shortest-lived: regular polish chips within a week, gel grows out in two to three, and your natural nails want looking after whether they're painted or bare. Do it on a gentle schedule — a fresh manicure roughly every two to three weeks, with a quick file-and-cuticle-oil touch-up in between — and your hands stay tidy, strong, and healthy instead of ragged and chipped by the time you get around to them.

Why it matters

"A manicure" sounds like a small vanity — a color on your nails for the week. But strip the polish away and what's left is real maintenance: nails that need the right shape, cuticles and hands that need care before they crack and snag, and nail changes worth catching early. The polish is the flourish; the upkeep is the point. Here's what's actually at stake:

Shaping keeps nails healthy

Filed to an even shape and kept a sensible length, nails resist splitting, peeling, and catching on things. Regular shaping is really just regular nail care — a smooth, snag-free edge is what keeps your nails out of trouble instead of tearing down into the quick.

Split, peeling, snagging nails

Cuticle & hand care matters

Tended cuticles and moisturized hands keep hangnails, dryness, and cracking at bay. The cuticle is a seal against infection and the skin around your nails takes a beating from washing and weather — a little oil and cream is what keeps it all soft and intact.

Hangnails & cracked, dry skin

Polish only looks good fresh

The color is the shortest-lived part of the whole thing: regular polish chips within a week, and gel grows out a visible gap in two to three. So the schedule isn't really about the manicure "wearing out" — it's about keeping it tidy before the chips and grow-out start to show.

Chipped, grown-out polish

A routine catches problems early

Sitting down with your hands regularly is what surfaces the small stuff — a nail changing color or texture, the start of a fungal infection, or thinning and damage from harsh gel removal. Caught early, most of it is nothing much; ignored, it gets worse quietly.

Damage & infection caught late

How often should you get one?

A fresh manicure roughly every two to three weeks — with a quick file and a swipe of cuticle oil in between — is the sensible default: regular polish chips in about a week, gel lasts two to three, and natural-nail upkeep wants tending about every two weeks. Hands-on work and fast-growing nails pull toward weekly touch-ups; bare, healthy nails stretch it out. Answer four quick questions:

1. What are you mostly after?
If you want the color and shine kept up, you'll go more often to stay ahead of chips and grow-out; if you mainly want strong, tidy natural nails, you can time it to the actual nail-and-cuticle upkeep instead.
2. How hard do your hands work?
Hands-on work chips polish and wears nails down fast — typing, cleaning, gardening, and dishes are all hard on a manicure. Busy hands reward keeping the interval short and doing quick touch-ups between.
3. What do you usually wear on your nails?
Regular polish chips within a week; gel and dip hold for two to three weeks and are best rebooked at grow-out; bare, buffed nails are timed purely to shaping and cuticle upkeep, so they stretch out the longest.
4. How are your nails doing right now?
Peeling or damaged nails — often from picked-off gel — do best with a break from polish to recover, timed to gentle bare-nail upkeep. Strong, healthy nails can follow whatever schedule your polish and hands call for.

    How to give yourself a manicure

    The method is short: soften everything in a brief soak, file the nails to shape in one direction, gently push back the cuticles (never cut them), lightly buff, then work in cuticle oil and hand cream — and polish only if you want to. Read the safety notes first (the cuticle, gel-removal, and salon-hygiene cautions especially), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Read this first — cuticles, gel removal, and salon hygiene

    • DON'T cut your cuticles — push them back instead. The cuticle is the seal that keeps bacteria and fungus out from under your nail. Cutting it opens tiny wounds right where infection gets in. After softening in warm water, gently push the cuticles back with a pusher; never trim them off.
    • NEVER peel or pick off gel, dip, or acrylic. Peeling it takes the top layers of your natural nail with it and leaves the nail thin, weak, and damaged — often for months. Soak it off in acetone or have a salon remove it properly, and give your nails periodic breaks from gel to recover.
    • Salon hygiene matters — tools and footbaths spread infection. Reused, unsterilized tools can pass on fungal and bacterial infections (and, rarely, bloodborne illness through a cuticle nick). Choose a clean, licensed salon, make sure tools are sterilized (autoclaved) or bring your own, and skip a service if you have a cut, sore, or infection on your hands.
    • Gel and UV lamps expose your hands to UV. The lamps that cure gel emit UV, and your hands get repeated doses. Many people apply sunscreen to the backs of their hands first or wear UV-protective gloves with the fingertips cut off — a simple, worthwhile precaution.
    • File in ONE direction, and don't over-buff. Sawing the file back and forth frays and splits the nail edge; instead, file in one direction to an even shape. And go easy with the buffer — over-buffing thins the nail and weakens it.
    • Moisturize, and watch for changes. Work cuticle oil and hand cream in often, especially with frequent hand-washing. And keep an eye out for nail changes — discoloration, pitting, or a nail lifting from the bed — which can signal fungus or a health issue worth showing a doctor.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Prep & soften

    Take off any old polish first, then soak your fingertips in warm, mildly soapy water for a few minutes. Softened nails file cleanly instead of splitting, and softened cuticles push back easily — the little soak is what makes every step after it gentler and safer.

    Shape the nails

    File each nail in one direction only — not back and forth — to an even shape you like: square, rounded, or oval. Sawing the file to and fro frays the edge and starts splits; single, consistent strokes leave a smooth edge that resists snagging and peeling.

    Tend cuticles & buff

    Push the cuticles back gently with a pusher — never cut them, since they seal out infection. Then, if you like, lightly buff the nail surface to smooth ridges and add a little shine. Go easy: over-buffing thins the nail and weakens it, so a few light passes is plenty.

    Moisturize

    Work a drop of cuticle oil into the base of each nail and massage a rich hand cream into your hands. This is the part that keeps nails from getting brittle and cuticles from cracking into hangnails — and it's the easiest thing to keep up between full manicures.

    Polish (optional) & maintain

    Polish is optional — on clean, dry nails, brush on a base coat, then color, then a top coat, or leave them bare. Then, between manicures, keep it easy: a quick file-and-oil touch-up as nails grow and chip stops anything from building back up before the next session. Log it.

    Signs to get help — and when it's a doctor, not a manicure

    A home or salon manicure handles ordinary nail upkeep beautifully. But a handful of problems aren't a manicure job at all — polishing or filing over them can make things worse. These are the signals to take a break, book a clean salon, or see a doctor:

    • Peeling, thin, or damaged nails. Nails that are peeling in layers, splitting, or paper-thin are often the result of picked-off gel or dip, or over-buffing. The fix isn't more polish — it's a break from gel and enhancements, plenty of cuticle oil, and gentle bare-nail upkeep while they grow back out.
    • Redness, swelling, or an infection around a nail. If the skin beside a nail is red, swollen, warm, or leaking fluid, that's an infection — often from a cut cuticle or a torn hangnail. Don't dig at it; keep it clean and see a doctor if it doesn't settle quickly, especially if pus develops.
    • Discolored, pitting, lifting, or crumbly nails. Yellow, thickened, crumbling, pitted, or lifting nails can signal a fungal infection or, sometimes, a health condition. They need a real diagnosis rather than polish over the top — see a doctor or dermatologist rather than just covering it up.
    • You want gel, dip, or acrylic removed safely. If you're tempted to peel it off, stop — that's exactly what damages the nail. Soak it off in acetone yourself, or have a salon remove it properly. It's cheap insurance against months of thin, weak regrowth.
    • Pro tip: for professional work — gel, a proper removal, or a treatment for a problem nail — choose a clean, licensed salon with sterilized (autoclaved) tools, or bring your own kit. And since a manicure is really nail-and-hand care, pair it with its toes-and-feet companion, the pedicure, and the fundamentals in the nail-trimming guide — fingers and toes both — and keep grooming tools clean the way you would your makeup brushes.

    Common questions

    How often should I get a manicure?

    About every two to three weeks is the sensible default, and it really depends on what you wear and how hard your hands work. Regular polish starts chipping within a week, so if you want it looking fresh you'll touch up or redo it around then; gel and dip hold for two to three weeks before the grow-out gap shows, which is the natural point to rebook. If you skip polish entirely, time it instead to the actual nail care — a shape-up and cuticle tending about every two weeks keeps things tidy. Whichever interval you land on, the trick is a little upkeep in between: a quick one-direction file when a nail gets long or catches, and a swipe of cuticle oil most days. That between-times maintenance is what keeps the full manicure a pleasant routine instead of a from-scratch rescue of ragged, chipped nails.

    How long does regular polish last versus gel?

    Regular polish typically looks perfect for a couple of days and starts chipping at the tips within about a week — sooner if your hands are busy with typing, cleaning, dishes, or gardening. It's easy to touch up or remove and redo at home, which is part of its appeal. Gel (and dip) is far more durable: cured under a lamp, it resists chipping and stays glossy for about two to three weeks, at which point the issue isn't chipping but grow-out — a visible gap appears at the base as your nail grows, and that's the cue to have it filled or removed and redone. The trade-off is removal: gel and dip have to be soaked off in acetone or professionally removed, never peeled — and that's the catch worth knowing before you commit to it.

    Should I cut my cuticles?

    No — this is one of the most important points on the page. Your cuticle is a living seal that keeps bacteria and fungus from getting under your nail, and cutting it opens tiny wounds right at that vulnerable spot, inviting infection and often leaving the area ragged and prone to hangnails. Nail techs frequently trim cuticles because it makes polish look neater, but dermatologists broadly advise against it. The safe approach is to soften the cuticles in warm water, then gently push them back with a cuticle pusher or an orange stick, and keep them soft and healthy with regular cuticle oil. If you get a hangnail — a small torn piece of skin — clip just that loose piece off cleanly; don't cut into the cuticle itself.

    Can I peel off gel or dip polish?

    Never — this is the single biggest way people damage their nails. Gel and dip bond tightly to the nail surface, so when you peel or pick them off, they take the top layers of your natural nail with them. The result is nails that are thin, weak, rough, and often peeling for months while they grow back out. The right way to remove them is to soak them off in acetone — wrap each nail in an acetone-soaked cotton pad and foil for ten to fifteen minutes, then gently push the softened product off — or have a salon do it. It's also worth giving your nails periodic breaks from gel to recover, and keeping them oiled and moisturized in between. If you ever find yourself picking at a lifting edge, stop and soak it off instead.

    Are salon manicures sanitary — and how do I avoid infections?

    They can be perfectly safe, but hygiene varies, so it's worth being choosy. Reused, unsterilized tools and whirlpool footbaths (on the pedicure side) can spread fungal and bacterial infections, and in rare cases a nick to the cuticle can transmit bloodborne illness. To stay safe: pick a clean, licensed salon, and make sure metal tools are sterilized in an autoclave between clients — or bring your own kit. Ask them not to cut your cuticles, skip any service if you have a cut, sore, or infection on your hands, and if a technician reaches for anything that looks worn or dirty, speak up or leave. If you're getting gel, note that the curing lamps emit UV, so consider sunscreen on the backs of your hands or UV-protective gloves. A good salon will happily accommodate all of this.

    How do I keep my nails from peeling and splitting?

    A few habits do most of the work. First, file in one direction to a smooth, even shape — sawing the file back and forth frays the edge and starts splits and peels. Second, moisturize: nails and cuticles get brittle when they dry out, so cuticle oil and hand cream, applied regularly and especially after washing your hands, keep them supple and strong. Third, be gentle — don't use your nails as tools, don't over-buff (it thins the nail), and never peel off gel or dip, which is a leading cause of peeling nails. Wear gloves for dishes and cleaning to spare them from water and chemicals. If your nails peel or split persistently despite all this, that can occasionally point to a nutritional or health issue worth mentioning to a doctor.
