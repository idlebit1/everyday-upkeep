How Often to Get a Pedicure

A pedicure isn't really about the polish — it's foot maintenance that happens to end in a nice color. Every few weeks your toenails need cutting straight across so they don't grow into the skin, your calluses need softening and filing before they crack, and your dry heels need cream before they split into something that actually hurts. Feet are the one part of you that you can't easily see, so the problems build quietly until sandal season or a painful heel forces the issue. Do it on a gentle schedule — a full pedicure roughly every four to six weeks, with a quick toenail-and-heel touch-up in between — and your feet stay comfortable, healthy, and ready for open shoes, instead of becoming a once-a-summer emergency.

🗓️ Full pedicure every 4–6 weeks ⏱️ ~30–45 min at home 💅 Salon runs ~$35–60 🧰 Home kit ~$20–40 once

Why it matters

"A pedicure" sounds like a small vanity — a color on your toes for the summer. But strip the polish away and what's left is real maintenance: nails that need the right cut, calluses and heels that need care before they crack, and feet that need looking at because you never really do. Here's what's actually at stake:

It keeps toenails healthy

Cut straight across and kept a sensible length, toenails resist ingrowing and stay comfortable in shoes instead of pressing into the skin. Regular foot care is really just regular nail care — the shape and the timing are what keep your toes out of trouble.

Ingrown, painful toenails

Calluses & cracks worsen untended

Left alone, calluses thicken and dry heels split into painful, infection-prone cracks. Softening in warm water, filing the buildup down, and moisturizing keeps the skin supple — so a heel never gets to the point where it bleeds and stings with every step.

Split, bleeding heels

Feet you can't see get neglected

Your feet are the one part of you that you never really look at, so fungus between the toes, a corn, a thickening nail, or a small sore can all build quietly. A regular hands-on routine is what catches those early, while they're still nothing much to fix.

Problems caught late

It's comfort and confidence

Smooth, cared-for feet simply feel good — no rough heels catching on the sheets, no ragged nails, no self-consciousness about open shoes. A gentle routine beats the once-a-summer scramble, and it makes sandal season something you're ready for rather than dreading.

The once-a-summer scramble

How often should you get one?

A full pedicure roughly every four to six weeks — with a quick toenail trim and a heel file-and-moisturize in between — is the sensible default. But sandal season, a lot of walking or running, and callus-and-cracked-heel trouble all pull toward more often, while quiet closed-shoe months stretch it out. Answer four quick questions:

1. What are you mostly after?
If you want the color and shine kept up, you'll go more often to stay ahead of grow-out and chips; if you mainly want comfortable, cared-for feet, you can time it to the actual foot maintenance instead.
2. How much are you on your feet — and is it sandal season?
Heavy walking and running build calluses and wear on the feet faster, and sandal season means your feet are both more visible and more exposed — both reward keeping up a tighter routine.
3. Any callus, dry-heel, or ingrown trouble?
Calluses and dry heels come back and thicken if you let them, and ingrown-prone toenails need staying on top of the straight-across trim — so recurring foot issues are the strongest reason to keep the interval short.
4. Any diabetes, neuropathy, or poor circulation?
This is the most important answer. If yes, foot care becomes a health matter, not a beauty one — a careful routine and clean tools matter enormously, and a podiatrist is often the safest choice over a salon.

    What it costs — and what skipping it costs

But the whole job is also cheap to do yourself: a simple kit — clippers, a foot file, a pumice or paddle file, a cuticle pusher, and a good foot cream — is a one-time $20–40 and does many pedicures.     How to do a pedicure the right way

    The method is short: soak to soften everything, cut the toenails straight across, gently push back cuticles and file (never shave) the calluses, then moisturize the heels and soles — keeping cream out from between the toes. Read the safety notes first (the callus, hygiene, and diabetic cautions especially), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Read this first — feet are where small mistakes turn serious

    • NEVER shave, razor, or aggressively cut calluses. A "credo" or callus shaver cuts too deep — it causes bleeding wounds and infection, and it's banned for salons in many places for exactly that reason. Soften calluses in warm water and gently file them down with a foot file or pumice instead. Slow and repeated beats deep and dangerous.
    • Cut toenails STRAIGHT across — not rounded into the corners, not too short. Rounding the corners or cutting down into them is what causes ingrown nails. Leave the corners slightly proud of the skin so the nail grows out over the edge, and file the squared edges smooth.
    • Don't cut your cuticles — push them back. The cuticle seals the nail against infection. Cutting it opens tiny wounds right where bacteria and fungus get in. After softening, gently push the cuticles back with a pusher; never trim them off.
    • If you have DIABETES, neuropathy, or poor circulation, take special care. You may not feel a cut, and a small wound on a slow-healing foot can become serious. Don't cut cuticles or calluses at all, inspect your feet each time, keep tools scrupulously clean — and seriously consider seeing a podiatrist rather than a salon. That's the safe, normal choice, not an overreaction.
    • Salon hygiene matters — footbaths spread infection. Whirlpool footbaths can harbor fungus, bacteria, and warts. Choose a clean salon, ask that tools be sterilized (autoclaved) or bring your own, don't shave your legs right before a pedicure (tiny nicks let bugs in), and skip a pedicure entirely if you have any cut, sore, or infection on your feet.
    • Moisturize — but keep cream out from between the toes. Work foot cream into the heels and soles for soft, crack-free skin, but leave the spaces between your toes dry. Trapped moisture there invites athlete's foot and other fungus.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Soak & clean first

    Soak your feet in warm, mildly soapy water for a few minutes and give them a wash. Softened nails cut cleanly instead of splitting, and softened calluses file down far more easily and safely — the soak is what makes every step after it gentler.

    Trim the toenails straight across

    Cut each toenail in a flat line across the top — not rounded, not too short — and leave the corners slightly proud of the skin. Then file the squared edges smooth. This single habit is the whole secret to preventing painful, ingrown toenails.

    Cuticles & calluses — gently

    Push cuticles back with a soft pusher — never cut them off, since they seal out infection. And on softened calluses, file with a foot file or pumice; never shave or razor them. Filing is slow but safe; shaving cuts too deep and invites infection.

    Moisturize the heels & soles

    Work a rich foot cream into the heels and soles until the skin is soft and supple — this is what keeps dry heels from cracking. Keep the cream out from between the toes, though; trapped moisture there is exactly what athlete's foot loves.

    Finish & keep up in between

    Polish is optional — brush it onto fully dry nails if you like the color. Then, between full pedicures, keep it easy: a quick straight-across toenail trim and a heel file-and-moisturize as needed stops anything from building back up before the next full session.

    Signs to see a pro — and when it's a podiatrist, not a pedicure

    A home or salon pedicure handles ordinary foot upkeep beautifully. But a handful of problems aren't a pedicure job at all — trying to file, cut, or polish your way through them can make things worse. These are the signals to book a podiatrist:

    • An ingrown toenail that's red, swollen, or oozing. Once the skin beside a toenail is painful, inflamed, or leaking fluid, it's an ingrown nail — possibly infected. Don't dig at it and don't let a salon dig at it; a podiatrist can relieve it properly and fix a recurring one for good.
    • Thick, crumbly, or discolored (fungal) nails. Yellow, thickened, brittle, or crumbling toenails usually mean a fungal infection. They're hard to cut safely, easy to spread, and often need a real diagnosis and treatment rather than just polish over the top or a harder trim.
    • Deep cracked heels, or painful corns and calluses. A heel cracked deep enough to bleed or sting, or a corn or callus that hurts to walk on, is past the file-and-moisturize stage. A podiatrist can safely reduce it and find what's causing it — never reach for a razor or callus shaver yourself.
    • Any foot wound that isn't healing. A cut, sore, or blister on the foot that's slow to close, spreading redness, warm, or increasingly painful needs to be looked at — especially on the feet, where infections escalate quietly.
    • Diabetes, neuropathy, or poor circulation. If you have any of these, the math changes entirely: a nick you can't feel can become a serious wound. Many people in this situation have a podiatrist handle their toenails and foot care completely — that is exactly the right, recommended call, not an overreaction.
    • Pro tip: a reputable salon with sterilized (autoclaved) tools, or your own clean kit at home, keeps ordinary feet in great shape between any podiatry visits. And since a pedicure is really foot-and-nail care, pair the toenail side of it with the full nail-trimming guide — fingers and toes — and keep the rest of your grooming on schedule.

    Common questions

    How often should I get a pedicure?

    A full pedicure roughly every four to six weeks is the sensible default — that's about how long it takes toenails to grow out and calluses and dry heels to build back up. Tighten toward every three to four weeks if you're on your feet a lot, it's sandal season, or you're prone to calluses, cracked heels, or ingrown nails; stretch toward six to eight weeks in quiet, closed-shoe months when your feet stay comfortable. Whichever interval you land on, the trick is to do a quick bit of upkeep in between: a straight-across toenail trim when they get long, and a heel file-and-moisturize when they get rough. That little between-times maintenance is what keeps the full pedicure a pleasant routine instead of a once-a-summer emergency rescue.

    Are calluses safe to shave off?

    No — and this is the most important safety point on the whole page. Never shave, razor, or aggressively cut a callus with a "credo" or callus shaver. It cuts too deep, causes bleeding wounds, and opens the door to infection, which is exactly why it's banned for salons in a lot of places. If a salon reaches for a razor on your feet, that's a red flag. The safe way to deal with a callus is to soften it in warm water and gently file it down with a foot file or pumice stone — a little at a time, over repeated pedicures rather than all in one go. You're trying to thin and smooth it, not remove it entirely; some callus is your body's protection. If a callus or corn is painful or very thick, that's a job for a podiatrist, not a blade at home.

    How do I prevent ingrown toenails?

    Three habits prevent the vast majority of them. First, cut straight across, not rounded, and leave the corners slightly proud of the skin rather than digging the clipper down into the sides — then just file the squared corners so they aren't sharp. Second, don't cut them too short; over-trimming exposes the skin at the corner and lets the growing nail press into it, so leave a little white edge. Third, give your toes room — shoes and socks that crush the toes push the nail edges into the skin, so well-fitting footwear matters more than people realize. Soften the nails in warm water first so the (often thicker) toenails cut cleanly. If a toenail does start to ingrow and the corner gets tender, red, or swollen, don't dig it out — soak it, keep it clean, and see a podiatrist if it doesn't settle.

    Is it safe to cut my cuticles?

    No — leave them alone. The cuticle is the little seal of skin where the nail meets the toe, and its whole job is to keep bacteria and fungus out of the nail bed. Cutting it removes that seal and creates tiny open wounds right at the base of the nail, which is a common way nail infections start — it's also why reputable salons that trim cuticles have to be scrupulous about sterile tools. The safe move is to soften the cuticles in warm water during the soak, then gently push them back with a cuticle pusher, and moisturize so they stay supple. If a salon asks whether you want your cuticles cut, "just pushed back, please" is the healthier answer. Trim only an actual hangnail — the loose, snagging bit of skin beside the nail — with clean tools, and never tear it off.

    Are salon footbaths sanitary — how do I avoid infections?

    They can be a real source of trouble if a salon is careless. Whirlpool footbaths, especially ones with jets, can trap skin, water, and bacteria in the pipes between clients and have caused outbreaks of skin infections; salons can also spread fungus and warts through poorly cleaned tools. To protect yourself: choose a clean, licensed salon and glance at whether the footbaths and stations are actually wiped down between clients; ask that metal tools be sterilized in an autoclave or simply bring your own clippers, file, and pusher; don't shave your legs right before your appointment, since tiny nicks give bacteria an entry point; and skip the pedicure entirely if you have any cut, sore, blister, or infection on your feet or lower legs. A good salon will happily answer questions about its cleaning — a defensive or vague answer is your cue to go elsewhere.

    Pedicures with diabetes — what's different?

    Quite a lot, and it's worth taking seriously. Diabetes can dull sensation in the feet (neuropathy) and slow healing (from poor circulation), which means two things at once: you might not feel a nick or cut, and a small wound that would be trivial on a healthy foot can become a serious, slow-to-heal infection — occasionally a limb-threatening one. So the rules change: never let anyone cut your cuticles or shave/cut your calluses, cut toenails straight across and never into the corners or skin, inspect your feet carefully every time, keep tools scrupulously clean, and check for any cut, redness, or sore afterward. Many people with diabetes, neuropathy, or poor circulation simply have a podiatrist handle their toenails and foot care entirely — that is the recommended, normal approach, not an overreaction. If you do visit a salon, tell them, ask for extra gentleness and clean tools, and skip anything that breaks the skin.
