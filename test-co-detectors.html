Test Your CO Detectors

Carbon monoxide has no color, no smell, and no taste — the only way you'll ever know it's filling your home is if a working alarm tells you. Pressing the test button on every CO alarm takes about five minutes, twice a year, and it's how you confirm the horn still blares. But the button only checks the alarm, not the sensor inside it — so this page also covers the expiration date that quietly decides when the whole unit has to go.

🗓️ Test every 6 months (do it with your smoke alarms) ⏱️ ~5 minutes whole house 🔋 Batteries yearly · units every 5–7 years ☠️ CO is odorless and deadly

Why it matters

Carbon monoxide is the gas your senses can't catch — odorless, colorless, and tasteless. It comes from anything that burns fuel, and at high enough levels it can knock you out before you understand anything is wrong. An alarm is the only warning you get, which is why it has to actually work:

You can't sense it

CO has no smell, color, or taste, and its first symptoms — headache, dizziness, nausea — read like the flu. People fall asleep and don't wake up. Without an alarm, there is no warning at all.

The stakes are lives, not dollars

Every fuel appliance is a source

Furnaces, water heaters, gas stoves and ovens, fireplaces, wood stoves, cars in the garage, and portable generators all produce CO. A cracked heat exchanger or a blocked flue is all it takes to send it indoors.

~400 U.S. deaths a year (non-fire)

The sensor expires

A CO sensor is a chemical cell that wears out — most are rated for about 5–7 years (some newer ones 10). Past that date the unit may still power on and pass a button test while no longer detecting CO at all.

$20 – $40 per replacement unit

The button only checks the horn

Pressing test confirms the battery, electronics, and siren work — it does not verify the sensor still detects CO. That's why the printed expiration date, not the test, decides when the unit retires.

Test ≠ proof the sensor works

One alarm isn't coverage

CO mixes through the air, so you need an alarm on every level of the home and one near every sleeping area — close enough to wake you. A single unit by the furnace won't rouse a closed-door bedroom upstairs.

$20 – $40 per missing alarm

It pairs with your smoke alarms

Fire and CO are two threats with one habit. Test CO alarms on the same schedule as your smoke detectors, or fit combination smoke/CO units — one less ceiling puck to remember, two dangers covered.

One routine covers both

How often should you test them?

Every six months — bundled with your smoke-alarm checks at the daylight-saving clock changes — is the sensible default, and monthly is the ideal. But gas appliances, an attached garage, or a fireplace raise the stakes enough that you shouldn't skip, and an aging alarm has a separate, hard deadline. Answer three questions:

1. What CO sources does your home have?
A furnace, water heater, gas range, fireplace, wood stove, or a garage that shares a wall with the house all produce CO. The more fuel-burning, the more an alarm earns its place — and the less you want to stretch the schedule.
2. When did you last press the test button?
If it's been a year or more — or you've never done it — start with a full check today, then settle into the every-six-months rhythm.
3. How old are your CO alarms?
The CO sensor wears out in about 5–7 years (some newer units last 10). The expiration date is printed on the back or side — that date, not the test button, decides when the whole unit retires.

    How to test every CO detector

    A full session — locate every alarm, press test, check dates, swap batteries, dust them off — takes about five to ten minutes in a typical home. Read the rules first (especially what the button does and doesn't prove), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Read this before you press anything

    • The test button only checks the horn and circuitry — not the sensor. A unit can pass the button test and still be blind to CO. The only way to know the sensor still works is to replace the whole alarm by its printed expiration date.
    • If a CO alarm ever actually sounds, get everyone outside to fresh air immediately and call 911 or your gas company from outside. Don't ignore it, don't assume it's false, and don't go back in until responders say it's safe.
    • Install an alarm on every level of the home and near every sleeping area — including the basement if you have one. CO mixes through the air, so coverage and proximity to bedrooms both matter.
    • Never run a car, generator, grill, or any fuel engine in an enclosed space — not in the garage, even with the door open, and never indoors. Generators belong well away from windows, doors, and vents.
    • It's loud — around 85 dB. Warn the household before you test, and know the difference between the steady emergency pattern and the occasional low-battery chirp.

    📐 Five things worth knowing

    Find every alarm first

    Walk the house and locate one on each level and near each sleeping area. Short anywhere — especially a bedroom level or a floor with the furnace — and that's a $20–$40 gap to close today.

    Press and hold the test button

    Hold the TEST button until the alarm sounds — usually a few seconds, often a loud four-beep pattern. A weak or silent response means a fresh battery and re-test, then replace the unit if it still fails.

    The expiration date decides

    Every CO alarm carries a manufacture and a replace-by date. At that date — roughly 5–7 years out — the whole unit is replaced, passing button test or not, because the sensor has worn out. No readable date? It's old enough.

    Fresh battery, then dust it off

    If the unit takes a battery, put a fresh one in (sealed 10-year units have none to change). Then vacuum or brush dust and cobwebs off the vents — a clogged grille can keep the sensor from reading the air.

    Learn the two sounds apart

    An occasional single chirp means low battery or end of life — deal with it that day. A loud, repeating four-beep pattern is the real CO emergency: get everyone out to fresh air and call for help.

    Signs to act on — and when to call a pro (or 911)

    Testing is the routine; these are the situations that need more than a button press. The first one is an emergency — treat it that way every time:

    • A CO alarm actually sounding (the steady, repeating pattern). This is not a drill. Get everyone — and pets — outside to fresh air, then call 911 or your gas company from outside. Don't re-enter until responders clear it, and have your fuel-burning appliances inspected before you do.
    • Anyone feeling sick when the alarm sounds — headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, or drowsiness. Get out and seek medical help immediately; mention possible CO exposure to the dispatcher.
    • An alarm past its expiration date — replace it now, not at the next session. A passing button test doesn't mean the sensor still works once the unit is past its replace-by date.
    • Repeated or recurring alarms, or you can't find the source. Don't keep resetting it. A qualified HVAC technician should inspect your furnace, water heater, and flues, and check for a cracked heat exchanger or a blocked vent.
    • A new gas appliance, a fireplace you've started using, or an attached garage. Add coverage and have a pro confirm the appliance vents properly — the most common indoor CO sources are also the most fixable.
    • You rent. In most U.S. jurisdictions the landlord must provide working CO alarms where fuel appliances or an attached garage exist — report a dead, missing, or expired one in writing, and never disable one yourself.

    Common questions

    How often should I test a CO detector?

    Press the test button at least every six months — the easy way to remember is to do it at the same time as your smoke alarms, on the daylight-saving clock-change weekends in March and November. Monthly is even better and takes only seconds per unit. Replace the batteries yearly (unless it's a sealed 10-year unit), and replace the whole alarm by its printed expiration date.

    Does the test button check whether the sensor still works?

    No — and this is the most important thing to understand about CO alarms. The test button verifies the battery, the electronics, and the horn. It does not expose the sensor to carbon monoxide, so it can't confirm the sensor still detects CO. The sensor is a chemical cell that degrades over years, which is exactly why every unit has an expiration date and why you replace the whole alarm on schedule rather than trusting a passing test.

    Where should I place CO alarms?

    Put one on every level of the home, including a finished basement, and one near (or inside) every sleeping area so it can wake you. Follow the manufacturer's height guidance — many modern CO alarms can go at any height because CO mixes evenly with air, though some makers suggest knee-to-eye level or near the ceiling. Keep them out of dead-air spots and at least 10–15 feet from fuel-burning appliances like the furnace or stove to avoid nuisance trips, and away from bathrooms and very humid or dusty areas.

    How long do CO detectors last?

    Most CO alarms are rated for about 5–7 years, though some newer models last up to 10 — the sensor is a consumable chemical cell that simply wears out. The replace-by date is printed on the back or side of the unit; once it passes, replace the whole alarm even if it still powers on and passes a button test. Many modern units chirp a distinct end-of-life signal as that date approaches. If you can't find a date, the unit is old enough to replace.

    What do I do if it actually goes off?

    Treat every CO alarm as real. Get everyone — including pets — out of the house and into fresh air immediately, then call 911 or your gas utility from outside. Don't open windows and "air it out" first, don't go looking for the source, and don't re-enter until emergency responders tell you it's safe. If anyone has a headache, dizziness, nausea, or confusion, tell the dispatcher you suspect carbon monoxide. Before running fuel appliances again, have them inspected to find what caused it.

    Can I get a combined smoke and CO alarm?

    Yes, and they're a tidy way to cover both threats with one device on the ceiling. Combination smoke/CO alarms test the same way — one button — and many announce which threat is detected ("Fire!" vs. "Carbon monoxide!") so you know how to respond. Two things to remember: replace the combo unit on the earlier of its two clocks (the CO sensor usually ages out first), and you still need enough units to cover every level and sleeping area, just as you would with separate alarms.

    What's the difference between the chirp and the alarm?

    A single chirp every minute or so, on its own, almost always means low battery or that the unit has reached end of life — replace the battery, or the whole alarm if it's at its date. The CO emergency signal is completely different: a loud, repeating pattern (commonly four quick beeps, a pause, then four more). If you hear that pattern, don't reset it — evacuate and call for help. When in doubt, treat it as the emergency.
