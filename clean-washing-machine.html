Clean Your Washing Machine

The machine that cleans everything else slowly gets dirty itself. Detergent, fabric softener, and the grime washed off your clothes leave a film that breeds a musty, sour-smelling biofilm — and on a front-loader, water trapped in the rubber door gasket grows the black mold behind that classic "sour towel" smell. Once a month you run an empty hot cleaning cycle and wipe down the few spots gunk hides. It takes about an hour (mostly the machine working), it costs near nothing, and it's the difference between laundry that comes out fresh and laundry that comes out smelling worse than it went in.

🗓️ Typically every month ⏱️ ~10 min hands-on, plus a cycle 🦠 Beats the musty, sour smell 💸 Costs ~$0–10

Why it matters

No load comes out perfectly clean — every wash leaves behind a little detergent, softener, lint, and the body soil rinsed off your clothes. In the warm, damp, dark drum that residue feeds a film of bacteria and mold, and the modern habit of cold washes and high-efficiency low water use only helps it along. Here's what that quiet buildup does:

It grows a musty, sour smell

Detergent, fabric softener, and washed-off body soil build a slimy biofilm where bacteria and mold thrive. That's the source of the musty, sour-milk odor that wafts out when you open the lid — and it only gets stronger the longer you leave it.

The "why does my washer stink?" problem

Front-loaders grow black mold

The rubber door gasket on a front-loader traps a little water in its folds after every cycle. Sealed up warm and dark, that puddle grows black mold — the source of the classic "sour towel" smell, and the most common front-loader complaint there is.

The black-gunk gasket problem

Residue ends up on clean clothes

Buildup doesn't stay put — flakes of detergent scum, softener gunk, and trapped lint break loose and redeposit on the very clothes you're trying to clean, leaving streaks, white residue, and a smell that survives the dryer.

Re-washing whole loads

It clogs and slows the machine

Sludge collects in the dispenser, the drain pump, and the filter that catches coins and lint, so the washer drains slower and works harder. Left long enough it can trip drain errors and shorten the life of an appliance you'd rather keep for a decade.

Drain errors, slow service calls

How often should you clean it?

Once a month is the sensible default for nearly every machine — but a front-loader that already smells, a houseful of laundry, or a habit of cold washes with a heavy hand on the detergent all build gunk faster, while a lightly used machine can stretch a bit. Answer four questions:

1. What kind of washer is it?
A front-loader has a round glass door on the front and a rubber gasket. An HE top-loader has a flat plate or low cone in the bottom instead of a tall central post; a traditional top-loader has that tall agitator post in the middle.
2. Does it smell musty or sour right now?
Open the door (or lid) after it's been closed a while and take a sniff. A musty, sour, or mildewy smell means a biofilm is already established — and that means cleaning more often until it's gone.
3. How do you usually wash?
Cold water dissolves less detergent and kills fewer bacteria, and extra detergent or fabric softener leaves more residue behind — so the cold-and-generous combo builds buildup fastest.
4. How many loads a week?
More loads means more residue passing through, and a damp drum that rarely gets a chance to dry out between washes.

    How to clean your washing machine

    The hands-on part is maybe ten minutes — the machine does the rest while you walk away. Read the techniques first (especially the front-loader gasket and the never-mix-bleach-and-vinegar rule) then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ A few care rules before you start

    • Never mix bleach and vinegar. Together they release chlorine gas, which is genuinely dangerous. Pick one cleaner per cycle — vinegar or bleach or a dedicated washer tablet — and never combine them in the same load or back-to-back without an empty rinse in between.
    • Run the cleaning cycle empty. No clothes, no towels — the point is to scour the machine, and fabric just absorbs the cleaner and the loosened gunk.
    • Easy on the vinegar with rubber seals. Some manufacturers warn that frequent acidic vinegar can degrade the rubber gasket and hoses over time. Check your manual; if in doubt, use a dedicated washer cleaner (like Affresh) for the cycle and save vinegar for occasional use.
    • Mind the drain-pump filter. If you open the little front access door to clean the filter (front-loaders), unplug the machine first, and have a shallow pan and towels ready — a surprising amount of trapped water pours out.
    • Check your machine's own clean setting. Many washers have a dedicated "Clean Washer," "Tub Clean," or "Sanitize" cycle. Use it if you have it — and follow the manual, which outranks this guide.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Start with an empty hot cycle

    The core of the job: add a washer tablet or about two cups of white vinegar to an empty drum and run the hottest, longest cycle (or your machine's "Clean Washer" setting). Hot water and the cleaner dissolve the biofilm that holds the smell.

    Front-loaders: clean the gasket

    This is where the sour-towel smell lives. Peel back the rubber door gasket's fold and wipe out the black mold, hair, and gunk trapped underneath with a cloth and a little vinegar or diluted bleach (never both). An old toothbrush gets the creases.

    Pull and wash the dispenser drawer

    Fabric softener and undissolved detergent cake up in the dispenser drawer into a slimy, mold-spotted gunk. Most drawers pop fully out (look for a release tab) — rinse it in the sink, scrub the slots with the toothbrush, and wipe the empty cavity too.

    Wipe the drum, glass, and outside

    After the cleaning cycle, wipe the drum and the inside of the door glass with a damp microfiber cloth to lift any loosened residue. Hit the control panel, knobs, and top of the machine too — and don't forget the underside of the lid on a top-loader.

    Clear the filter, then air it out

    Front-loaders hide a drain-pump filter behind a small panel near the floor — it catches coins, hair, and lint, and clogs cause smells and slow draining. Unplug, drain it into a pan, rinse it, and reseat it. Then leave the door and drawer open so the drum dries.

    Signs something's wrong — and when to call a pro

    A monthly clean handles odor and buildup. These are the signals it's more than residue — and the cases where the smart move is a repair tech instead of another cleaning cycle:

    • Water on the floor or leaking from the machine. A clean fixes smells, not leaks. Stop using it and have the hoses, door seal, or pump checked — a slow leak rots floors and a sudden one floods the room.
    • It won't drain or won't spin. Clothes left soaking wet usually means a clogged drain-pump filter (worth checking yourself first) or a failing pump or belt — the latter is a service job.
    • An error code that returns after you've cleaned the filter. Modern washers flash codes for drain, door-lock, and balance faults. If clearing the filter doesn't clear the code, look it up in the manual or call for service.
    • Mold that comes right back within days of a thorough clean. A gasket or dispenser that re-grows black mold almost immediately can mean water isn't draining fully or a seal is failing — worth a technician's eyes.
    • Loud banging, grinding, or violent shaking on the spin cycle. Beyond an unbalanced load, that points to worn bearings, shocks, or suspension — a repair, not a cleaning.
    • The drain filter is stuck, cracked, or won't reseal. Forcing a brittle filter housing can break it and cause a leak. If it won't open or close cleanly, leave it to a pro.
    • Pro tip: if the smell survives a hot cleaning cycle and a clean gasket, run a second cycle with a cup of baking soda, then check the drain filter — the gunk is usually hiding in the one spot you skipped.

    Common questions

    Vinegar or Affresh — which should I use?

    Both work. Washer-cleaning tablets like Affresh are designed for the job, dissolve slowly over a long hot cycle, and are gasket-safe, so they're the easy default — figure roughly $0.50–$1 a tablet. White vinegar (about two cups) plus an optional half-cup of baking soda is the cheap, no-extra-shopping option and works well, but some manufacturers warn that frequent acid can degrade rubber seals and hoses. A reasonable middle path: use a tablet most months and vinegar occasionally, or check your manual for what it recommends. Whatever you use, never combine it with bleach.

    Why does my front-loader smell when my old top-loader never did?

    Two reasons. First, front-loaders use far less water and seal up tight, so the drum stays damp and warm — ideal for mold and bacteria — instead of drying out. Second, that rubber door gasket has folds that trap a little water, lint, and detergent after every wash, and sealed in the dark they grow the black mold behind the "sour towel" smell. Older top-loaders held more water, dried out faster between loads, and had no gasket to trap anything. The fix isn't to go back — it's the monthly clean, a wiped gasket, and leaving the door open to air out.

    Should I really leave the door open all the time?

    Yes — for front-loaders especially, it's the single most effective habit. Leaving the door (and the dispenser drawer) cracked open between loads lets the drum dry out instead of sitting sealed and damp, which is what stops mold before it starts. Wipe the gasket dry after big or hot loads, too. The only caveat is households with small children or pets, where an open washer door is a hazard — in that case dry the drum and gasket with a towel after the last load of the day and close it.

    How exactly do I clean the gasket and the drain filter?

    Gasket: with gloves on, peel back the rubber fold around the door opening and wipe out the trapped water, hair, lint, and black mold with a cloth dampened in vinegar or a diluted bleach solution (one, never both). An old toothbrush reaches the creases; finish by drying it. Drain filter: on most front-loaders it's behind a small panel near the floor. Unplug the machine, set a shallow pan and towels underneath, slowly unscrew the cap to let the trapped water drain, then pull the filter, clear out the coins and lint and the surprising gunk, rinse it, and screw it firmly back in. Check the manual — some HE top-loaders have a filter too, others are self-cleaning.

    Am I using too much detergent?

    Almost certainly, if you have an HE machine — and it's a major cause of buildup and smell. High-efficiency washers use a fraction of the water, so they need far less detergent than the cap suggests; the excess doesn't rinse away and instead feeds the biofilm. Use detergent marked "HE," measure to the low end (often less than the line), go easy on fabric softener (or skip it — it's the worst offender for gunk), and if you see suds left at the end of a cycle, you're using too much. Less detergent genuinely means cleaner clothes and a cleaner machine.

    My machine has no "clean" cycle — what do I run?

    Just run the hottest, longest wash cycle you have, empty, with your cleaner added — that's effectively the same thing. For a traditional agitator top-loader, you can also fill it with the hottest water, add the vinegar (and optional baking soda), let it agitate for a minute, then pause and let it soak for an hour before finishing the cycle — the soak gives the cleaner time to break down buildup the short cycle wouldn't reach.

    Can I just leave a smelly washer and use more fragrance?

    No — masking it makes it worse. Scented detergents and softeners pile more residue onto the biofilm that's causing the smell, feeding the very problem you're trying to cover. The only real fix is removing the buildup: a hot cleaning cycle, a wiped gasket and dispenser, a cleared drain filter, and the habit of leaving the door open to dry. Once it's clean, a monthly cycle keeps it that way for pennies.
