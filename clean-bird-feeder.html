Clean Your Bird Feeder

A feeder is a kindness — and a crowd. Birds gather at it shoulder to shoulder, dropping seed husks and droppings, and in damp weather the leftover seed clumps, molds, and turns toxic. Left dirty, that feeder stops being a gift and becomes a disease hub: salmonella, avian pox, and trichomoniasis spread bird-to-bird at the very spot you set out to help them, and a nectar feeder can grow black mold in a matter of days. The fix is simple and cheap. Take the feeder down, toss the old seed, scrub it with hot soapy water, sanitize it with a quick bleach or vinegar rinse, and — the step everyone skips — let it dry completely before you refill. Do that on a schedule and your backyard flock stays healthy instead of sickened by your generosity.

🗓️ Seed feeders ~monthly 🐦 Nectar feeders every few days in heat ⏱️ ~15–20 min per feeder 💸 Costs ~$5–10

Why it matters

Feeding birds feels like pure generosity — and it can be. But a feeder concentrates dozens of birds in one small, shared spot, day after day, and that crowding is exactly how disease spreads. The seed and sugar water you put out only stay safe if the feeder stays clean and dry. Here's what a neglected feeder actually does to the flock you're trying to help:

A dirty feeder spreads deadly disease

Moldy seed, droppings, and damp at a crowded feeder spread bird diseases — salmonellosis, avian pox, and trichomoniasis pass mouth-to-mouth and through contaminated food. A single filthy feeder can sicken or kill a whole local flock during an outbreak.

Sick & dying backyard birds

Wet seed molds and turns toxic

Rain and humidity clump the seed, and damp, packed seed grows mold and aflatoxin — a fungal poison that's harmful even in small amounts. Birds eating moldy, fermenting seed get sick from the very food you put out for them.

Aflatoxin in moldy seed

Nectar ferments & molds in days

Sugar water is a magnet for fermentation and black mold, and in the heat a hummingbird feeder can go cloudy and foul in just a day or two. Spoiled nectar can harm the tiny birds that rely on it — so nectar feeders need cleaning far more often than seed.

Cloudy, moldy hummingbird nectar

It protects people, too

Handling old seed and droppings can pass salmonella to you — that's why you wear gloves, clean outside (never in the kitchen sink), and wash your hands afterward. A quick, careful scrub keeps the birds and your household healthy.

Salmonella risk to your family

How often should you clean it?

A seed feeder wants a real scrub about every month — every two weeks if the weather is hot and humid or your feeder is mobbed all day. Nectar and hummingbird feeders are in a different league entirely: in summer heat they need cleaning every few days. And the moment you see a sick or dead bird, the schedule goes out the window — clean now, and consider pausing feeding. Answer four quick questions:

1. What kind of feeder is it?
Sugar water ferments and molds within days in warm weather, so nectar feeders need cleaning far more often than any seed feeder — this single answer can override everything below.
2. What's the weather like where it hangs?
Heat and moisture are what grow the mold. Wet seed clumps and ferments fast, and warm sugar water spoils almost overnight — so a hot, humid or rainy spot pushes the schedule much tighter.
3. How busy is the feeder?
More birds means more droppings, more shed feathers, and more seed turned over and left damp — so a crowded feeder both spreads disease faster and gets dirty far sooner than a quiet one.
4. Seen anything worrying lately?
A sick or dead bird near the feeder is a red flag for disease. If you've seen one — or mold and clumped, sour seed — don't wait for the schedule: clean and disinfect now, and consider pausing feeding.

    How to clean your bird feeder

    The order matters: empty out the old seed, scrub everything with hot soapy water, sanitize with a diluted bleach or vinegar rinse, then rinse clean and — the step that actually protects the birds — let it dry completely before refilling. Read the techniques first (especially the safety box on protecting both birds and yourself), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Protect the birds — and yourself

    • Wear gloves and clean outside — never in the kitchen sink. Old seed and droppings can carry salmonella and other pathogens that infect people. Use a dedicated bucket and brush kept just for this, work outdoors, and wash your hands well afterward.
    • Scrub with hot soapy water, then sanitize. After scrubbing, soak or rinse in a diluted bleach solution — about 1 part bleach to 9 parts water — or a white-vinegar solution for a few minutes to kill the pathogens a scrub alone leaves behind.
    • Rinse thoroughly and let it DRY COMPLETELY before refilling. Rinse out every trace of bleach or soap, then air-dry the feeder fully — ideally in the sun. A damp feeder grows mold, and wet seed makes toxic aflatoxin; drying is the whole point.
    • Discard moldy, clumped, or wet seed — don't just top it off. Old seed at the bottom is where mold and toxins live. Dump it all out and start fresh rather than pouring new seed on top of stale.
    • For nectar feeders, use only a 1:4 sugar-water mix. One part white table sugar to four parts water — NEVER honey, brown sugar, or red dye, all of which can harm hummingbirds. Clean and refill every few days in heat, sooner if the nectar looks cloudy.
    • If you see sick or dead birds, take the feeder down. Disinfect everything and pause feeding for a couple of weeks to break the spread. During avian-flu or salmonella outbreaks, follow your local wildlife agency's guidance on whether to feed at all.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Take it down & empty it

    Bring the feeder down and dump out everything — toss any moldy, wet, or clumped seed straight into the trash, never onto the ground for birds. Clear the seed tray and ports, too. Don't ever pour fresh seed on top of old.

    Scrub with hot soapy water

    Work a bottle brush and hot, soapy water into every part — the tube, the seed ports, the perches, the corners, and the seams where gunk hides. An old toothbrush gets into the tight spots. This is what physically removes the film and droppings.

    Sanitize to kill pathogens

    Scrubbing isn't enough on its own. Soak or rinse the parts in a roughly 1:9 bleach solution (one part bleach, nine parts water) — or a white-vinegar solution — for a few minutes to kill the salmonella and other pathogens left on the surfaces.

    Rinse & dry completely

    Rinse out every trace of bleach, vinegar, and soap, then let the feeder air-dry all the way through — ideally in the sun — before you refill. Moisture is what grows the mold, so a feeder refilled while still damp is back to square one.

    Nectar feeders & ground litter

    Hummingbird feeders come fully apart — disassemble every piece, clean every few days in heat, and refill with fresh 1:4 sugar water (no dye or honey). And under any feeder, rake up the spilled seed and droppings so mold and disease don't fester below.

    Signs it's overdue — and when to act now

    A clean feeder is quiet and easy to miss: clear glass, dry seed, healthy birds coming and going. These are the warning signs it's past due — and the handful that mean you should stop and disinfect everything immediately, not wait for your usual schedule:

    • Sick, lethargic, or dead birds near the feeder. This is the one that overrides everything. A puffed-up, sluggish, or dead bird at your feeder is a disease signal — take the feeder down, disinfect it all, and consider pausing feeding for a couple of weeks to break the spread.
    • Black mold inside a nectar feeder. Cloudy, dark, or stringy growth in the sugar water means it's gone bad — empty it, scrub out the ports and reservoir, sanitize, and refill with fresh nectar. In heat, this is a clean-every-few-days job, not a weekly one.
    • Clumped, sprouting, or crusted seed. Seed that's stuck together, growing little shoots, or caked around the ports has gotten wet and is breeding mold and aflatoxin. Dump it, clean the feeder, and dry it fully before refilling.
    • A sour, fermented, or musty smell. If the feeder or its seed smells off, fermentation and mold are already underway. Trust your nose — clean it now rather than topping it off.
    • A noticeable wave of unwell birds in the area. If neighbors or local birders are reporting sick birds, treat it as an outbreak: clean and disinfect everything, and follow your state wildlife agency's guidance on whether to keep feeding at all.
    • During avian-flu or salmonella outbreaks, follow local guidance. Wildlife agencies sometimes recommend taking feeders down entirely for a period. This isn't a cleaning question — it's a public-health one, so check your local advisories when an outbreak is active.
    • Pair it with the rest of the outdoor cleaning. A feeder scrub slots in nicely alongside cleaning the patio furniture and pressure washing the deck or patio — knock them out together when you're already outside with the bucket and hose.

    Common questions

    How often should I clean my bird feeder?

    For a seed feeder — a tube, hopper, or tray — about once a month is the sensible baseline, tightening to every two weeks when the weather is hot and humid or rainy, or when the feeder is mobbed with birds all day. The damper and busier the feeder, the faster seed clumps and molds and the faster droppings build up, so those conditions push you toward the shorter interval. Nectar and hummingbird feeders are a completely different story: sugar water ferments and grows mold within days, so in summer heat they need cleaning every few days. And regardless of schedule, if you ever see a sick or dead bird, mold, or clumped, sour seed, clean immediately — don't wait for the calendar.

    How do I clean it, and what disinfectant should I use?

    Work outside, wearing gloves, with a bucket and brush you keep just for this — never the kitchen sink, because old seed and droppings can carry salmonella. First empty the feeder completely and throw out the old seed (don't top it off). Then scrub every part — tube, ports, perches, corners — with hot, soapy water and a bottle brush, using an old toothbrush for the tight spots. Next, sanitize: soak or rinse the parts for a few minutes in a roughly 1-part-bleach-to-9-parts-water solution, or a white-vinegar solution. Finally, rinse out all the bleach and soap thoroughly, and let the feeder air-dry completely — ideally in the sun — before refilling. Wash your hands well when you're done.

    How often do hummingbird and nectar feeders need cleaning?

    Far more often than seed feeders. Sugar water is essentially a culture medium — in warm weather it ferments and grows black mold within a day or two, and spoiled nectar can harm the tiny birds that depend on it. In summer heat, plan to empty, clean, and refill every two to three days; in cooler weather you can stretch it to roughly twice a week. Don't just top off the reservoir — dump the old nectar, take the feeder apart, scrub the ports and bottle, and refill with a fresh batch. If the nectar ever looks cloudy, dark, or stringy, it's gone bad and needs cleaning right away no matter how recently you filled it.

    Can a dirty feeder make birds sick — or make me sick?

    Yes to both. A feeder crowds many birds into one shared spot, and moldy seed, droppings, and damp let diseases spread bird-to-bird — salmonellosis, avian pox, and trichomoniasis are the common ones, and a single contaminated feeder can drive a local die-off during an outbreak. Wet, clumped seed also grows aflatoxin, a fungal toxin that poisons birds directly. And people aren't immune: handling old seed and bird droppings can pass salmonella to you, which is exactly why you wear gloves, clean outdoors rather than in your kitchen sink, and wash your hands afterward. Regular cleaning protects the whole flock and your household at the same time.

    What sugar-water ratio is right for hummingbirds — and what about red dye?

    Use a simple 1:4 ratio — one part plain white granulated sugar dissolved in four parts water. That's it. Do not use honey (it ferments fast and can grow a fungus deadly to hummingbirds), brown or raw sugar (the iron content can harm them), or any artificial sweetener. And skip the red dye entirely: it's unnecessary — the red parts of the feeder itself attract birds — and there are concerns it may be harmful, so clear sugar water is the safe choice. Make a batch, store extra in the fridge for up to a week, and bring it to room temperature before refilling. In heat, change it out every few days even if there's plenty left.

    What do I do if I see sick or dead birds at the feeder?

    Treat it as a disease event. Take the feeder down, dump and bag the seed, and thoroughly clean and disinfect everything — feeder, tray, and the ground beneath it where droppings and spilled seed collect. The strongest recommendation from most wildlife agencies is to then pause feeding for a couple of weeks (some say longer), which disperses the birds and breaks the chain of transmission, before cleaning again and resuming. If you find a dead bird, handle it with gloves or a plastic bag, and check your state wildlife agency's website — during active avian-flu or salmonella outbreaks they may ask you to report it and to keep feeders down for a defined period. When in doubt, err toward taking it down and following local guidance.

    A feeder is a kindness only if it's clean.

    Left dirty, it quietly spreads disease through the very flock you set out to help — and nectar feeders go bad in days, not weeks.
