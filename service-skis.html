Service Your Skis or Snowboard

Skis and boards don't get slow all at once — they lose it a run at a time. The wax wears out of the base until it dries pale and grabby and you find yourself poling across the flats; the steel edges round off on hard-pack and ice until the thing skids sideways where it used to bite; and a few rocks in an early-season traverse leave gouges that snag and let water into the core. A tune fixes all three: sharp edges to grip, a waxed base to glide, and base repairs for the nicks. Some of it is a happy garage evening with an iron and a scraper; some of it — grinds, deep repairs, and anything to do with your bindings — belongs to a shop. And the one rule you never skip is the off-season storage wax, because an unwaxed base oxidizes all summer. Do it right and an expensive setup stays fast for years.

🗓️ Full tune each season 🕯️ Wax every few outings 🔧 DIY wax, shop the grinds 💸 Shop tune ~$30–60

Why it matters

A ski or board is a tuned instrument: it grips because the steel edges are sharp, it glides because the base is waxed, and it stays healthy because the nicks get filled before water finds the core. Let any one of those go and the whole thing feels wrong — slow, grabby, and hard to turn just when you need it to hold. Here's what a skipped tune actually costs:

Sharp edges grip

The steel edges are what let the ski carve and hold on a slope. Dull, rounded edges skid instead of biting, and on hard-pack or ice they simply won't hold a turn — that's a control problem, and a safety one, not just a slow day.

Skidding & no grip on ice

A waxed base glides & lasts

Wax fills the base's pores so it slides smoothly and fast — but it does more than make you quicker. It keeps the base from drying out, going chalky white, and oxidizing. A dry base is a slow base, and oxidation is damage you can't just wax away.

Slow, dry, oxidizing base

Rock nicks get worse

A gouge or core shot from an early-season rock catches snow and drags, so a nicked base is a slow one. Worse, an open gouge lets water into the core, and left alone it spreads. Fill the small ones and get the deep ones repaired before they grow.

Snagging & water in the core

Tuning beats replacing

A season tune and regular waxing are cheap next to what they protect — a setup that easily runs into the hundreds. Keep the edges sharp and the base fed with wax and your gear stays fast and lasts for many years instead of going dead in a few.

How often should you tune?

The default is a full tune once a season — start of winter — plus a fresh coat of wax every few outings if you ride a lot. But it shifts with how you ski: a season-pass regular on icy hard-pack wants edges and wax far more often than someone who takes a couple of soft-snow trips a year. And whether you do it yourself or hand it to a shop changes the plan entirely. Answer four quick questions:

1. How often do you ride?
Every day on snow scrubs a little wax off the base and rounds the edges a touch. The more you ride, the more often the base needs feeding and the edges need a touch-up between full season tunes.
2. What conditions do you usually ride?
Ice and hard-pack dull edges fast, and rocky or thin early-season snow gouges bases. Soft powder is gentle on both. Rough conditions mean more frequent edge work and more base repairs.
3. What shape is the gear in right now?
Run a fingernail across the edge — if it doesn't shave a curl, it's dull. A chalky white, dry-looking base needs wax now. Visible gouges or a core shot need repair before your next day out.
4. Do you tune at home or take it to a shop?
Home waxing is cheap and easy once you're set up, so DIYers can wax often. Grinds, deep repairs, and bindings are shop jobs regardless — this answer just shapes how much of the how-to below you'll do yourself.

    The cost of skipping it

    Tuning is one of the cheapest ways there is to protect an expensive setup. A full shop tune runs about $30–60; a home wax kit is a one-time $40–80 that covers dozens of waxings; an edge tool is another $20–40.     How to service your skis or snowboard

    A full tune goes in order: inspect, repair the base, tune the edges, hot wax, then — for the off-season — leave a coat of storage wax on and put it away dry. Some of this is a satisfying garage evening; some of it belongs to a shop. Read the safety notes first (especially the iron and the storage wax), then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Sharp steel, a hot iron, and jobs that belong to a tech

    • Sharp edges and tuning tools cut — protect yourself. Wear gloves when you handle the steel edges and files, and secure the ski or board in a vise or on a stable bench so it can't shift while you work a tool along it.
    • Leave grinds and deep repairs to a shop. Base grinds and core-shot (deep gouge) repairs are easy to overdo, and a shop does them properly. Over-filing an edge or removing too much base ruins the ski — do light touch-ups at home and hand the big stuff to a tech.
    • Use a proper waxing iron and keep it moving. If you hot-wax at home, use a real waxing iron (or a dedicated old clothes iron) at the right temperature and never let it sit still — overheating scorches the base and can delaminate it. Work in a ventilated area.
    • Storage wax is the single most important off-season step. Leave a thick, unscraped coat of wax on the base for the summer. An unwaxed base dries out and oxidizes — the number-one off-season damage — so this one habit protects the gear more than anything else you'll do.
    • Store it dry, in a cool place. Wet steel edges rust. Dry the ski or board and put it somewhere cool — never a hot attic or a damp garage — for the off-season.
    • Bindings and DIN are a certified-tech job — never DIY. Do not adjust your own binding release settings. A wrong setting can fail to release, or release early, and cause injury. Have a shop check and set your bindings and DIN.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Inspect first

    Look the base over for dry, chalky white patches and any gouges, run a fingernail across the edges to feel for sharpness and burrs, and check the steel for rust. Knowing what you're dealing with tells you what the tune needs.

    Base repair (or shop it)

    Fill small gouges with a P-Tex candle drip, letting it cool and shaving it flush. Deep core shots and a full base grind, though, go to a shop — a burning candle can't reach the core, and a grind needs a machine.

    Tune the edges

    Lightly sharpen and deburr the steel edges with an edge tool held at the right angle — a few even passes to bring back the bite. Don't overdo it: taking off too much steel ruins the ski, so leave major edge work to a shop.

    Hot wax

    Drip wax onto the base, then iron it in with the waxing iron moving the whole time so it never scorches. Let it cool fully, scrape off the excess with a plastic scraper, and brush the base out for glide.

    Store waxed & dry

    For the off-season, leave a thick coat of storage wax on the base (unscraped), dry the edges so they can't rust, and loosen the bindings per current advice. Store it cool, and have a shop check the bindings and DIN. Then log the tune.

    Signs your gear needs a tune — and when to see a shop

    A well-tuned pair is quiet about it: it grips when you tip it on edge, glides on the flats, and turns without a fight. These are the signals it's overdue — and the handful of jobs that belong to a certified technician, not your garage bench:

    • Chattering or skidding on hard snow. If the ski rattles and washes out sideways on hard-pack or ice where it used to hold, the edges are dull and rounded. A sharpen brings the bite back.
    • A slow, dry, chalky-white base. When the base looks pale and dried-out and you're poling across the flats, it's starved of wax. A hot wax makes it fast and smooth again — and stops the drying from turning into oxidation.
    • Deep base gouges or a core shot. Small nicks you can fill with P-Tex; a deep gouge that reaches the core catches snow and lets water in. That's a P-Tex or base-grind repair — the deep ones at a shop.
    • Rusty edges. Rust on the steel means the gear went away wet. Light surface rust cleans up, but rust is a sign the storage habits need fixing — dry it and keep it cool.
    • Delamination — the base or topsheet peeling or lifting. Layers separating (often from an overheated iron or age) is not a DIY fix. Take it to a shop to see whether it's repairable or the gear is done.
    • Base grinds, deep repairs, and anything binding-related. A full base grind and core-shot repairs need a shop's machines and hands. And bindings and DIN release settings must be set by a certified technician — never adjust your own, and have them checked each season.
    • Pro tip: the store-it-right habit isn't just for skis. The same off-season logic applies to your camp gear — seam-sealing a tent and washing a sleeping bag — and keep your winter footwear ready by re-waterproofing boots.

    Common questions

    How often should I tune and wax my skis or snowboard?

    Get a full tune once a season — the start of winter is ideal, so you begin the year with sharp edges, a repaired base, and fresh wax. Waxing is more frequent: if you ride a lot, plan on a fresh coat every few outings, because each day on snow scrubs wax out of the base. Someone who skis only a few soft-snow days a year can stretch that, while a season-pass regular on icy hard-pack will want to wax often and touch up the edges between full tunes. The one appointment nobody should skip is the off-season storage wax at the end of the season — that's what keeps the base from drying out over the summer.

    What does a full ski tune include?

    Three things, in order. First the base: any gouges are filled and the base is flattened and cleaned up, often with a machine grind that resets the base structure. Then the edges: the steel is sharpened and deburred to the right angle so the ski grips again. Finally the wax: a hot wax is ironed into the base, cooled, scraped, and brushed so it glides fast and the base stays fed. A shop tune ($30–60) does all three by machine; at home you can handle the wax and light edge work while leaving grinds and deep repairs to a tech.

    Can I wax and tune my skis at home?

    Some of it, yes — and it's satisfying and cheap once you're set up. Hot waxing is very doable at home with a waxing iron, wax, a scraper, and a brush, and light edge touch-ups with an edge tool are within reach too. What you should not do at home is a full base grind, a deep core-shot repair, or anything to do with your bindings. Grinds need a machine, deep repairs need a shop's tools and skill, and binding and DIN release settings must be set by a certified technician for safety. A good split is: wax and light edges at home, grinds and bindings at the shop.

    How do I store my skis or board for the summer?

    The single most important step is a storage wax: iron a thick coat of wax onto the base and leave it unscraped for the whole off-season. An unwaxed base dries out and oxidizes over the summer — the number-one cause of off-season damage — and that thick coat seals it. Beyond that, make sure everything goes away dry, because wet steel edges rust. Store the gear somewhere cool and stable, not a hot attic or a damp garage, and loosen the bindings per current advice. In the fall, scrape and brush off the storage wax, tune, and you're ready.

    Why is my base dry and white?

    That pale, chalky look is oxidation — the base has dried out and the surface is degrading, usually because it went a long time without wax (often a summer stored unwaxed). It's more than cosmetic: an oxidized, dried base is slow and won't hold wax well. A hot wax or two will feed the surface and darken it back up if the oxidation is light. If it's badly oxidized, a shop base grind removes the dead layer and gives you a fresh surface to wax. The fix for next time is simple: wax regularly, and never store it over the summer without a storage-wax coat.

    Should I adjust my own bindings?

    No. Binding release settings — the DIN — are a safety system, and setting them is a job for a certified technician with the right tools and a release-check machine. A setting that's too high can fail to release in a crash and injure your knee; too low and the ski releases when it shouldn't, which is its own hazard. The correct number depends on your weight, height, boot sole length, ability, and age, and it should be verified, not guessed. Have a shop set and check your bindings each season, and never turn those screws yourself.
