Trim Your Trees

Most of what kills a roof, a car, or a fence in the next big storm is already up there, plainly dead, a full year before it falls. A light yearly walk-around — taking out the dead, broken, low, and crossing branches you can reach from the ground — keeps a tree healthy, shaped, and far less likely to drop a limb on something expensive. The trick is knowing your lane: small branches, from the ground, with the right cut. Anything big, high, near a power line, or that needs a chainsaw up a ladder isn't a DIY job — it's a call to a certified arborist, and that's not a cop-out, it's the whole safety plan.

🗓️ A light pass once a year 🌳 Best in late dormant season 🪚 Hand tools ~$60–150 ⚠️ Big/high work → certified arborist

Why it matters

A tree mostly takes care of itself — which is exactly why it's easy to forget that the dead, weak, and overgrown wood in it is a liability quietly waiting for a windy night. A light yearly pass on the branches you can safely reach earns its keep four ways:

Dead limbs are what fall in storms

The dead, weak, and overgrown wood up in a tree is what comes down in high wind — onto roofs, cars, fences, and people. Taking it out on a calm day, while it's small and reachable, is how you skip the expensive 2 a.m. surprise.

A limb through the roof

Good cuts keep a tree healthy

Removing dead and diseased wood and the branches that cross and rub each other improves airflow and structure, so the tree puts its energy into strong growth instead of fighting infection or wounds it can't heal.

Disease taking hold

Clears paths, drives & sightlines

Low and wayward branches that hang over the driveway, scrape the siding, block a window, or hide the corner where the driveway meets the street are a daily nuisance — and a hazard. Clearing them keeps everything open and safe.

A scraped car or blocked view

Right cuts heal — bad ones rot

A cut just outside the branch collar seals itself over. Flush cuts, stubs, and "topping" tear the bark and leave open wounds that invite rot, disease, and weak, fast regrowth — branches that are more dangerous than what you removed.

Rot, decay & weak regrowth

How often should you trim them?

A light yearly pass — dead, broken, low, and crossing branches — is the sensible default for most trees, with bigger structural pruning every few years. But a fast-growing or storm-battered tree right over the house tightens the schedule, while a small, healthy ornamental can stretch out. Just as importantly, the right answer for a big tree is often "call an arborist" — answer four quick questions:

1. How big is the tree — and how close to the house?
A big tree leaning over the roof, the driveway, or near power lines has the most at stake if a limb fails — and the work up there is squarely a pro's job. A small ornamental you can reach from the ground is the safe DIY case.
2. How fast does it grow / how messy is it?
Fast-growing species (silver maple, willow, poplar) and brittle, messy droppers put on weak wood quickly and shed limbs more readily, so they're worth checking on more often than a slow, tidy tree.
3. What kind of shape is it in right now?
Dead, broken, diseased, or rubbing wood is the priority — it's the stuff that fails and the stuff that spreads. Storm damage and disease especially mean acting sooner (and often calling a pro to assess it).
4. Can you do the work from the ground?
This is the line that matters most for safety. If you can reach it standing on the ground (or with a pole pruner), it can be a DIY job. The moment it needs a ladder, climbing, or a chainsaw overhead, it's an arborist's job — no exceptions.

    The cost of skipping it

The real money is in what neglect invites — a dropped limb on your roof or car, or a tree slowly killed by bad cuts and disease.     How to trim your trees — the safe way

    This is built around doing only the safe, reachable work yourself: small branches, from the ground, with a clean cut — the same light-and-often logic as trimming hedges and bushes, just up a tree. Read the safety box first — it's the most important part of this page — then the techniques (what to cut, when, and the proper cut), and work the checklist. Keep your pruners sharp so every cut is clean. Your progress saves automatically. When in doubt, hire it out.

    ⚠️ Read this first — most tree-work injuries are preventable

    • Never run a chainsaw while standing on a ladder. A chainsaw kicks, the ladder shifts, and the limb drops where you don't expect — it's one of the most dangerous things a homeowner can do. Chainsaw-up-a-ladder work is an arborist's job, full stop.
    • Never prune anything near power lines. A metal pole, a wet branch, or even your body close to a live line can be lethal. Don't touch it — call your utility (often free for line clearance) or a line-clearance professional. Every time.
    • If it's big, high, over the roof, or above shoulder height, call a certified arborist. Anything that needs you to climb, work overhead, or handle a heavy limb is a pro job. Falls and falling limbs are a leading cause of serious tree-work injury — this is the single most important rule on this page.
    • Wear the gear. Eye protection and a hard hat (things fall), gloves, and sturdy closed footwear. Falling debris doesn't announce itself.
    • Never stand under the limb you're cutting. Plan where it will fall, keep your footing clear, and make sure no one (and nothing) is in the drop zone. Stand to the side, not beneath.
    • Look up and around before you start. Check for wildlife and active nests, dead "widow-maker" branches hung up above you, and overhead hazards. Don't disturb an active nest — in many places they're legally protected.
    • Keep tools sharp and clean. A sharp blade makes a clean cut that heals; a dull one tears. Disinfect blades between trees if disease is around, so you don't spread it.
    • Know your limits. The whole strategy here is: do the small, ground-level stuff, and hire the rest. When in doubt, hire it out.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Decide what to cut — the 4 Ds first

    Start with the four Ds: dead, diseased, damaged, and crossing/rubbing branches — plus any low limbs in the way. That's nearly all the trimming most trees need. Don't remove more than about a quarter of the canopy in a single year, or you stress the tree.

    Time it right — dormant season

    Most trees prune best in late dormant season — late winter into early spring, before the buds break — when wounds close fast and the structure is easy to see. Avoid heavy summer and fall cuts. The exception: spring-flowering trees, which you prune right after they bloom.

    Cut just outside the branch collar

    Find the branch collar — the slightly swollen ring of bark where the branch meets the trunk — and cut just outside it. Not flush against the trunk (that removes the tissue that heals the wound), and not leaving a long stub (which rots). The collar does the healing; leave it intact.

    The 3-cut method for any limb with weight

    For a limb heavy enough to tear the bark as it falls: (1) an undercut partway through from below, a foot or so out; (2) a top cut a little farther out to drop the limb cleanly; then (3) a final cut at the collar to remove the stub. The undercut stops the bark from peeling down the trunk.

    Never "top" a tree — thin and shape

    "Topping" — hacking the whole crown back to stubs — is one of the worst things you can do: it triggers weak, dense regrowth that's more likely to fail later, and starves and disfigures the tree. Thin and shape instead, then clean up — chip or compost the small stuff and haul off the rest.

    Signs it's a pro's job — and when to call one

    The most important skill in tree work is knowing what not to do yourself. A light yearly pass on small, reachable branches is genuinely DIY — but the moment you hit any of these, stop and call a certified arborist (and for power lines, the utility). It's the cheaper outcome, every time:

    • Large dead limbs, or a leaning or cracked trunk. Big deadwood overhead and any sign of structural failure — a lean that's new, a crack, a split fork, heaving roots — is serious. Don't probe it from a ladder; get an arborist to assess and remove it safely.
    • Branches in or near power lines. Never your job, ever. Call your utility (line clearance is often free) or a line-clearance professional. A pole, a wet branch, or your body near a live line can kill.
    • Big cuts over the house, the driveway, or anything you can't afford to drop on. Heavy limbs over a roof or car need to be rigged down in pieces — that's professional rigging, not a one-person ground job.
    • Signs of disease, pests, or root and structural problems. Mushrooms at the base, oozing cankers, large areas of dead bark, sawdust from boring insects, or a tree that's declining overall — an arborist can diagnose it and tell you whether it's treatable or a hazard.
    • Anything you can't reach with both feet on the ground. If it needs a ladder, climbing, or a chainsaw overhead, it's a pro job. This is the bright line — when in doubt, hire it out.
    • Pro tip: hire an ISA-certified arborist specifically (ask to see the credential), get the scope in writing, and confirm they carry insurance. A good arborist prunes to keep the tree healthy — and will refuse to top it. Once-yearly DIY clearing pairs well with a pro check every few years on a big tree. Slot this yearly walk-around in with the rest of your seasonal yard work, like fall leaf cleanup and mulching beds.

    Common questions

    How often should I trim my trees?

    For most trees, a light pass once a year handles the routine work: take out the dead, broken, low, crossing, and rubbing branches you can safely reach from the ground. On top of that, bigger structural pruning every three to five years keeps a tree well-shaped over the long run — though on a large tree that's usually an arborist's job. Fast-growing or messy species (silver maple, willow, poplar) and any tree that's storm-damaged or hangs over the house are worth checking more often. Young trees benefit from light formative pruning in their first several years to build good structure, which saves a lot of trouble later. The one thing to avoid is the opposite of light-and-often: don't let a tree go for years and then cut it hard all at once.

    When is the best time of year to prune?

    For most trees, late dormant season — late winter into early spring, before the buds break — is ideal. The tree isn't actively growing, wounds close quickly as growth resumes, there are no leaves in the way so you can see the structure, and many pests and diseases that spread through fresh cuts are inactive. Avoid heavy pruning in summer (the tree is working hard and cuts stress it) and especially in fall, when fresh cuts heal slowly and decay fungi are releasing spores. Two exceptions: you can remove dead, broken, or hazardous branches any time of year, and spring-flowering trees (like flowering cherry, dogwood, or redbud) should be pruned right after they finish blooming, so you don't cut off the buds that became this year's flowers.

    How much can I safely cut off at once?

    As a rule of thumb, don't remove more than about a quarter (25%) of a tree's living canopy in a single year — and on mature trees, less. The leaves are how the tree feeds itself, so over-pruning starves it and triggers a flush of weak, watersprout regrowth as it scrambles to replace them. Dead wood doesn't count toward that limit — you can take out all the dead, broken, and diseased branches you find, since they're not contributing anything. If a tree clearly needs more than that to get back in shape, spread the work over two or three years rather than doing it all in one drastic session. Going slow is almost always better for the tree.

    How do I make a proper cut — and what's the 3-cut method?

    For a small branch, cut just outside the branch collar — the slightly swollen ring of bark where the branch joins the trunk or parent limb. Don't cut flush against the trunk (that removes the tissue that seals the wound and invites rot), and don't leave a long stub (it dies back and decays). The collar grows over the cut and heals it. For any limb heavy enough that its weight could tear the bark as it falls, use the 3-cut method: first, an undercut a third of the way through from below, about a foot out from the trunk; second, a top cut an inch or two farther out, which lets the limb drop cleanly without the bark peeling down the trunk; third, a final cut to remove the remaining stub right at the collar. Skip the undercut on a heavy limb and the falling weight can strip a long wound of bark down the trunk — exactly the damage you're trying to avoid. And skip the wound paint or tar sealers: research shows they don't help and can trap moisture; a clean cut at the collar heals best on its own.

    Is tree topping bad?

    Yes — topping (cutting the whole crown back to stubs to reduce a tree's height) is one of the most harmful things you can do to a tree, and reputable arborists won't do it. It removes so much leaf area at once that the tree is starved and stressed, and it responds with a dense burst of fast, weakly-attached "watersprout" growth. That regrowth is actually more hazardous than the original limbs, because it's poorly anchored and prone to breaking — so topping makes the storm-damage problem worse, not better, within a few years. The big stub wounds also rot, opening the tree to decay and disease, and a topped tree is permanently disfigured. If a tree is genuinely too big for its spot, the right answer is proper crown reduction by a certified arborist (selective cuts back to healthy laterals) or, if it's truly the wrong tree in the wrong place, removal and replacement — never topping.

    When do I need to call an arborist instead of DIY?

    The simplest test: if you can't do it with both feet on the ground (or with a pole pruner from the ground), it's a pro's job. Specifically, call a certified arborist for anything big or high, work over the roof or driveway, large or heavy limbs, anything that would require you to climb the tree or run a chainsaw from a ladder, or any sign of disease, pests, or structural problems like a lean, crack, or root issue. Anything in or near power lines is never DIY — call your utility or a line-clearance pro. None of that is a failure on your part; matching the job to the right person is the safety plan. Falls and falling limbs are a leading cause of serious injury in this kind of work, so the rule is genuinely "when in doubt, hire it out." Look for an ISA-certified arborist, ask for proof of certification and insurance, and get the scope in writing.
