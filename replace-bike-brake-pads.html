Replace Your Bike Brake Pads

Brake pads are the small, cheap, consumable part that decides whether your bike actually stops — and they wear out a stop at a time, so quietly that you adjust to weaker braking without ever noticing. Worse, a pad worn down to its metal backing doesn't just brake badly; it grinds metal-on-metal and chews up the expensive surface behind it — a grooved rim or a scored rotor that costs far more than the pads would have. Catch the wear line, fit the right pads, align them to the rim (not the tire), keep oil off disc pads, and bed them in, and your brakes stop crisply again. Here's how to do it on both rim and disc brakes — and when to hand it to a shop.

🗓️ Check yearly, replace as worn 📏 Go by wear, not the calendar ⏱️ ~20–40 min to swap 💸 Pads $10–40 vs a ruined rim

Why it matters

Brakes are the one system on a bike where "good enough" isn't — they're what stops you on a descent, in the wet, and a foot short of a car door swinging open. Pads are the wear part that does the stopping, and because they fade so gradually you stop noticing how weak they've gotten. Here's what worn pads cost you:

Worn pads mean longer, weaker stops

As the friction material thins, it takes more lever and more distance to slow down — and you adapt without realizing it. That extra few feet is a real safety problem on a steep descent, in the rain, or in traffic, exactly where you most need the bike to stop now.

A stop you can't make in time

Worn pads destroy the braking surface

Let a pad wear down to the metal holder and it grinds metal-on-metal into the surface behind it. On rim brakes that gouges the rim — eventually a new wheel build; on disc brakes it scores the rotor. Both cost far more than the pad you skipped.

A grooved rim or scored rotor

Pads wear unevenly and fast

Pads are a consumable — and wet, gritty, muddy, and steep riding chews them up far faster than dry flat miles. Grit embeds in the pad and turns it into sandpaper. So "they were fine last year" isn't enough; you have to look, not assume.

Gone before you'd guess

Done right, they stop crisply

The right pad compound, aligned squarely to the rim (not rubbing the tire), and bedded in, gives you firm, quiet, reassuring braking. Done wrong — a pad eating the tire, or oil-contaminated disc pads — it's dangerous. The method is what makes it safe.

Crisp stops, not a guess

How often should you replace them?

There's no mileage number that fits every bike — pads go by wear, not the calendar. Inspect them regularly and plan to replace about yearly, but the real trigger is the wear line: on rim brakes, when the grooves disappear; on disc brakes, when the friction material drops under about a millimeter or it starts squealing or stopping weakly. Wet, muddy, steep, and heavy riding wears pads far faster. Four quick questions:

1. How much do you ride?
Every braking event wears a little material away, so high-mileage and commuting bikes simply reach the wear line sooner — check those pads more often and keep a spare set on hand.
2. What do you ride in?
Water and grit turn the rim or rotor into a grinding paste against the pad, and long steep descents bake heat into the brakes — all of it eats pads dramatically faster than dry, flat riding.
3. What brakes does the bike have?
The brake type drives both the method and the pad choice — rim pads bolt to the caliper and have a visible wear line; disc pads slot into the caliper around the rotor and are measured by friction-material thickness.
4. How do the brakes feel and look now?
Any of these — a squeal, a soft or weak lever, or pads worn past the line — means replace now, whatever the calendar says. Worn pads only get more dangerous and start damaging the rim or rotor.

    How to replace your bike brake pads

    The job is short, but brakes are safety-critical, so the order and the details matter: check the wear, remove the old pads (noting how the washers and springs went), fit the matching new ones, align them — to the rim and not the tire on rim brakes — then bed them in and test before you trust them. Read the safety box and the techniques first, then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Brakes are safety-critical — read this first

    • If your brakes feel weak, spongy, or are grinding, fix it before you ride — don't "test it" at speed. A brake that's failing on the stand will fail worse at speed on a hill. Sort it in the workshop or take it straight to a shop.
    • Check pad WEAR, and replace by wear — not the calendar. Rim pads have molded grooves or a wear line; once those are gone, replace now, because a worn-through pad lets the metal holder gouge your rim. Disc pads should be replaced when the friction material is under about 1 mm — running them to the metal backing scores the rotor.
    • Rim brakes: align the pad to strike the braking track squarely — NOT the tire. A pad set too high will rub or bite the tire sidewall and can blow the tire out at speed. Center the pad on the metal braking surface and set a slight "toe-in" (the front edge touches first) to stop squeal.
    • Disc brakes: never let oil, chain lube, degreaser, or even oily fingers touch the pads or rotor. Contamination is usually permanent — it kills braking and ruins the pads. Handle pads by the metal backing only, and clean the rotor with isopropyl (rubbing) alcohol and a clean rag.
    • Use the correct pad type and compound for your brake and rims. Carbon rims need carbon-specific pads (regular pads can destroy a carbon braking surface); disc brakes use resin/organic or metallic/sintered pads — match what your brake calls for.
    • Bed in new pads, then test at walking pace before any real ride. Fresh pads don't grip fully until they're bedded in with a series of controlled slowing stops. Confirm firm braking at low speed first — and if anything about the job leaves you unsure, have a bike shop do it.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    1. Check the wear

    Rim pads: look for the molded wear grooves or line — once they're gone, replace. Disc pads: pop the wheel out and check the friction-material thickness; under about 1 mm means replace. Either way, a squeal or weak, fading braking is your cue to look.

    2. Remove the old pads

    Rim: release the brake (or the cable), undo the pad bolt, and slide the pad out — note the order of the washers so the new pad goes back the same way. Disc: take the wheel out, remove the retaining pin or clip, and pull the pads and the spreader spring.

    3. Fit the new pads

    Match the type, compound, and orientation. Rim pads are often left/right specific or directional (an arrow or "forward" mark) — get them the right way round. Disc pads seat into the caliper with their spreader spring between them; handle them only by the backing.

    4. Align & set

    Rim: center the pad on the metal braking track — never the tire — with a slight toe-in, and check there's clean clearance above and below. Disc: reinstall the wheel and make sure the rotor runs clean and centered in the caliper, not rubbing on either side.

    5. Bed in & test

    Bed the pads in with a series of repeated, controlled slowing stops so the friction surface mates to the rim or rotor. Confirm firm braking at low speed before any real ride, and re-check the bolts and feel after the first proper ride.

    Signs your pads are due — and when to call a shop

    Good brakes are quiet, firm, and stop the bike without drama. These are the signals the pads are due — and the handful of problems that are a job for a bike shop, not a quick pad swap. The rest of your bike kit pairs with this: tire pressure, the chain, cleaning and lubing it, and the annual tune-up. The car version of the same job is a brake inspection.

    • Squealing or grinding. A new squeal can mean glazed or contaminated pads; a grinding noise usually means the friction material is gone and metal is contacting metal — stop riding and replace now before it ruins the rim or rotor.
    • Weak or spongy braking. If it takes more lever and more distance to slow down than it used to, the pads have thinned (or, on hydraulic discs, there may be air in the line). Either way, don't keep riding it on a hill or in traffic.
    • A lever that pulls to the bar. When the brake lever comes most of the way to the handlebar before it bites, the pads are worn (rim or mechanical disc) or the hydraulic system needs attention — get it sorted before relying on it.
    • Visible wear past the line or under ~1 mm. Rim pads with the grooves worn away, or disc pads down near the metal backing, are simply done. Replace them; there's no adjusting a worn pad back to safe.
    • A contaminated or glazed disc pad. Oil, chain lube, or degreaser on a disc pad (or a shiny, glazed surface) kills braking and usually can't be cleaned out — plan to replace the pads and clean the rotor with isopropyl alcohol.
    • A grooved or worn-through rim. If the braking track is visibly grooved, dished, or has a worn-rim indicator showing, that's beyond pads — a worn rim can fail under braking pressure. See a shop about a new rim or wheel before riding hard.
    • Pro tip: a worn rim, a hydraulic disc brake that needs bleeding, a sticking caliper or piston, or simply anything you're unsure about is a bike-shop job. Brakes are the wrong place to "see if it's fine" — a good mechanic will sort it quickly and you'll ride away confident.

    Common questions

    How do I know when my bike brake pads are worn out?

    Go by wear, and check both how they look and how they feel. On rim brakes, the pad has molded grooves or a wear line in the rubber; once those grooves are worn flat, the pad is done — and if you can see the metal holder approaching the rim, replace it immediately. On disc brakes, pop the wheel out and look at the friction material sitting on the metal backing plate: when it's down to roughly a millimeter or less, it's time. Either type, the feel matters too — if braking has gone weak, the lever pulls further than it used to, or you hear a new squeal or a grind, look at the pads. When in doubt, a thinner-than-a-new-pad pad with a worn or missing wear indicator is your answer: replace it.

    How often should I replace bike brake pads?

    There's no single mileage that fits every rider, because pads wear by use and conditions, not the calendar. A good habit is to inspect them regularly — a quick look every month or two, and any time the brakes feel off — and plan to replace them about once a year as a baseline. But the real trigger is wear: a commuter riding through wet, gritty winters might go through a set or two a year, while someone doing occasional dry, flat rides could go much longer. Steep descents, mud, grit, rain, and heavier loads all wear pads dramatically faster, so let what you actually see on the pads — and how the brakes feel — set your timing, not a number.

    How do rim and disc brake pads differ?

    Rim-brake (caliper) pads are blocks of rubber compound that press against the metal braking track on the wheel's rim; they bolt to the caliper arms, often as left/right or directional pairs, and you read their wear from molded grooves or a wear line. Disc-brake pads are smaller pads of friction material on a metal backing that squeeze a rotor at the hub; they slot into the caliper around the rotor with a retaining pin and a spreader spring, and you judge wear by the thickness of the friction material. Disc pads also come in compounds — resin/organic (quieter, good in the dry) and metallic/sintered (more durable and better in wet, hard use) — so you match the compound to your riding and what the brake supports. The methods differ too: rim work is mostly alignment to the rim and toe-in; disc work is all about keeping the pads and rotor free of any contamination.

    Why are my brakes squealing or feeling weak?

    Squeal and weakness usually trace back to the pads. A squeal can mean the pads are glazed (a hard, shiny surface from heat), contaminated, or — on rim brakes — set without enough toe-in, so the pad chatters against the rim. Weak braking most often means the pads have simply worn thin, so there's less material and less bite; on rim brakes a glazed or worn pad also grips poorly when wet. On disc brakes, weak braking plus a squeal can point to contamination — oil, lube, or degreaser that's soaked into the pad. The fix depends on the cause: re-bed lightly glazed pads, set toe-in on rim brakes, and replace pads that are worn or contaminated. If the lever feels spongy rather than just weak on a hydraulic disc brake, that's likely air in the system and a job for a bleed, not new pads.

    Can I clean oil or contamination off disc brake pads?

    Usually no — contaminated disc pads are best replaced. The friction material is porous, so once oil, chain lube, degreaser, or even oils from your fingers soak in, they're absorbed deep into the pad and keep bleeding back out onto the rotor, killing braking and causing squeal. People try burning it off with heat or scrubbing with alcohol, and it occasionally helps a little, but it rarely restores full, reliable braking — and brakes are the wrong place to gamble. The reliable fix is new pads, plus cleaning the rotor thoroughly with isopropyl (rubbing) alcohol and a clean rag to remove any contamination from it, and bedding the new pads in. The better move is prevention: handle pads only by the metal backing, wear gloves, and keep all lubes and degreasers well away from the rotor and caliper.

    What does "bedding in" new pads mean, and why do it?

    Bedding in (also called burnishing) is the break-in process that mates new pad material to your rim or rotor and lays down an even transfer layer of friction material on the braking surface. Until that's done, fresh pads don't grip at full strength and braking can feel weak or grabby and inconsistent. To bed them in, ride somewhere safe and clear, get up to a moderate speed, then brake firmly and smoothly down to near-walking pace — without fully locking the wheel or skidding — and repeat that controlled stop a number of times (often a dozen or so), letting the brakes cool a little between. You'll feel the braking get progressively stronger and more consistent. Always do this — and a slow walking-pace test of both brakes — before any real ride, and re-check the feel and the bolts after your first proper outing.
