Winterize Your Sprinkler System

Every fall, the water sitting in your irrigation lines gets one freeze away from becoming a crowbar. Blow out the lines before the first hard freeze and the system shrugs off winter; skip it and you find the cracks in April — under pressure, under the lawn. Here's when, how, and exactly what to do.

🗓️ Once a year, before the first hard freeze ⏱️ 30–60 min DIY · ~20 min for a pro 💸 Pro blow-out $75–150 💥 Skipping it risks $500+ repairs

Why it matters

Water expands about 9% when it freezes, and it doesn't negotiate with PVC, poly pipe, or brass. Anything left full — lines, valves, the backflow preventer — splits quietly over winter, then announces itself the first time you pressurize in spring. Here's what one skipped blow-out can cost (typical U.S. estimates):

Cracked backflow preventer

It sits above ground, holds water on both sides, and freezes first. Brass bodies and plastic bonnets split alike — the single most common (and most expensive) casualty of a missed winterization.

$300 – $600 replaced

Burst valve manifold

The cluster of zone valves in the ground box traps water in every chamber. Frozen manifolds crack at the fittings — and one cracked valve usually means rebuilding the whole assembly.

$150 – $400

Split underground lines

Lateral lines at low spots hold water even after a "drain." A split pipe hides until spring, then soaks one patch of lawn — each leak is a locate-dig-splice repair.

$150 – $350 per dig

Cracked heads & risers

Pop-up bodies and the fittings under them hold a cup of water apiece. They're cheap individually — but freezes don't crack just one, and finding them all takes a full walk-through.

$20 – $75 per head, fixed

The spring geyser

Cracks stay invisible until you re-pressurize. The classic spring surprise: turn the main on fast, and a split fitting becomes a geyser — or worse, a silent leak that runs for weeks.

Wasted water + a flooded bed

Spring repair backlog

Every irrigation company spends April fixing the systems that froze in November. Damage found at start-up means weeks of waiting — with the lawn drying out in the meantime.

2 – 4 week waits, peak rates

When — and how — should you winterize?

The job is yearly everywhere it freezes, but the right month and the right method depend on your climate, how your system drains, and what kind of air you can put on it. Three questions:

1. When does your first hard freeze usually arrive?
A hard freeze is roughly 28°F or below for a few hours — cold enough to freeze water in pipes, not just frost on the windshield. Your local extension office publishes average first-freeze dates.
2. How does your system drain?
Most systems installed since the 1990s have no drain valves and are designed to be blown out. Auto-drains open on their own when pressure drops; manual drains are small valves at the low points.
3. If you blow it out yourself, what air can you bring?
For blow-outs, the number that matters is CFM (air volume), not PSI. Check the "CFM @ 90 PSI" rating on the compressor's plate.

    How to blow out your sprinklers

    With the right compressor, the whole job is 30–60 minutes. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can pick it back up if you take a break.

    ⚠️ Safety first — compressed air doesn't compress its temper

    • Regulate the pressure down: stay under ~50 PSI for poly pipe (most cold-climate systems) and under 80 PSI for rigid PVC. A tow-behind compressor can put out far more — set the regulator before you connect.
    • Never run the compressor into a closed system. Always have at least one zone valve open before air flows — dead-heading builds pressure until something bursts.
    • Never stand over heads, the backflow, or open fittings while air is on. Caps, internals, and grit launch with real force. Wear safety glasses.
    • Don't blow air through the backflow preventer. Connect downstream of it and drain the device itself — air pressure and heat damage its seals and check valves.
    • Limit air time per zone to about 2 minutes. Once water stops, compressed air runs hot and can melt poly pipe and head seals. Mist turning to fine fog means stop.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Compressor with real CFM (rented or tow-behind) Pressure regulator + gauge Quick-connect / blow-out adapter Air hose Safety glasses Channel-lock pliers Flat screwdriver (test cocks) Insulated backflow cover or wrap

    📐 Four techniques worth knowing

    Shut off, then drain the backflow

    Close the irrigation supply, then set the backflow's ball valves to 45° and crack the test cocks half a turn. Part-open positions can't trap a sealed slug of water that would split the valve body.

    Regulate down, always

    Blow-outs clear lines with volume (CFM), not pressure. Dial the regulator to ~50 PSI for poly pipe, 80 PSI absolute max for rigid PVC — more pressure doesn't dry pipes faster, it breaks fittings.

    One zone at a time, farthest first

    Open the farthest (or highest) zone, blow until the heads run mist, close it as you open the next. One zone is always open while air flows — that's the rule that keeps fittings intact.

    Put the controller to bed

    Switch it to "rain mode" (or off) so it can't fire valves dry all winter. Rain mode keeps your zone programs in memory; if you cut power entirely, expect to reprogram in spring.

    When to call a pro instead

    • Your compressor can't move enough air — and most can't. A blow-out needs volume (CFM), not pressure (PSI): a typical residential zone wants 10–25 CFM sustained, while a pancake compressor delivers 2–3 CFM no matter what its 150 PSI badge says. It will spit, stall, and leave water in the low spots. Pros tow 85+ CFM machines that clear a zone in a minute.
    • You can't find a blow-out port. If there's no quick-connect or threaded fitting downstream of the backflow, a pro (or an hour of plumbing) needs to add one before anyone can blow the system out properly.
    • Backflow preventer rules. Many municipalities require an annual certified backflow test, and certified testers are usually the same companies doing fall blow-outs — bundling the two visits is the cheap way to stay legal.
    • Well or pump-fed systems, lake draws, or drip-heavy yards. Pump housings, filters, and drip emitters have their own winterizing quirks (and drip lines want much gentler air) — worth a pro the first year, then DIY from their playbook.
    • Typical pro blow-out runs $75–$150; book in September. Watch for neighborhood "blow-out day" pricing — when the truck is already on your street, everyone pays less.

    Common questions

    Can I skip the blow-out if my system has drain valves?

    Auto- and manual-drain systems were designed to empty themselves, and in mild climates that's often enough. But drains only empty what flows downhill to them — water still pools in flat runs, low heads, and valve bodies. That's why the blow-out is the most reliable method everywhere, and the standard recommendation anywhere winters get serious. If you rely on drains, at least open the manual ones, run the auto-drain cycle, and drain the backflow by hand.

    My pancake compressor says 150 PSI. Why isn't that enough?

    Because the blow-out runs on volume, not pressure. Clearing a zone means pushing a steady river of air through hundreds of feet of pipe — that's CFM (cubic feet per minute). A pancake unit stores a small tank at high pressure, dumps it in seconds, then takes minutes to recover, while the water just settles back into the low spots. You need roughly 10+ CFM sustained; rent it or hire it.

    When exactly should I winterize? I don't want to cut the watering season short.

    Aim for the window after your lawn stops needing water but before your average first hard freeze (about 28°F for several hours). Buried lines survive a light overnight frost fine, so don't panic over the first 31°F night — but the above-ground backflow preventer is exposed and freezes first. If an early hard freeze sneaks up before your blow-out, shut off the supply and drain the backflow that evening; the buried pipe can wait a few more days.

    What do I do with the backflow preventer over winter?

    Leave it drained and "cracked open": ball valves at 45°, test cocks open a half turn, so no chamber can trap a sealed slug of water. Then insulate it with a backflow cover or wrap (skip sealing the drain ports). In brutal climates, some owners unscrew the device and store it indoors — fine too, just bag the open pipe ends.

    How do I start the system back up in spring?

    Slowly — this is where winter damage shows itself. Close the test cocks, return the backflow valves to fully open, then open the main shut-off a quarter turn at a time so the lines fill gently (a fast fill causes water hammer that can pop fittings that survived the winter). Once pressurized, run each zone and walk it: look for heads that don't pop, gurgling, soggy spots, or a spinning water meter with all zones off.

    I forgot, and we've already had a hard freeze. Is it too late?

    Winterize anyway, today — one freeze doesn't guarantee damage, but every additional freeze-thaw cycle raises the odds, and the water is still in the lines. Whatever cracked has already cracked; blowing the system out now protects everything that survived. Then plan a careful, slow spring start-up and budget time to find any casualties.

    One freeze date. One job. Don't lose the race.
