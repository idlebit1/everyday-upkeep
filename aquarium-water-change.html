Do an Aquarium Water Change

Your filter does a lot, but there's one thing it can't touch: nitrate. Fish waste turns to ammonia, then nitrite, then nitrate — and that last one just keeps building, invisible and odorless, slowly stressing and poisoning the fish no matter how good your filter is. A regular partial water change is the single most important thing you do for an aquarium: it dilutes that nitrate and the dissolved gunk, and replenishes the minerals old water loses. The trick is doing it right — change only 20–30% at a time, always dechlorinate the new water, match the temperature, and never "deep clean" your way into crashing the whole biological cycle. Done on a rhythm, it's fifteen quiet minutes that keep your fish healthy for years.

🗓️ Typically every 1–2 weeks 💧 Change ~20–30% at a time ⏱️ ~15–20 min per change 💸 Kit ~$45–70 one-time

Why it matters

An aquarium is a closed loop, and everything the fish eat eventually has to go somewhere. The filter handles the most acutely toxic part — but it can't make waste disappear, only convert it. A partial water change is the only thing that actually removes what builds up between cleanings, and doing it on a rhythm is what separates a thriving tank from a slow decline. Here's what's really going on:

Nitrate is the part no filter removes

Fish waste becomes ammonia, then the filter's bacteria turn it to nitrite, then to nitrate. The bacteria handle the first two deadly steps — but nitrate just accumulates, and only a water change takes it out. Let it climb and it quietly stresses, sickens, and slowly kills your fish.

Invisible nitrate poisoning

Fresh water resets the chemistry

Old tank water doesn't just collect waste — it loses the minerals fish need and slowly drifts in pH as it acidifies. Swapping in a fresh batch replenishes those minerals and nudges the water back toward stable, livable conditions before the drift becomes a shock.

Mineral-poor, drifting water

It clears gunk from the gravel

Uneaten food, fish waste, and detritus sink and settle into the gravel, where they rot, foul the water, and feed algae blooms. Vacuuming the substrate during a change pulls that trapped gunk out before it can break down and tip the whole tank's balance.

Rotting gunk & algae blooms

Doing it wrong is worse than skipping

This is the part that catches new keepers. Untreated tap water — or a filter "deep clean" — can wipe out the beneficial bacteria you depend on, triggering a deadly ammonia spike that crashes the whole tank. The method genuinely matters more than the frequency.

A crashed cycle, dead fish

How often should you change the water?

About 20–30% every one to two weeks is the sensible default for most community tanks. But a heavily stocked or small "nano" tank can foul fast and may need a weekly change, while a large, lightly stocked, heavily planted tank can stretch toward monthly. The real arbiter is always your test kit — if nitrate climbs, change more often. Answer four quick questions:

1. How heavily stocked is the tank?
More fish — and especially big, messy eaters like goldfish or cichlids — produce far more waste, so nitrate and gunk build up faster and the water needs refreshing more often.
2. How big is the tank?
A small volume of water swings fast — nitrate concentrates quickly and conditions shift on a dime, so nano tanks need more frequent attention. A large tank is a bigger, more forgiving buffer.
3. Planted and well-filtered?
Live plants actually consume nitrate as fertilizer, and strong filtration keeps more waste in suspension where it can be processed — so a lush, well-filtered tank lets you stretch the interval a little.
4. What does your nitrate test read?
This is the one that overrides everything else. Your liquid test kit tells you the truth about your specific tank — if nitrate is high before your next scheduled change, change more often, full stop.

    How to do an aquarium water change

    The whole job is gentle and unhurried: test the water, ready your gear, siphon out 20–30% while vacuuming the gravel, prepare the replacement water (dechlorinated and temperature-matched), refill slowly, and restart your equipment. Read the fundamentals first — especially "always dechlorinate" and "never nuke the bacteria" — then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Fish-keeping fundamentals — get these right or skip the change

    • Never add untreated tap water. Chlorine and chloramine kill fish and the beneficial bacteria your tank depends on. Always treat the new water with a dechlorinator / water conditioner, dosed for the amount you're adding, before it goes in the tank.
    • Temperature-match the new water. Cold water shocks fish and can kill them. Get the replacement water within a degree or two of the tank — feel it against your wrist, or use a thermometer — before you pour it in.
    • Change only ~20–30% at a time. A huge or 100% change crashes the nitrogen cycle and swings the pH — that's a bigger shock to the fish than slightly dirty water. Small, regular changes keep conditions stable.
    • Don't clean the filter media in tap water or replace it all at once. That's where the beneficial bacteria live. Rinse filter sponges only in old tank water you just removed, gently, and never swap out all the media in one go.
    • Unplug the heater and filter if the water will drop below them. A heater run dry can crack; a filter run dry can burn out. Switch them off before the level drops, and switch them back on only once the water's back up.
    • Use a bucket and tools dedicated to the aquarium. No soap, no household-cleaner residue — even a trace is toxic to fish. Keep your aquarium bucket and siphon for the tank alone, and never wash them with detergent.
    • Test the water to guide the change. Ammonia and nitrite should read 0; nitrate should be low. The readings tell you how much and how often to change for your tank — far better than guessing.
    • Wash your hands before and after, with plain water — no soap or lotion residue going into the tank, and a good rinse afterward.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Test & prep your kit

    Start by testing nitrate (and ideally ammonia and nitrite) so you know what the water actually needs — the reading tells you how much to change. Then lay out a clean, aquarium-only bucket, the gravel siphon, and the dechlorinator within reach.

    Siphon out & vacuum the gravel

    Unplug the heater and filter if the level will drop below them, then start the siphon and push its wide head into the gravel — it pulls up the trapped waste while it drains. Take out only about 20–30% of the tank's volume.

    Prep the new water

    Fill your bucket, add the right dose of dechlorinator for that volume, and bring the water to the tank's temperature — within a degree or two. Feel it against your wrist or check a thermometer. Cold, untreated water is what kills fish.

    Refill gently

    Pour the treated, temperature-matched water back in slowly — onto a plate, a rock, or your hand — so the stream doesn't blast a crater in the substrate or stress the fish. Bring the water back up to its original level.

    Restart & finish

    Plug the heater and filter back in once the water's up. Separately, on its own schedule, rinse filter sponges gently in the old tank water you just removed — never tap water, never all the media at once — to protect the bacteria.

    Signs your tank is overdue — and when to act now

    A healthy tank is quietly clear: bright water, active fish, readable test results. These are the signals it's overdue for a change — and the few that mean something's gone wrong and you need to act right now rather than wait for the next scheduled change:

    • Cloudy or smelly water. Clear, near-odorless water is the baseline. A milky haze or a musty, swampy smell means waste is breaking down faster than the system can handle — do a water change and check your readings.
    • An algae bloom — green water or green film. Algae feeds on the same dissolved nutrients (nitrate and phosphate) that water changes remove. A sudden bloom usually means those nutrients have built up; tighten your change schedule to starve it back.
    • Fish gasping at the surface. Fish hanging at the top gulping air can mean poor oxygenation or, more urgently, toxins in the water. Treat it as a now problem — test immediately and do a change if ammonia or nitrite show up.
    • Clamped fins, lethargy, or hiding. Fins held tight to the body, listlessness, loss of color, or fish that suddenly stop eating are classic stress signs — and chronic high nitrate or a creeping toxin is a common cause. Test and change.
    • Any ammonia or nitrite on the test, or high nitrate. Ammonia and nitrite should always read 0. Any reading there is an emergency — do an immediate partial change to dilute it and protect the fish while you find the cause.
    • A new tank with spiking ammonia or nitrite. If your tank is only weeks old, a spike usually means it's still cycling — the bacteria haven't caught up yet. Read up on "new tank syndrome," keep fish lightly fed, and do small changes to keep toxins survivable while the cycle establishes.
    • Act now: a sudden, whole-tank problem — multiple fish sick at once, a crash you can't explain — is worth a free water test at most local fish stores. Bring a sample; they can spot a chemistry problem your home kit might miss and point you at the fix.
    • It's the same deal as the rest of your pets: you clean the habitat to keep the animal healthy — the very logic behind scooping the litter box and the disease-prevention scrub of a bird feeder. And like every animal in the house, fish do best when the routine care stays on track alongside the annual vet checkup for the furrier members of the family.

    Common questions

    How often should I change my aquarium water?

    For most community tanks, about 20–30% every one to two weeks is the reliable default. Tighten to weekly if your tank is heavily stocked, has big messy fish like goldfish, or is a small "nano" tank where conditions swing fast; stretch toward every couple of weeks (or even monthly) only if it's large, lightly stocked, and heavily planted with strong filtration. But the schedule is just a starting point — your liquid test kit is the real arbiter. If nitrate is climbing high before your next change, change more often, full stop. The whole point of a regular partial change is to keep nitrate and dissolved waste from ever building to a level that stresses the fish, so let the readings, not the calendar, have the final say.

    How much water should I change at once — why not 100%?

    Change about 20–30% at a time, and resist the urge to do more. It feels like a bigger change must be a better clean, but the opposite is true: a huge or 100% change is a shock, not a deep clean. It swings the pH and temperature suddenly, and it can dilute or disturb the beneficial bacteria and the established water chemistry your fish are acclimated to — which stresses them far more than slightly dirty water would. Small, frequent changes keep conditions stable while still steadily removing nitrate. The only times you'd go bigger are genuine emergencies, like a serious ammonia spike, and even then you do it carefully with fully treated, temperature-matched water. For routine upkeep, partial and regular beats big and occasional every time.

    Do I really have to treat tap water first?

    Yes — every single time, with no exceptions. Municipal tap water is treated with chlorine or chloramine to make it safe for people, and both are toxic to fish even at low levels. Worse, they also kill the beneficial bacteria in your filter and gravel that keep the tank cycled — so untreated water can trigger an ammonia spike on top of directly harming the fish. A water conditioner (dechlorinator) neutralizes chlorine and chloramine instantly; you just dose it for the volume of water you're adding, either in the bucket before it goes in or in the tank as you refill. A bottle is inexpensive and lasts a long time. Chloramine in particular won't gas off if you just let water sit out, so don't rely on that old trick — always use a conditioner.

    Can I clean the filter at the same time?

    You can, but do it carefully and never on the same aggressive schedule as a water change, because your filter media is where most of your beneficial bacteria live. The cardinal rules: never rinse filter media in tap water — the chlorine kills the bacteria — and never replace all the media at once. When a sponge or filter pad gets clogged enough to slow the flow, give it a gentle squeeze in a bucket of the old tank water you just siphoned out; that rinses off the gunk while keeping the colony alive. If you have cartridges that "need replacing," swap only one at a time and weeks apart so the bacteria on the others carry the load. Treat the filter as a living thing, not a part you scrub clean — "deep cleaning" it is one of the fastest ways to crash a tank.

    Why is my water cloudy or green even after changes?

    It depends on the color. A milky white or grey cloudiness is usually a bacterial bloom — common in new tanks, after a big disturbance, or when there's excess waste to feed on; it typically clears on its own as the system balances, helped by not overfeeding and keeping up regular changes. Green water is an algae bloom, fed by light plus dissolved nutrients (nitrate and phosphate). The fix there is to cut the fuel: do consistent water changes to lower nutrients, reduce how long the lights are on (and keep the tank out of direct sun), don't overfeed, and consider live plants that compete with the algae. If changes alone aren't enough, test your nitrate and phosphate — persistently high readings mean the underlying buildup is the real problem, and more frequent or slightly larger partial changes are the cure.

    What is the nitrogen cycle, and why do water changes matter?

    The nitrogen cycle is the invisible engine that makes a tank livable. Fish produce waste (and uneaten food rots), which releases ammonia — highly toxic. Beneficial bacteria that colonize your filter and gravel convert that ammonia into nitrite (also toxic), and a second group of bacteria convert nitrite into nitrate (much less toxic). A "cycled" tank is one where those bacteria are established enough to keep ammonia and nitrite at zero. But the cycle has a dead end: nothing in a normal freshwater tank removes nitrate, so it just accumulates. That's exactly why water changes matter — they're the only thing that physically removes the nitrate the cycle leaves behind, while also replenishing minerals and stabilizing pH. The bacteria handle the deadly first steps; the water change handles the leftover. Skip the changes and nitrate climbs unchecked until it slowly harms the fish, which is why the partial change is the single most important maintenance task in the hobby.
