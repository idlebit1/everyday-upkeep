Replace Your String Trimmer Line

There's a specific kind of frustration that comes from firing up the trimmer mid-job and finding a bare, spinning head waving at the weeds — no line, no cutting, nothing. Re-spooling looks like the sort of thing you take to a shop, which is exactly why so many people do. But it's a five-minute job once you know the moves: kill the power so it can't start, use the right line diameter for your head, and wind it on tightly in the arrow's direction without crossing the strands. Do that — or just drop in a pre-wound spool — and you're back to clean edges and a trimmer that feeds line every time you bump it, instead of one collecting dust because the string ran out.

🗓️ Re-spool as needed ⏱️ ~5 min once you know it 🌿 Clean-cutting edges again 💸 Costs ~$5–20

Why it matters

A string trimmer doesn't cut with a blade — it cuts with a piece of nylon line whipping around at a few hundred miles an hour. That line is a consumable: it wears down, snaps off, and runs out, and when it does the trimmer simply stops working. Almost every "my trimmer's broken" complaint is really a line problem in disguise. Here's why keeping fresh, correct line on the spool is the whole game:

No line, no cutting

Worn-down or broken line means you're waving a bare head at the weeds — fast, loud, and cutting nothing. The line is the cutting edge, so the moment it's gone the trimmer is just a noisy stick. Fresh line is what actually cuts.

A loud stick that cuts nothing

The wrong line fights you

Too thick and it bogs the motor and won't feed; too thin and it snaps constantly; the wrong shape doesn't cut clean. Line comes in specific diameters and profiles for a reason — match your trimmer and it just works.

A bogged motor & constant snapping

Old line goes brittle

Nylon dries out and gets brittle from sun and age, so a spool that's been baking in the shed feeds poorly and snaps the instant it touches anything. Old, dry line is half the reason people think their trimmer "won't feed."

Brittle line that snaps on contact

It's a five-minute DIY

Knowing how to re-spool yourself turns a "take it to the shop" errand into a five-minute job in the garage. No appointment, no wait, no markup — just wind on fresh line or drop in a spool, and you're back to cutting.

A shop trip & a stalled chore

How often will you need to re-spool?

Line is a consumable, so there's no fixed schedule — you re-spool whenever it runs out or snaps short, plus a fresh spool to start each season. How fast you burn through it depends entirely on your yard: a big lot with lots of concrete, fence, and gravel edging eats line fast, while a small yard with soft grass edges stretches a spool a long way. Answer four quick questions:

1. How big is the yard — and how often do you trim?
More trimming time means more line worn away and snapped off. A big lot you go over every week burns through string far faster than a small patch you tidy up occasionally.
2. What are you trimming against?
Hardscape is what really eats line — every brush against a sidewalk, a brick wall, a chain-link fence, or a gravel bed shaves the tip down or snaps it. Soft grass edges are gentle by comparison.
3. How's the line behaving right now?
If line snaps the moment it touches anything, feeds reluctantly, or came off a spool that's been baking in the shed for a year, it's gone brittle — re-spool with fresh line rather than fight it.
4. What kind of head does your trimmer have?
A bump- or auto-feed head holds a long wound spool you refill from a bulk roll; a pre-wound head takes a drop-in cartridge; a fixed-line head takes short individual strands. They change how — and how often — you reload.

    How to replace your trimmer line

    The whole job is five short moves: power it down so it can't start, open the head and note how the spool sits, wind fresh line on tightly in the arrow's direction (or drop in a pre-wound spool), thread the ends and reassemble, then test-feed and trim. Read the techniques first — especially powering down safely and matching the line diameter — then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Kill the power first — and match the line

    • Power it down before you touch the head — it can start. Unplug a corded trimmer, remove the battery on a cordless, or disconnect the spark-plug wire on a gas trimmer. A trimmer that fires while your fingers are at the head can take them off — this is non-negotiable.
    • Let the head stop completely and cool. The head keeps spinning after you let go of the trigger, and a gas head runs hot. Wait for it to come to a full stop before you start prying off the cap.
    • Use the line diameter and shape your manual specifies. Common sizes are 0.065", 0.080", and 0.095" — too-thick line strains and overheats the motor and won't feed, too-thin snaps constantly. Match the diameter (and round/twisted/serrated profile) to your trimmer.
    • Wind in the arrow's direction and don't let the strands cross. The spool is stamped with a winding arrow for a reason. Wind tightly and keep the two strands parallel — crossed or tangled line is the number-one cause of a head that won't feed.
    • Store line out of the sun, and revive brittle line in water. UV makes nylon brittle, so keep the spool in a cool, dark spot. If old line snaps the moment it's used, soak it in water for a day to make it pliable again.
    • Gear up before you trim, and keep people and pets clear. Flying line and debris come off the head fast — wear eye protection and long pants with closed shoes, keep bystanders well back, and never bypass or remove the head's guard.

    Fresh line in your trimmer's diameter (or a pre-wound spool) Your trimmer's manual (for the size & head type) Scissors or wire cutters Work gloves Eye protection A clean, flat work surface A bucket of water (to revive brittle line)

    📐 Five moves that make up the whole job

    Power down safely first

    Before anything else, make it impossible to start: unplug a corded trimmer, remove the battery on a cordless, or pull the spark-plug wire on a gas one. Let the head spin down to a full stop and cool. This step is the one that matters most.

    Open the head & note the layout

    Press the two tabs on the sides of the cap and pop it off to lift out the spool. Before it's all the way out, look at how it sits and which way the line ran — a quick mental snapshot makes reassembly foolproof.

    Wind fresh line in the arrow direction

    Cut the length your manual calls for, anchor the end in the spool's notch, and wind it on tightly in the direction of the arrow, keeping the strands parallel and never crossing. Or skip all this and drop in a pre-wound spool.

    Thread the eyelets & reassemble

    Feed each line end out through its eyelet in the head, seat the spool back down on its post, and press the cap until the tabs click home. A firm click means it's seated — a half-seated cap throws the spool the first time you bump it.

    Test-feed, trim & store the rest

    Power back up and bump the head (or let auto-feed run) to release line, letting the guard's cutoff blade trim it to length. Confirm both strands feed, then store the leftover spool somewhere cool and dark so it stays pliable.

    When it's not the line — and when to replace the head

    Most trimmer trouble is solved by fresh, correctly wound line. But sometimes the head itself or the trimmer is the problem, and no amount of re-spooling fixes it. Here's how to tell the difference, and what's worth a new head or a service visit:

    • It won't feed line no matter what you do. First re-check the obvious — crossed or tangled winding and brittle, dried-out line cause most no-feed problems. If the winding's clean and the line's fresh and it still won't advance, a worn feed spring, a stripped bump knob, or a chewed-up eyelet is the culprit — replace the head.
    • The head is cracked or melted. A trimmer head takes a beating against curbs and rocks, and a gas head runs hot. Cracks let the spool wobble or fly out, and a melted cap won't seat — either way it's time for a new head, which usually swaps on in a couple of minutes.
    • The auto-feed mechanism has failed. Bump- and auto-feed heads have small springs and indexing parts that wear out. If it stopped advancing line even after a clean re-spool, the mechanism is done — a replacement head is far cheaper than a whole trimmer.
    • The trimmer bogs, stalls, or won't run. If the motor itself struggles, surges, or won't start — not just the line failing to feed — that's an engine or motor issue: stale fuel and a clogged carburetor on a gas unit, or a tired battery or motor on an electric. Time for a tune-up or service.
    • You're constantly fighting the wrong line. If line snaps or bogs the motor no matter how carefully you wind it, double-check the diameter and shape against the manual before blaming the trimmer — using line that's too thick for the head is a surprisingly common, fixable mistake.
    • Pro tip: keep a backup spool (or a couple of pre-wound cartridges) on the shelf and a spare head for older trimmers. The whole point of learning to re-spool is never being stopped mid-job again — a backup on hand is what makes that true.

    Common questions

    How do I replace or wind string trimmer line?

    Five steps. First, make the trimmer impossible to start — unplug a corded model, remove the battery on a cordless, or disconnect the spark-plug wire on a gas one — and let the head stop and cool. Second, press the tabs and pop the cap off to remove the spool, noting how it sits. Third, either wind fresh line of the correct diameter onto the spool: anchor the end in the notch and wind it on tightly in the direction of the arrow stamped on the spool, keeping the two strands parallel; or just drop in a pre-wound replacement spool. Fourth, thread the line ends out through the eyelets, seat the spool, and click the cap back on. Fifth, power up, bump the head to feed line, and trim it to the guard's cutoff. The whole thing takes about five minutes once you've done it once.

    What size and type of line do I need?

    Use the diameter and shape your trimmer's manual specifies — that's the single most important spec. Diameter is measured in inches (or millimeters), with common sizes being 0.065" for light-duty electric trimmers, 0.080" and 0.095" for general residential use, and thicker still for heavy gas units. Going too thick strains the motor, overheats it, and often won't feed through the eyelets; too thin snaps constantly. Shape matters too: round line is durable and cheap, twisted or multi-sided (square, star, serrated) profiles cut a bit cleaner and faster but wear quicker. When in doubt, match exactly what the manual lists, or read the size off the old spool or the head itself.

    Why won't my trimmer feed line?

    Three causes account for nearly all no-feed problems. The most common is crossed or overlapped winding — if the two strands cross each other on the spool, they bind and jam instead of unspooling smoothly; rewind it neatly with the strands kept parallel. Second is brittle, dried-out line: old nylon that's been baking in the sun gets stiff and snaps off short before it can feed, so it never advances; replace it with fresh line (and soak any brittle line in water for a day to revive it). Third is worn parts — a tired feed spring, a stripped bump knob, or a chewed eyelet on a bump- or auto-feed head. If the winding is clean and the line is fresh and it still won't feed, the head's mechanism has worn out and is worth replacing.

    Can I use any line in any trimmer?

    No — and this trips a lot of people up. Trimmer line comes in specific diameters, and your trimmer is built around one range. Line that's too thick won't feed through the eyelets and forces the motor to work harder than it was designed to, which overheats and prematurely wears it; line that's too thin for a powerful head snaps almost immediately. The shape matters less for compatibility but affects how it cuts and wears. The safe move is to match the diameter (and ideally the profile) the manufacturer specifies. The good news is that "the right line" is cheap and widely available — there's no reason to make do with the wrong size.

    How do I keep line from breaking so often?

    Start with the right diameter — undersized line for your trimmer snaps constantly, so confirm the size against the manual first. Then look after the line itself: nylon goes brittle from UV and age, so store spare line in a cool, dark place rather than letting it bake in the sun, and if a spool has already gone stiff, soak it in a bucket of water for a day before use to make it pliable again. Technique helps too — trim with just the tip of the line rather than burying the whole string into thick growth or grinding it against concrete and rock, which is what shaves and snaps it fastest. Right size, fresh and pliable, and a lighter touch against hard edges: that's most of the battle.

    Bump-feed, fixed-line, or pre-wound spools — what's the difference?

    They're three ways a head holds and releases line. A bump-feed (and the similar auto-feed) head carries a long spool you wind from a bulk roll; you tap the head on the ground to advance more line as it wears, which makes a bulk spool by far the cheapest way to go. A pre-wound spool head takes a drop-in cartridge already loaded with line — costlier per refill but about as easy as it gets, since there's no winding at all. A fixed-line head holds a few short, individual pieces of line you push into place by hand; there's no spool to wind, just replace each strand as it wears. Knowing which one you have tells you whether to buy a bulk roll, cartridges, or pre-cut strands — and how the reload goes.
