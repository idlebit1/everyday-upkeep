Wash Your Duvet or Comforter

The cover gets washed with the sheets, so the big fluffy insert underneath is easy to forget — for years. But even tucked inside a cover, a duvet or comforter slowly drinks up sweat, body oils, skin cells, and allergens, until it sleeps flat and smells faintly stale. Good news: most down and synthetic comforters wash beautifully, at home or at the laundromat. The catch is size and drying — cram it into a small machine or pull it out even a little damp, and the fill clumps. Here's how to do it right, about twice a year.

🗓️ Wash every ~6 months 🧺 Use a large-capacity machine 🌀 Dry fully or the fill clumps 💸 About $0–5 at home

Why it matters

A duvet cover is a duvet's washable armor, and it does catch most of the daily wear — which is exactly why people assume the insert never needs washing. It does. A little soaks past the cover every night, and over months it adds up. Here's what builds in a comforter that's never actually been washed:

Sweat & oils seep past the cover

Cotton covers breathe, and they have to — which means moisture, body oils, and skin cells pass right through to the fill over months. The cover takes the brunt, but the insert quietly absorbs the rest, where a wash of the cover alone never reaches.

Stale, saturated fill

Dust mites & allergens settle in

The warm, slightly damp loft of a comforter is prime real estate for dust mites, which feed on shed skin cells. Their droppings are a leading year-round trigger for allergies and asthma — and a comforter is draped over you all night, every night.

Stuffy nose & flares

Clumping that ruins the loft

Oils and residue mat down clusters and synthetic fibers together, so the fill drifts into lumps with thin, cold patches between them. The right wash-and-dry method clears that residue and re-fluffs the fill — protecting the loft instead of wrecking it.

Lumpy, uneven warmth

A comforter that simply sleeps better

A clean, fully dried, well-fluffed comforter lofts higher, breathes better, and smells like nothing at all — which is exactly what you want against your face for eight hours. Skip washing for years and the fill saturates and flattens early, retiring it before its time.

Replaced years early

How often should you wash it?

Twice a year — roughly every 6 months — is the sensible default for the insert itself, because the cover is doing the daily work. But no cover, allergies, or a hot sleeper all tighten that toward every 3 months, and the fill type changes how you wash. Answer three quick questions:

1. What's the fill? (Check the care label if you're not sure.)
2. Do you use a duvet cover over it?
3. Allergies, or a hot / heavy-sweating sleeper?

    How to wash your duvet or comforter

    Mostly machine time — the hands-on part is about ten minutes, plus a long, patient dry that's the real key to getting it right. Read the techniques first, then work the checklist; your progress saves automatically, so a slow-drying comforter can span the whole afternoon.

    ⚠️ Read this before the comforter goes near the machine

    • Check the care label first — it outranks this guide. Most down, feather, and synthetic/polyester comforters are machine-washable. Wool, silk, or very large down comforters may need a commercial machine or dry cleaning — the label settles it.
    • Use a LARGE-capacity machine. A comforter crammed into a small home washer won't rinse or spin evenly — it just gets wet, not clean, and can throw the drum off balance. A laundromat's big-bin machine is often the best (and cheapest-by-the-hour) choice.
    • Gentle cycle, mild detergent, extra rinse. Use a small amount of mild detergent on a gentle/delicate cycle, then add a second rinse — trapped soap is what leaves fill stiff and matted.
    • Dry on LOW heat with dryer balls, and be patient. Low heat plus wool or tennis balls keeps the fill tumbling and lofting instead of clumping. Pull it out periodically to break up clumps and redistribute the fill by hand.
    • It MUST be bone-dry inside before use. A core that's even slightly damp clumps and grows mildew within days — down especially. Drying a comforter takes far longer than you expect; don't rush it.

    🧰 What you'll need

    A large-capacity washer (no center agitator) Mild detergent (a small amount) A large dryer Wool dryer balls or clean tennis balls Stain pretreater (optional) A trip to the laundromat (for oversized comforters) The care label (actually read it)

    📐 Five techniques worth knowing

    Check the label, spot-treat stains

    "Machine wash" covers most down and synthetic comforters; "dry clean only" usually means wool, silk, or special fills. Pretreat any visible stains with a dab of mild detergent before the whole thing goes in — heat sets stains.

    Wash alone in a big machine

    Wash it by itself in a large-capacity machine so there's room to move — gentle/warm cycle (or as the label says) with only a small dose of mild detergent. Too tight a fit and it never rinses clean; too much soap and the fill stiffens.

    Run a second rinse

    Thick fill traps detergent deep inside, and trapped soap dries stiff and attracts grime. An extra rinse cycle flushes it out so the comforter comes back soft and clean — well worth the few extra minutes.

    Dry low, pause to fluff

    Low heat plus two or three wool dryer balls (or clean tennis balls) keeps the fill tumbling so it dries evenly and re-lofts instead of clumping. Expect several cycles — pause each one to pull the comforter out and break up clumps by hand.

    Confirm it's dry to the core

    A comforter can feel dry on the surface while the center is still damp — and a damp core clumps and mildews fast. Squeeze the middle and the thick seams: any coolness, dampness, or lump means run another low cycle, then air it out before it goes back on the bed.

    When to replace instead of wash

    • The fill is permanently clumped. If you wash, dry with dryer balls, fluff by hand, and it still comes out lumpy with cold, flat patches that won't redistribute, the clusters or fibers have broken down. That's structural, not dirt, and no wash fixes it.
    • Stains or odor survive a proper wash. Light aging is normal. Deep, permanent staining or a musty smell that's still there after a full wash-and-dry means the fill is saturated past saving.
    • It's gone flat with no loft. A comforter that lies thin and lifeless, with no warmth or spring left even after washing and fluffing, is done — the loft that made it warm is gone.
    • It rips and leaks fill. A seam or shell that's splitting will spill down or fiberfill every time you move. A small tear can be mended; a shell breaking down in several places means it's time to replace it.
    • It survived a mildew episode. A comforter put away damp and now smelling of mildew at the core is very hard to rescue. Replace it, and next time dry it bone-dry before it goes anywhere near the bed.

    Common questions

    How often should I actually wash a duvet or comforter?

    The insert itself only needs washing about twice a year — roughly every 6 months — as long as you use a duvet cover, which takes the daily wear. Wash it more often (every 3 months or so) if you don't use a cover, if you have allergies, or if you're a hot or heavy-sweating sleeper. The removable duvet cover is a different story: wash that often, on the same weekly-to-biweekly rhythm as the rest of your sheets.

    Can I machine wash a down comforter?

    Usually yes — most down and feather comforters are machine-washable if the care label allows it. The two rules that matter: use a large-capacity machine so the down can move and rinse freely (a front-loader or any drum without a center agitator), and dry it completely. Use a small amount of mild or down-safe detergent on a gentle cycle, add an extra rinse, and budget a few hours of low-heat drying with dryer balls. Down that's put away even slightly damp clumps and mildews, so the drying is the part you can't shortcut.

    Home machine or laundromat — which is better?

    It comes down to capacity. If your home machine is large enough that the comforter fills the drum no more than about two-thirds and still has room to tumble, home is great and nearly free. If you have to cram it in, take it to a laundromat: their oversize big-bin washers and large dryers are built for exactly this, and the few dollars buys a cleaner wash and a faster, more even dry than fighting a stuffed home machine. Wool, silk, or very large down comforters often need that commercial machine — or dry cleaning.

    How do I dry a comforter without it clumping?

    Low heat, lots of patience, and dryer balls. Two or three wool dryer balls (or clean tennis balls) keep the fill tumbling and breaking apart instead of settling into lumps. Run multiple low-heat cycles rather than one long hot one — high heat can scorch synthetic fill and damage down. Between cycles, pull the comforter out and shake and knead it to redistribute the fill, break up any clumps, and feel for wet spots. Don't stop until the center and the thick seams squeeze bone-dry.

    Do I still need to wash the comforter if I use a cover?

    Less often — but yes. A duvet cover catches most of the sweat, oils, and skin cells, which is why the insert can stretch to about twice a year instead of every few months. But a little soaks through every night, so the insert still needs a deep refresh now and then. The cheapest, easiest split: wash the cover frequently with your sheets, and wash the insert itself roughly every 6 months.

    How do I re-fluff a flat comforter?

    Tumble it on low (or an air/no-heat cycle) for 15–20 minutes with two or three dryer balls — the tumbling redistributes the fill and lifts the loft, and the gentle warmth drives off absorbed moisture. Giving it a good shake and draping it over a couple of chairs to air out in the sun helps too. If it's flat because the fill is genuinely broken down and won't bounce back even after washing and fluffing, that's a replacement signal, not a fluffing one.
