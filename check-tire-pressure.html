Check Tire Pressure

Four palm-sized patches of rubber are all that connect your car to the road, and they work right only at the right pressure. Tires quietly lose air every month whether you drive or not. Here's how often to check, why five minutes matters — and exactly how to do it.

🗓️ Check every month ⏱️ 5 minutes for all four 🧰 A $10 gauge is all you need 💸 Low tires waste $50–100/yr in fuel

Why it matters

Underinflation is invisible — a tire can be 25% low and look perfectly normal. But it's quietly costing you money and grip the whole time (typical U.S. cost ranges):

Blowouts

Underinflation is the #1 tire-related factor in crashes. A soft tire flexes, overheats, and can fail outright at highway speed — exactly when you least want it to.

Tow + tire: $300 – $800

Shoulder wear

A low tire rides on its outer edges instead of its full face, eating the shoulders early. That can cut tire life by 25% or more — replacing a set years ahead of schedule.

$600 – $1,200 per set

Wasted fuel

Soft tires drag. Fuel economy drops about 0.2% for every 1 PSI a tire is low — and most cars run several PSI low on all four. It adds up to 2–3% of every tank.

$50 – $100 / year

Longer stops, mushy handling

An underinflated tire squirms instead of gripping — braking distances stretch and steering goes vague, especially in rain. Pressure is the cheapest handling upgrade there is.

Free fix, real margin

Air leaves on its own

Tires naturally seep about 1 PSI per month through the rubber itself — plus another 1 PSI for every 10°F the temperature drops. A tire set in summer is seriously low by November.

~1 PSI lost / month

How often should you check?

"Once a month" is the rule of thumb — and it's a good one. But your climate, your driving, and what your dashboard has been telling you can shift it. Answer three questions:

1. What's your climate like?
2. How much do you drive?
3. How often does your TPMS warning light come on?

    How to check your tire pressure

    Five minutes, no jack, no grease. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can pick it back up if you get interrupted.

    🌡️ Three rules that make or break the reading

    • Check cold. Measure before you drive, or after the car has sat 3+ hours. Driving heats the air inside and inflates the reading by several PSI.
    • Use the door-jamb placard number, NOT the sidewall. The placard on the driver's door jamb is what your car needs. The "MAX PSI" molded into the tire is the tire's ceiling — not a target.
    • Don't forget the spare. A flat spare is just trunk ballast. Compact spares often need 60 PSI — check it a few times a year.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Tire pressure gauge (digital or pencil, ~$10) Portable inflator or gas-station air Tread depth gauge or a penny (optional) Phone or notebook to log readings

    📐 Three things worth knowing

    Placard, not sidewall

    The sticker in the driver's door jamb lists the pressure your car was engineered for — often different front vs. rear. The number on the tire is a maximum, not a goal.

    Press straight, seal fast

    Push the gauge squarely onto the valve stem in one firm motion until the hiss stops. A brief psst is fine; an ongoing hiss means it's crooked — re-seat and read again.

    Bonus: the penny test

    While you're down there, stick a penny head-down in a tread groove. If you can see all of Lincoln's head, you're at ~2/32" — legally worn out and due for tires.

    When to see a tire shop

    • One tire is repeatedly low while the others hold steady. That's a slow leak — usually a nail or screw in the tread, or a corroded valve stem. Patches typically run $20–$45, and many shops fix punctures free on tires they sold.
    • Sidewall damage: bulges, bubbles, gouges, or cracking. Sidewalls can't be patched — a bulge is a structural failure waiting for a pothole.
    • The TPMS light stays on after filling (or flashes at startup). A flashing light usually means a dead sensor battery or a system fault — a shop can read which sensor and why.
    • Uneven wear patterns: one shoulder worn bald, feathering, or cupping. Pressure alone doesn't cause those — you likely need an alignment or rotation (typically $75–$200).

    Common questions

    The sidewall says 51 PSI but my door jamb says 35. Which is right?

    The door jamb, always. The sidewall number is the maximum pressure the tire can safely hold — a ceiling set by the tire maker. The placard is the pressure your specific car was engineered to ride, brake, and wear best at. Filling to the sidewall max gives you a harsh ride, a smaller contact patch, and rapid center wear.

    Is nitrogen fill worth paying for?

    For everyday driving, mostly no. Nitrogen molecules do seep out slightly more slowly than plain air (which is already 78% nitrogen), but the difference is small — you still need to check monthly either way. If it's free with your tires, enjoy it; don't pay much for it, and never skip a top-up of regular air because you "have nitrogen."

    How accurate are gas station gauges?

    Treat them as rough. Public air-machine gauges get dropped, frozen, and abused, and they're commonly off by several PSI. Use the station's hose for air but your own $10 gauge for the reading — a decent digital gauge stays within about 1 PSI of true.

    Should I run different pressure in winter?

    Same placard number — but you'll need to add air to hold it. Pressure drops about 1 PSI per 10°F, so a tire set on a 90°F afternoon can be 5–6 PSI low on a 30°F morning. When the first real cold snap hits, expect all four tires (and the TPMS light) to ask for attention at once. Set them cold to the placard number and recheck after big temperature swings.

    Can I drive on a slightly low tire?

    A few PSI low to reach an air pump? Fine — drive gently and fill soon. But once the TPMS light is on, you're roughly 25% under, and that's real territory: more heat, less grip, longer stops. Visibly squatting or flat? Don't drive on it at all — even a short roll can destroy the sidewall and turn a $30 patch into a $200 tire.
