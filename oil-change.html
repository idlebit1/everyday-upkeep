Oil Change

Oil is the only thing keeping your engine's moving metal from grinding itself to dust — and it wears out whether you drive a lot or barely at all. Here's how often to change it (in miles and months), why it matters, and exactly how to do it.

🗓️ Typically 5,000 mi or 6 months ⏱️ 30–45 min DIY 💵 $35–50 in materials 💸 A seized engine runs $4,000–$10,000

Why it matters

Oil doesn't just lubricate — it cools, cleans, and seals. As it ages it turns acidic, fills with combustion gunk, and stops doing all four jobs at once. These are the repairs a $50 oil change prevents (typical U.S. cost ranges):

Sludge buildup

Old oil oxidizes into tar-like sludge that blocks the tiny passages oil travels through. Once it forms, flushing it out is a teardown job, not a drain-and-fill.

$600 – $4,000

Engine wear

Worn-out oil is full of abrasive grit and too thin to keep metal apart. Bearings, cam lobes, and cylinder walls wear years ahead of schedule.

$1,500 – $4,500

Overheating

Oil carries away a surprising share of engine heat. Degraded or low oil means hot spots, warped heads, and cooked gaskets.

$1,000 – $3,000

Turbo damage

Turbochargers spin up to 200,000 rpm on a thin film of oil. They're the first thing dirty oil kills — and they're not cheap to replace.

$1,500 – $4,000

Voided warranty

Skipped or undocumented oil changes are the classic reason engine warranty claims get denied. Keep receipts — or a log with dates and mileage.

Claim denied: $0 covered

Worst case: seized engine

Run long enough on dead or no oil and the bearings weld themselves to the crank. At that point you're buying an engine, not a repair.

$4,000 – $10,000+

How often should you change it?

"Every 5,000 miles" is the rule of thumb, but the right interval depends on your oil, your driving, and your conditions — and it's always two numbers: miles or months, whichever comes first. Answer three questions for your personal schedule:

1. What oil goes in your engine?
2. What does most of your driving look like?
3. What conditions does the car live in?

    How to change your oil

    A DIY oil change takes 30–45 minutes once you've done it twice. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can pick it back up if you take a break.

    ⚠️ Safety first — a jack is for lifting, never for holding

    • Never get under a car held only by a jack. Lift it, then set it on rated jack stands at the factory jack points — and give the car a firm shake before you slide under.
    • Work on flat, solid ground (concrete, not gravel or grass), set the parking brake, and chock the wheels staying on the ground.
    • Warm oil drains best; hot oil burns. A 2–3 minute idle is plenty — if you just drove, let it cool 20–30 minutes first.
    • Torque the drain plug, don't gorilla it. Most plugs spec around 25–30 ft-lb; over-tightening strips the oil pan, an expensive way to save oil.
    • Keep old oil off your skin and off the ground — gloves on, drips on cardboard, everything into a sealed container for recycling.

    Correct oil (check the manual) New oil filter New drain-plug washer Drain pan Socket or box wrench Filter wrench Floor jack + jack stands Wheel chocks Funnel Nitrile gloves & rags Sealable jug for old oil

    📐 Three things worth knowing before you slide under

    Stands hold, jacks lift

    Lift at the factory jack point, set a rated stand under the pinch weld or frame, lower onto it, and shake the car hard before going under. The jack stays as backup only.

    Find the plug and the filter first

    The drain plug is the lowest bolt on the oil pan; the filter is a spin-on canister nearby. Place the drain pan offset a few inches — the first gush arcs outward.

    Read the dipstick right

    Check on level ground, engine off a few minutes. Pull, wipe, reinsert fully, pull again. Anywhere between the marks is fine; MIN to MAX is roughly one quart.

    When to let a shop do it

    • No safe place to work. Street parking, a sloped driveway, gravel, or grass — if you can't set jack stands on flat concrete, this isn't your job today.
    • Leased car or active warranty. DIY is allowed, but a shop's stamped invoice is bulletproof paperwork if a warranty claim ever comes up. If you DIY, keep receipts and log every change with date and mileage.
    • Stuck drain plug or stripped threads. If the plug won't budge or turns without biting, stop — forcing it can crack the pan. A shop can extract it and fit an oversize plug or new pan.
    • A previous shop over-torqued the filter or plug. Annoyingly common. Don't fight a filter with a screwdriver through it on your back; let a lift and a pro handle it.
    • Big skid plates or zero clearance. Some cars hide the plug behind a dozen fasteners. If access is the whole job, the shop's lift earns its fee.
    • Typical shop pricing: quick-lube $50–$90, dealer $80–$150 (synthetic at the high end) — most include a basic inspection and top-offs.

    Common questions

    Is full synthetic actually worth the extra money?

    Usually, yes. Synthetic costs $15–30 more per change but resists heat breakdown and cold-start thickening far better, and safely stretches the interval to 7,500–10,000 miles — so the per-mile cost often comes out even or better. For turbocharged engines, extreme climates, or short-trip driving, it's strongly recommended; many newer engines require it outright.

    Can I trust my car's oil-life monitor?

    Mostly. Modern monitors track real operating data (temperatures, trip lengths, revs) and are reasonably good at the miles side of the question. They're weaker on the months side — a car that barely drives can show "70% oil life" on oil that's been collecting moisture for a year. Use the monitor, but still change at least every 12 months, and check the dipstick monthly regardless.

    My oil is low — can I just top it off instead of changing it?

    Top off now, but don't confuse the two jobs. Adding oil fixes the level; changing oil fixes the condition. Fresh oil poured into old, acidic, gritty oil dilutes the problem without removing it, and the filter is still full. If you're topping off more than a quart between changes, also get the consumption or leak looked at.

    What weight (grade) of oil do I need?

    Whatever the owner's manual or the oil filler cap says — commonly something like 0W-20 or 5W-30. The numbers describe viscosity cold (the "W" number) and hot; modern engines are built around a specific grade, and tolerances are tight enough that "close" isn't good enough. Match the grade and the listed specification (API/ILSAC, or the maker's own standard) exactly.

    Where do I get rid of the old oil?

    Never down a drain or in the trash — one oil change can foul a million gallons of water. Pour it into a sealed jug (the new oil's container works) and take it, plus the old filter, to any auto parts store (AutoZone, O'Reilly, Advance), many quick-lubes, or your municipal hazardous-waste site. Nearly all take it free; it gets re-refined.
