Replace Your Contact Case & Solution

You replace your lenses on schedule and wash your hands like you're supposed to — and then you drop those clean lenses into the same little case you've been using since who-knows-when, topped off with whatever solution was left from last night. That case is the forgotten half of contact hygiene, and it's the germiest part of the whole routine: within a few weeks it grows a bacterial biofilm that fresh solution can't fully clear, and a grimy case is one of the leading sources of serious, sight-threatening eye infections. The fix costs almost nothing. Swap in a new case about every three months — a fresh one comes free with most solution bottles — empty it and refill with fresh solution every single time, never top off, never use water, and let it air-dry open. Do that, and the case stops undoing everything else you're getting right.

🗓️ Case every ~3 months 🦠 A case grows biofilm in weeks 💧 Fresh solution, never topped off 💸 A case is ~$1–3 (often free)

Why it matters

You can do everything else right — replace your lenses on time, wash your hands, never sleep in them — and still hand microbes a straight path to your eye through a neglected case and reused solution. The case is where the trouble hides, because it's the one part of the routine nobody thinks to replace. Here's what an old case and tired solution are actually doing. This is general information, not medical or eye-care advice — your eye doctor guides your routine.

The case is an infection reservoir

Within weeks, a contact case grows a bacterial biofilm — a slimy microbial layer on its inner walls that fresh solution alone can't fully clear. A grimy, old case is a leading source of serious eye infections, so it quietly undoes every bit of your other lens hygiene.

A biofilm that re-seeds every lens

"Topping off" solution is dangerous

Adding a splash of fresh solution to the old, used solution in your case dilutes what disinfecting power is left and lets resistant microbes breed in the leftover mix. Solution disinfects once, then it's spent — so you must empty the case and refill with fresh solution every time.

A weak, contaminated mix

Water and saliva are never OK

Tap, well, distilled, or bottled water — and saliva — can carry Acanthamoeba, a microbe that causes a rare but devastating, notoriously hard-to-treat eye infection. Never rinse or store your lenses or case in water. Only ever contact-lens solution touches your lenses.

Acanthamoeba keratitis

Solution expires too

An opened bottle has a "discard X days after opening" window and a printed expiration date, and expired or old solution simply doesn't disinfect the way it should. Check both dates, and don't dose fresh lenses with a bottle that's past its time.

Solution that no longer disinfects

How often should you replace the case?

The short answer is about every three months, and a fresh case usually comes free with each new solution bottle — so this is really about which habits to tighten and how urgently. If you store reusable lenses in the case every night, this routine matters most; if you've been topping off solution or nursing an old case, that's the risky habit to fix today. Answer four quick questions. Whatever it says, your eye doctor's guidance rules.

1. What kind of lenses do you wear?
If you store reusable two-week or monthly lenses in the case every night, the case is in constant contact with your lenses and matters most. Daily-disposable wearers still keep a case for backups or rewetting — just used far less.
2. Be honest about your solution and case habits
Topping off old solution and keeping a tired, biofilm-coated case are the two habits that quietly undo everything else. If that's you, this is the one to fix today.
3. How old — and how clean — is your current case?
Biofilm builds up no matter how well you rinse, so age alone is reason to swap. A cloudy, filmy, or cracked case is well overdue — and a fresh one usually costs nothing.
4. What kind of solution do you use?
Multipurpose cleans, disinfects, and stores in one step. Hydrogen-peroxide systems disinfect powerfully but need their special case and a full neutralization time — never put un-neutralized peroxide in your eye. Saline only rinses; it does not disinfect.

    The cost of skipping it

    This is one of the most lopsided cost comparisons in all of personal care. A contact case is genuinely cheap — a dollar or three, and usually free, since a new one comes tucked in the box with most solution bottles. Solution runs about $8 to $15 a bottle. Set that against an eye infection: an urgent visit, medicated drops, days you can't see straight, and — at the far end — the risk of lasting vision damage. Replacing the case is the cheapest insurance there is.

    How to replace and care for your case and solution

    There are really two habits here: swap in a fresh case about every three months, and use fresh solution the right way in between — emptied and refilled every time, never topped off, never water. Read the safety box first, because this is the part that protects your sight, then work through the techniques and the checklist. Your progress saves automatically. And keep up the rest of your replace-on-schedule care while you're at it — this is the companion to your contact lens replacement schedule, and eye hygiene ties to tossing old mascara and cleaning your makeup brushes.

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Replace the case quarterly

    Swap in a fresh case about every three months — even a clean-looking one grows biofilm no rinse fully removes. Don't buy special: a new case usually comes free in the box with each solution bottle, so keep the spare and retire the old one on schedule.

    Empty & refill fresh

    Dump the used solution and fill the case with fresh solution every single time — never top off. Solution disinfects once and is then spent, so adding a splash to the old mix just dilutes it and breeds resistant microbes.

    Clean & air-dry the case

    After use, empty the case, rinse it with fresh solution — never water — and wipe it. Then store it open and upside-down on a clean tissue so it fully air-dries. A closed, wet case is a breeding ground; a dry one starves the bacteria out.

    Use solution right

    Wash and dry your hands first, check the bottle's expiration and its discard-after-opening date, and don't let the tip touch your fingers, the case, or the counter — one touch can contaminate the whole bottle. And never, ever use water or saliva.

    Peroxide systems

    If you use hydrogen peroxide, always use its special case with the neutralizing disc, and give it the full neutralization time. Never put un-neutralized peroxide in your eye — it burns — and never swap peroxide into a regular case. Log the case swap either way.

    Signs to act — and when to see your eye doctor

    A healthy case-and-solution routine is quiet: a clean, dry case, fresh solution, comfortable eyes. These are the signals it's time to swap the case, tighten a habit, or get eye care — some of them urgently. Your eye doctor guides your lens-and-solution routine throughout; this list is about knowing when to reach for them.

    • A red, painful, light-sensitive, watery, or blurry eye — take the lens out and get eye care promptly. Along with a feeling that something's stuck even after you've removed the lens, these can be early signs of a serious eye infection, including the kind a contaminated case or water can cause. Don't wait it out.
    • A grimy, cloudy, cracked, or old case. If the case looks filmy or is more than about three months old, it's overdue — biofilm is there whether you can see it or not. Swap in the fresh one from your last solution bottle and log the date.
    • Recurring irritation or "gritty" eyes with fresh lenses. If new lenses still feel off, a contaminated case or spent solution is a prime suspect. Replace the case, switch to fresh solution every time, and if it continues, consult your eye doctor.
    • You've been topping off, or you're not sure your solution is right. Topping off and using saline or an expired bottle to disinfect are common mistakes worth correcting today — and worth confirming your product and routine with your eye doctor.
    • You use hydrogen peroxide and are unsure about the case or timing. Peroxide is only safe with its special case and the full neutralization time. If you're ever unsure whether a lens is neutralized, don't put it in — ask your eye doctor.
    • Bottom line: your eye doctor guides your lens, case, and solution routine, and sets your schedule. When something feels off, defer to them — it's your sight, and it's not worth a guess.

    Common questions

    How often should I replace my contact case?

    About every three months, even if it looks clean. Cases quietly grow a biofilm — a slimy layer of microbes on their inner surfaces — that fresh solution alone won't fully remove, and that film can recontaminate clean lenses every night. A grimy case is one of the leading sources of serious eye infections, so age itself is the reason to swap; don't wait for it to look dirty. The good news is it costs almost nothing: a brand-new case comes free in the box with most solution bottles, so keep the spare and retire the old one on a regular schedule. Between uses, empty the case, rinse it with fresh solution, wipe it, and stand it open and upside-down on a clean tissue to air-dry. This is general information — follow your eye doctor's guidance.

    Can I "top off" old contact solution?

    No — never. "Topping off" means adding a splash of fresh solution to the used solution already sitting in your case, and it's one of the most dangerous shortcuts in lens care. Solution disinfects once and is then spent, so the old solution in the case is already contaminated with whatever it pulled off your lenses and case. Adding fresh solution just dilutes what little disinfecting power remains and gives resistant microbes a place to breed — you end up storing your lenses in a weak, dirty mix. The rule is simple and non-negotiable: empty the case completely and refill it with fresh solution every single time, then dump that solution out again after you take your lenses out. Solution is single-use, every use.

    Can I use water to rinse my case or lenses?

    Never — not tap, well, distilled, or bottled water, and not saliva. Water can carry Acanthamoeba, a microbe found in tap water, pools, hot tubs, and soil that causes Acanthamoeba keratitis — a rare but devastating eye infection that's notoriously painful, hard to treat, and can threaten your sight. Only ever use contact-lens solution to rinse or store your lenses and your case. This is also why you shouldn't shower, swim, or use a hot tub in your lenses; if lenses must stay in around water, wear tight goggles, and many eye doctors suggest daily disposables you can toss right after. If water ever does contact your lenses, don't put them back in — clean or replace them per your eye doctor's guidance.

    How should I clean and store the case?

    Keep it simple and dry. After you put your lenses in, dump the leftover solution out — don't leave lenses stewing in used solution longer than needed, and never let old solution sit for the next round. When you take the lenses out, empty the case again, rinse it with fresh solution (never water), rub or wipe the wells with a clean, lint-free tissue if you can, and then stand the case open and upside-down on a clean tissue to air-dry. Air-drying is the part people skip and it matters most: a closed, wet case is a warm, still place for bacteria to multiply, while a dry case starves them out. Then swap in a brand-new case about every three months, since biofilm builds up no matter how carefully you clean.

    Does contact solution expire?

    Yes, in two ways, and both matter. First, every bottle has a printed expiration date — past it, the preservatives and disinfecting agents break down and the solution no longer does its job. Second, and easier to overlook, most solutions have a "discard X days after opening" window — often 90 days — printed on the label or symbolized by a little open-jar icon with a number in it. Once a bottle's been open past that window, toss it even if there's solution left, because the disinfecting power fades and the risk of contamination climbs. Buy a size you'll finish within the window, note the date you open it, keep the cap on and the tip untouched between uses, and never decant solution into another container. Using expired or old solution is like storing your lenses in nothing at all.

    Do I need a special case for hydrogen-peroxide solution?

    Yes — always. Hydrogen-peroxide systems disinfect powerfully, but the peroxide has to be neutralized before the lenses can safely touch your eye, and that only happens in the special case that comes with the system, which has a built-in neutralizing disc (or a separate neutralizing tablet). You must give it the full neutralization time the instructions specify — usually about six hours — before wearing the lenses. Never put un-neutralized peroxide solution in your eye: it stings and burns badly. And never pour peroxide into a regular flat multipurpose case, and never top off or reuse it. Because a peroxide case still ages, replace it on the schedule the manufacturer recommends, and log the swap alongside your other lens care. If you're ever unsure whether a lens has neutralized, don't wear it — check with your eye doctor.
