Replace Your Sunscreen

Sunscreen is a drug with a shelf life, not a lotion that lasts forever. The FDA requires it to stay at full strength for three years — but a summer in a hot car or beach bag can use that stability up much faster, and the bottle won't tell you. Once a year, check every tube in the house, toss what's gone off, and restock.

🗓️ Check & replace every year ⏳ FDA shelf life: 3 years (stored cool) 🥃 Full-body dose: ~1 oz — a shot glass 💸 New bottle: $10–$20

Why it matters

Expired sunscreen doesn't look dangerous — it looks like sunscreen. That's the problem. The active filters and the emulsion holding them break down quietly, so you apply something that feels protective and isn't. Here's what's actually at stake:

SPF fades silently

The number on the label is only guaranteed while the formula is intact. As filters degrade, an SPF 50 can quietly deliver far less — and there's no color change or warning at the moment it stops being enough.

Label says 50, bottle delivers less

Skin-cancer risk compounds

UV exposure is the major preventable driver of skin cancer, and roughly one in five Americans develops it in their lifetime. Sunscreen only counters that if the bottle still works — every "protected" burn is exposure you didn't mean to take.

~1 in 5 develop skin cancer

Photoaging never takes a day off

UVA rays drive most visible aging — wrinkles, sun spots, lost elasticity — and they're strong even on cloudy days. A degraded broad-spectrum formula often loses its UVA protection first, so the damage runs ahead of any burn.

UVA damage is cumulative

False confidence on the big days

The expired bottle does its worst work on a beach day: you applied something, so you stay out for hours. The worst burns most people remember happened while they believed they were covered.

A burn that ruins the vacation

How often should you replace it?

The FDA requires sunscreen to stay at full strength for at least three years — stored cool. Real life stores it in cars, beach bags, and bathroom windowsills, and rarely remembers when it was bought. Answer four questions for your actual schedule:

1. Where does your main bottle live?
2. Does it have a printed expiration date?
3. How often do you actually use it?
4. Mineral or chemical formula?

    How to do the yearly sunscreen audit

    This is a 20-minute roundup, not a chore: gather every bottle, judge each one, toss the losers, restock smart, and label the new ones so next year's audit takes five minutes. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Worth knowing before you start

    • Don't rely on smell alone. Sunscreen can lose effectiveness without smelling off — a normal scent doesn't prove it's still working. Date and storage history outrank your nose.
    • Past the date (or three years), it's done — no exceptions for "looks fine." The failure you care about is invisible.
    • Dispose of the contents, not just the bottle. Squeeze leftovers into the trash, then recycle the empty if your program takes it. Don't wash large amounts down the drain.
    • Never decant sunscreen into unlabeled bottles. You lose the SPF, the expiry, and the ingredients — and an unmarked white lotion is a guessing game nobody wins.
    • Anything that spent a summer in a car, glovebox, or hot garage should be replaced regardless of its printed date — interiors can pass 130°F, far beyond what the formula was tested for.

    📐 Four things worth knowing

    Where the expiry code hides

    Look at the crimped seam of tubes, the shoulder near the cap, and the stamped bottom of bottles. Many brands print it only on the outer box — the one you threw away at the store door.

    The shot-glass rule

    A full-body application is about one ounce — a shot glass — and the face alone takes two finger-lengths. At that honest dose, a bottle lasts weeks of real use, not years.

    Heat is the real expiration clock

    The three-year shelf life assumes cool, dark storage. A parked car can pass 130°F inside; a beach bag bakes for hours. Heat-soaked bottles can degrade in a single season — replace them on a season clock, not a year clock.

    The back-of-hand test

    Squeeze a dab onto your hand. Watery liquid running ahead of the cream, grainy or sandy clumps, yellowing, or a changed smell all mean the emulsion has broken — toss it. (A clean look doesn't prove it's good; this test only catches the obvious failures.)

    Buying the replacement: what actually matters

    Sunscreen marketing is loud; the things that matter fit on one hand. When you restock, check for these and ignore most of the rest:

    • "Broad spectrum" on the label. That's the regulated term meaning it covers UVA (aging, cancer risk) as well as UVB (burning). SPF alone only describes UVB.
    • SPF 30 or higher. Dermatology groups generally recommend 30+ for real-world use — it allows for the fact that almost everyone under-applies. Past ~50 the gains get thin; consistency matters more than the number.
    • Water resistance — 40 or 80 minutes — if you'll swim or sweat. No sunscreen is waterproof; those two numbers are the only claims allowed, and both still mean reapply after toweling off.
    • A formula you'll actually wear. The best sunscreen is the one that goes on every day — a light face formula you like beats a heavy "perfect" one that stays in the drawer.
    • Enough quantity. Buy a real body bottle plus a face tube plus a travel size. One sad 3 oz tube "for the family" guarantees under-application by July.
    • Check the date at the store. Clearance bins and end-of-summer sales move old stock — a discounted bottle that expires by next June is no bargain.
    • Mineral vs chemical is preference, not correctness. Zinc/titanium formulas are more heat-stable and gentler for sensitive skin; modern chemical formulas feel lighter and vanish on darker skin tones. Both work when fresh and applied generously.

    Common questions

    My bottle has no expiration date — how long is it good?

    U.S. rules require sunscreen to stay at full strength for at least three years; brands that skip the printed date are relying on that default. So an undated bottle is presumed good for about three years from purchase — which is the catch, because almost nobody remembers when they bought it. That's why the practical move is to write the purchase month on every new bottle with a marker, and treat any undated bottle of unknown age as expired.

    Does mineral sunscreen last longer than chemical?

    Somewhat, on the filter side. Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are stable minerals that don't break down the way some chemical filters do — avobenzone, the main UVA filter in many chemical formulas, is known to degrade with heat and light unless stabilized. But the lotion itself is an emulsion of water, oils, and the filters, and that emulsion breaks down in any formula: separation, graininess, and uneven filter distribution mean unreliable coverage even if the zinc is technically fine. Same dates, same yearly audit, either way.

    Is sunscreen from last summer fine?

    Usually yes — if it's within its date and spent the year somewhere cool and dark, not in a car or beach bag. Check the date, do the back-of-hand texture test, and use it with confidence. The honest follow-up question: if a bottle survived a whole summer, were you applying enough? At a shot glass per full-body coat, most households should be finishing bottles, not storing them.

    What does heat actually do to sunscreen?

    Two things. First, heat accelerates the chemical breakdown of some UV filters, so the real SPF drifts below the label. Second — and often sooner — it destabilizes the emulsion: the water and oil phases separate, the texture turns watery or grainy, and the filters stop being evenly distributed, so even a "still potent" formula goes on patchy. The three-year shelf life was established under reasonable storage conditions; a 130°F glovebox is not one of them. Treat any heat-soaked bottle as a one-season item.

    Can I use expired sunscreen in a pinch?

    If the choice is recently-expired sunscreen or nothing at all on an unavoidable sun day, most experts would say apply it — partial protection beats none. But don't let it change your behavior: assume it's underperforming, reapply often, and lean on shade, a hat, and clothing as the real protection. Then replace the bottle the same day. What you shouldn't do is plan around expired sunscreen, because you can't know how much protection is left.

    Does SPF moisturizer or makeup expire too?

    Yes — same rules. In the U.S., anything making an SPF claim is regulated as an over-the-counter drug, so it carries the same stability requirements and the same three-year presumption when undated. SPF moisturizers and tinted products often sit on shelves longer than beach sunscreen because the daily dab makes them last; check them in the same yearly audit, and remember that the thin layer most people apply delivers only a fraction of the labeled SPF anyway.

    Sprays, sticks, lotions — does the format change the shelf life?

    The dates work the same, but the failure modes differ. Lotions show separation clearly, which makes them easiest to judge. Sticks are mostly waxes — heat melts and re-hardens them, which can leave the filters unevenly distributed even though they look normal. Sprays hide their contents entirely, so the printed date and storage history are all you have — and a clogged or sputtering nozzle is a practical reason to replace one regardless. Whatever the format, the marker-on-the-bottle habit is your best evidence next year.
