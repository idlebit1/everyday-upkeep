Clean Your Range Hood Filter

That metal mesh panel under your range hood is quietly soaking up the airborne grease from every sear, fry, and simmer. Once a month, pop it out and let it soak — a clean filter pulls smoke and steam away, keeps the fan working, and keeps a layer of flammable grease from building up right over the burners.

🗓️ Typically once a month ⏱️ ~15 minutes, mostly hands-off soaking 🧼 Just dish soap + baking soda 🔥 Caked grease over a burner is a fire hazard

Why it matters

A range hood doesn't make grease disappear — it pulls greasy air through a metal mesh that traps the droplets before the fan moves the air on. When that mesh clogs, every problem below follows (costs are typical U.S. estimates):

Grease soaks the mesh shut

The mesh is the whole point — it catches airborne grease so the fan doesn't. Skip cleaning and those tiny holes pack with sticky, hardening grease until almost no air can pass.

Free to fix — today

Smoke & steam stay in the room

A clogged filter chokes the airflow, so the hood can't pull smoke, steam, and cooking odors out. They drift back into the kitchen — and into the rest of the house.

Every meal you cook

A fire hazard over the burners

Caked-on grease sits directly above an open flame. A grease fire on the stove can reach up and ignite a saturated filter — keeping it clean removes the fuel that turns a flare-up into a real fire.

Insurance-claim territory

The fan strains and weakens

The motor fights to pull air through a blocked filter, so it works harder and moves less. A clean filter is what lets a fan you already own actually do its job.

$120 – $300 motor

Greasy film on everything

Grease the hood can't capture settles as a thin, sticky film on cabinets, walls, and countertops near the stove. It attracts dust and is miserable to scrub off once it sets.

Hours of degreasing

    How to clean your range hood filter

    Most of the job is hands-off soaking. Work through the checklist — your progress saves automatically, so you can start the soak, walk away, and pick it back up when the timer goes.

    ⚠️ Before you start — grease is flammable, so be careful

    • Turn the hood off and let everything cool. Don't clean over a hot burner or a lit flame — grease and hot fat ignite easily, and you'll be reaching right over the cooktop.
    • Use hot water and a grease-cutting dish soap. Heat plus a degreasing soap is what actually breaks grease down; cold water just smears it around.
    • Go easy on aluminum filters in the dishwasher. Harsh dishwasher detergent can oxidize and darken aluminum mesh. Hand-washing is safest; if you do use the dishwasher, choose a gentle cycle and an aluminum-safe detergent.
    • Charcoal filters are different. If your hood recirculates air back into the room, it has a carbon/charcoal filter that cannot be washed — it gets replaced, not cleaned. More on telling them apart below.

    🧰 What you'll need

    Grease-cutting dish soap Baking soda Hot water Soft brush or old toothbrush Sink, tub, or large basin Gloves Optional: white vinegar / degreaser Recirculating hood? A replacement charcoal filter

    📐 Three things worth seeing first

    Slide or unclip it out

    Reach under the hood, find the spring tab or finger pull on each metal mesh filter, push and tilt it down, and lift it out. Most hoods have one or two; no tools needed.

    Soak it in hot, soapy water

    Fill a sink or tub with hot water, a good squeeze of grease-cutting dish soap, and a scoop of baking soda. Submerge the filter and let it soak 10–20 minutes — the soak does most of the work.

    Scrub, rinse, and dry fully

    Work a soft brush over the loosened grease, paying attention to the corners. Rinse under hot running water until it stops feeling slick, then let it dry completely before it goes back.

    Signs & when to call for service

    • A lingering grease smell or weak suction even after a clean filter. If air still isn't moving, the problem may be the ductwork (greased-up or blocked) or the fan motor itself — worth a service look.
    • The fan won't run, or runs but moves almost no air. A motor that hums, trips, or stays silent usually needs a technician; that's not a filter fix.
    • A charcoal filter that's badly overdue. On recirculating hoods the carbon filter can't be washed — if odors have returned and it's been more than 3–6 months, replace it. If a fresh one doesn't help, have the unit checked.
    • Grease has reached the ductwork. For ducted hoods, grease buildup inside the duct run is both an airflow and a fire concern; professional duct cleaning is the right call for long-neglected systems.
    • Typical appliance diagnostic visits run $100–$150, often credited toward the repair. Mention you've already cleaned (or replaced) the filter — it's the first thing they'd check.

    Common questions

    How often should I clean the range hood filter?

    For a washable metal mesh (baffle) filter, once a month is the standard for an average kitchen. If you fry often or cook at high heat daily, every two weeks keeps it from gumming up; light cooks can stretch toward every six weeks. A charcoal filter is a different story — it isn't cleaned at all, it's replaced, typically every three to six months.

    Can I just put the filter in the dishwasher?

    Often yes, but with a caveat. Stainless steel mesh handles the dishwasher fine. Aluminum mesh can oxidize and turn dull, dark, or chalky from harsh dishwasher detergent and hot drying. If yours is aluminum (a lot are), hand-washing in hot soapy water is the safe bet; if you do use the dishwasher, run a gentle cycle with an aluminum-safe detergent and place the filter so water reaches both sides.

    What's the boiling-water and baking-soda method?

    It's the heavy-duty version of the soak. Bring a large pot of water to a boil, stir in a few tablespoons of baking soda a little at a time (it foams), and lower the filter in for a few minutes — or pour the boiling soda-water over the filter in a heatproof basin. The combination of heat and the mild alkalinity of baking soda lifts hardened grease that a lukewarm soak leaves behind. Use tongs and gloves, keep it away from the burner once the filter's in, and rinse well afterward.

    How do I know if I have a mesh filter or a charcoal filter?

    Look at where your hood sends the air. A ducted/vented hood pushes air outside and uses a metal mesh (or baffle) grease filter — that's the washable one you clean monthly. A recirculating/ductless hood filters the air and blows it back into the kitchen; behind the mesh it has a charcoal/carbon filter to trap odors. That carbon filter is a sealed, granular pad — it can't be washed and must be replaced on a schedule. Many recirculating hoods have both: wash the mesh, replace the charcoal.

    How do I clean the greasy hood itself?

    While the filter soaks, wipe down the hood's underside, the visible surfaces, and the area around the filter slots with hot soapy water or a kitchen degreaser. A microfiber cloth and a little dish soap handle most of it; baking soda paste helps on stubborn baked-on spots. On stainless steel, finish by wiping in the direction of the grain so it doesn't streak. Don't forget the light cover and the controls, which collect a surprising amount of film.

    My suction is still weak after cleaning the filter. What now?

    First confirm the filter is fully dry and reseated correctly and that the fan is on its higher setting. If it's still weak, the likely culprits are a greased-up or partially blocked duct, a worn fan motor, or — on a recirculating hood — a spent charcoal filter that needs replacing. If a fresh charcoal filter and a clean mesh don't restore airflow, it's time for a service visit to check the motor and ductwork.

    Monthly degreasing only works if you remember it.

    You now know your filter schedule — monthly for the mesh, every few months to swap a charcoal one. The hard part is remembering it all year.
