Service Your Generator

A generator only earns its keep if it fires up when the power's out — and that's exactly the moment neglected ones refuse to start. The usual culprit isn't the engine; it's the fuel. Ethanol-blended gas left in the tank gums up the carburetor and clogs the tiny jets within months, so the machine that sat untouched since the last storm just won't catch. One unhurried service a year — oil, spark plug, air filter, and fuel system — plus a monthly test-run keeps it ready. And before any of that: run it outside, never indoors, because carbon monoxide is invisible and deadly. Here's how to do the whole job right.

Why it matters

A generator fails in exactly one circumstance: the day you finally need it, with the lights already out and the fridge warming up. Almost every no-start traces back to the same handful of neglected jobs — and the worst part is you don't find out until it's too late to fix. Here's what skipping the service actually costs (typical U.S. estimates):

It won't start when it counts

A generator that sits unserviced for a year routinely refuses to start when the storm hits — and an outage is exactly the moment failure hurts most, with every repair shop already buried in a backlog.

No power when you need it most

Stale ethanol fuel is the #1 killer

Pump gas with ethanol absorbs water and turns to varnish in months, gumming the carburetor and clogging its pinhole-sized jets. That sticky residue is the single most common reason a stored generator won't catch.

Carb rebuild ~$75–200

Old oil and a fouled plug wear it out

Stale oil, a carbon-fouled spark plug, and a clogged air filter make a small engine run rough, overheat, and run lean or rich. Left alone, a no-start becomes a worn-out, hard-to-repair engine.

Rough running, then real damage

Service keeps the whole unit reliable

Regular running and an annual tune-up keep the engine, the carburetor, and the electrical output all dependable. A generator that's exercised monthly and serviced yearly is one you can actually count on through storm season.

Reliable power, season after season

How often should you service it?

A full service once a year is the sensible baseline for almost every portable generator — plus a test-run about every month and after every 50–200 run-hours, and a check before storm or winter season. But how you use it and what you feed it move the dial: frequent home-backup or jobsite duty and plain ethanol pump gas tighten the intervals, while a stabilized, ethanol-free, rarely-run unit can relax a little. Answer four quick questions:

1. How do you actually use it?
Run-hours drive engine wear. A generator that sits is mostly a fuel-and-storage problem; one that works hard on a jobsite needs oil and filter service on a much shorter clock.
2. What fuel goes in it?
Ethanol gas is the top reason stored generators won't start — it absorbs water and varnishes the carb. Ethanol-free gas, stabilizer, and propane all dodge most of that.
3. What's your climate and storage like?
If you live where the power goes out in big storms, the cost of a no-start is highest — so test more often. Long idle storage is when fuel goes stale and carbs gum up.
4. How old is it, and how many hours?
Older engines and high-hour units are less forgiving — seals, the carburetor, and the plug all want fresher attention than a near-new machine.

    How to service your generator

    The service itself is straightforward small-engine work — change the oil, service the plug, clean or swap the air filter, treat the fuel, and prove it runs under load. It's the same engine know-how behind servicing the snow blower, and the basics carry straight over from a plain oil change and a set of spark plugs. But two safety rules come before everything and have no exceptions: run it only outdoors (carbon monoxide kills), and never backfeed your house through a wall outlet (it kills line workers). Read the safety box and the techniques first, then work through the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Read this first — carbon monoxide and backfeeding can kill

    • NEVER run a generator indoors, in a garage, in a shed, or near open windows or doors — not even with the door open. The exhaust is carbon monoxide: invisible, odorless, and deadly within minutes. Run it outside, well away from the house (at least 20 feet) with the exhaust pointed away, under an open canopy if it's raining, and keep a working CO alarm inside.
    • NEVER plug a generator into a wall outlet to "backfeed" your home. It pushes power back onto the utility lines and can electrocute line workers trying to restore service, and it bypasses your home's breakers and can start a fire. For whole-home power, use a proper transfer switch or interlock installed by a licensed electrician; otherwise run appliances directly on heavy-duty, outdoor-rated extension cords.
    • Turn the engine OFF and let it COOL before servicing. A hot muffler and engine cause burns, and you don't want it starting while your hands are near moving parts. Let it cool fully before you touch oil, the plug, or the filter.
    • Disconnect the spark-plug wire before working on the engine. Pull the boot off the plug so the engine can't possibly fire while you're servicing it.
    • Refuel only on a cold engine — never while it's running or hot. Spilled gas on a hot muffler can ignite. Shut it down, let it cool, then fill it.
    • Store fuel safely in approved containers and stabilize it. Keep gas in proper, sealed cans away from living spaces and ignition sources, add stabilizer, and never store fuel near the running generator.

    🧰 What you'll need

    The owner's manual (oil grade, spark plug & gap) Fresh engine oil (usually SAE 10W-30 or 5W-30) A new spark plug (right part number) A spark-plug socket & gap gauge A new or cleanable air filter Fuel stabilizer An oil drain pan & funnel Shop rags & gloves A CO alarm (inside the house)

    📐 Five things worth knowing before you start

    Change the oil

    Run it a minute to warm the oil so it flows, shut it off, then drain the old oil into a pan and refill to the dipstick mark with the grade your manual lists (often 10W-30). On a brand-new unit, do the early break-in change too. Recycle the old oil.

    Service the spark plug

    Pull the plug wire, unscrew the plug, and read it: tan is healthy, black and sooty or oily means trouble. Clean light deposits and set the gap to spec, or just fit a fresh plug — they're a couple of dollars. Reconnect the wire firmly when you're done.

    Clean or replace the air filter

    A clogged filter chokes the engine and makes it run rich and rough. Pop the air-box cover, lift out the element, and tap or wash a foam filter clean (re-oil if it's the oiled type) — or just drop in a fresh paper one. An engine that breathes starts easier.

    Treat the fuel system

    Add stabilizer to fresh gas and run it a few minutes so the treated fuel reaches the carburetor. For storage, either run the carb dry (close the fuel valve and let it stall) or drain the bowl. Stale ethanol gas left to varnish is the number-one no-start.

    Test under load monthly

    Once a month, take it outside, start it, and actually run a few appliances on it for 20–30 minutes — a load circulates fuel, charges the battery on electric-start units, and proves it works. Then log it, so you know it's ready before the next storm.

    Signs it's overdue — and when to call a pro

    A healthy generator starts in a pull or two, idles steady, and puts out clean power. These are the signals it's overdue for service — and the handful of jobs that belong to an electrician or a small-engine dealer, not a Saturday afternoon:

    • Hard starting or won't start at all. If it takes many pulls, needs ether, or simply won't catch after sitting, suspect stale fuel and a gummed carburetor first — that's the classic neglected-generator symptom and exactly what the annual service prevents.
    • Surging, hunting, or stalling under load. An engine that revs up and down or dies when you plug something in usually has a dirty carburetor, a clogged jet, or a fouled plug. Fresh fuel, a carb cleaning, and a new plug often fix it.
    • No output or low/unstable voltage. If the engine runs fine but nothing's powered, or the voltage sags and surges, the problem is electrical — the alternator, capacitor, AVR, or outlets. That's diagnostic territory; have it checked rather than guessing.
    • Smoking or burning oil. Blue smoke means oil is getting into the combustion chamber (often from overfilling or a tipped unit); black smoke means it's running too rich. Persistent smoking after a service points to deeper engine wear — a dealer's call.
    • A carburetor that won't clear. If draining, fresh gas, and a cleaner-soak don't restore smooth running, the carb may need a full rebuild kit or replacement. Many people hand a badly varnished carb to a shop.
    • Any standby or whole-home generator. Permanently installed standby units, automatic transfer switches, and the wiring that ties a generator into your house are licensed-electrician and authorized-dealer work — annual professional service and proper installation aren't DIY.
    • Pro tip: the surest way to never get caught out is the monthly test under load — it surfaces a starting or fuel problem on a calm sunny day, not at 2 a.m. in the storm when the shops are closed. And a working generator is only half of outage readiness; pair it with a stocked emergency kit so the lights, water, and supplies are sorted too.

    Common questions

    How often should I service my generator?

    Give it a full service — oil change, spark plug, air filter, and fuel-system treatment — about once a year, and after the manufacturer's run-hour intervals (often an early break-in oil change, then every 50–200 hours for a hard-working unit). Between services, run it under a real load for 20–30 minutes about once a month, top up stabilizer in the tank, and always do a test-run before storm or winter season so you know it'll start. If you use it heavily on a jobsite or run plain ethanol pump gas, tighten the oil, filter, and fuel intervals; a stabilized, ethanol-free, rarely-run unit can lean toward the relaxed end. Always check your owner's manual for the exact oil grade, plug gap, and service schedule for your model.

    Why won't my generator start after sitting all year?

    Almost always: stale fuel. Pump gas with ethanol (E10) absorbs moisture and oxidizes within a few months, turning into a gummy varnish that clogs the carburetor's pinhole-sized jets — and a starved carb won't fire no matter how hard you pull. The fix and the prevention are the same: don't store untreated gas. Add stabilizer to fresh fuel, and for longer storage either run the carburetor dry (shut the fuel valve and let it stall) or drain the carb bowl. If it's already gummed up, draining the old fuel, filling with fresh stabilized gas, and cleaning or replacing the carburetor usually brings it back. Also check the basics: fuel valve open, choke set, oil at the right level (low-oil sensors prevent starting), and a good spark plug. A monthly test-run is the cheapest insurance against ever discovering this at the worst moment.

    Can I run it in my garage if I leave the door open?

    No. This is the most dangerous mistake people make with a generator, and it kills every year. The exhaust is carbon monoxide — invisible, odorless, and lethal — and it builds to deadly levels in an enclosed or partly enclosed space faster than you'd believe, even with the door open or near an open window. A garage, a shed, a carport, a covered porch, or a basement are all off-limits. Run the generator outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the house with the exhaust pointed away from doors and windows, and keep a working carbon-monoxide alarm inside your home. If it's raining, use a generator tent or open-sided canopy designed for the purpose rather than bringing it under cover. There is no safe way to run a generator indoors.

    Can I plug it into a wall outlet to power my house?

    No — this is called "backfeeding," and it's both illegal in most places and deadly. Plugging a running generator into a wall outlet pushes power backward through your home's wiring and out onto the utility lines, where it can electrocute the line workers trying to restore your power; it also bypasses your breakers and can overload wiring and start a fire. The safe ways to power your home are a manual transfer switch or generator interlock kit installed by a licensed electrician, which isolates your circuits from the grid and lets the generator feed them safely — or, for individual appliances, plugging them directly into the generator with heavy-duty, outdoor-rated extension cords sized for the load. Never improvise a connection into your home's wiring.

    What fuel should I use, and do I really need stabilizer?

    Use the fuel grade your manual specifies — for most portable gas generators that's fresh regular unleaded, ideally 87 octane with no more than 10% ethanol (E10); avoid higher ethanol blends like E15 or E85 unless your unit is specifically rated for them. Ethanol-free gas (sometimes sold as "rec fuel") stores much better and is worth seeking out if you don't run the generator often. And yes — stabilizer is genuinely worth it: ethanol gas starts degrading in as little as 30 days, and stabilizer extends usable life to a year or more by slowing oxidation and helping repel water, which is what protects the carburetor from varnish. Add it to fresh gas (not gas that's already gone stale), and run the engine a few minutes so the treated fuel reaches the carb. Dual-fuel and propane generators sidestep the fuel-staling problem entirely, since propane doesn't go bad — one reason many people like them for backup.

    How do I store it for the off-season?

    The goal is to leave no untreated fuel sitting in the carburetor. Two good approaches: either fill the tank with fresh, stabilized gas and run it a few minutes so the treated fuel circulates (which keeps the system wet and protected), or run the system dry — close the fuel valve while it's running and let the engine consume the gas in the carb until it stalls, and drain the tank. Whichever you choose, also change the oil before long storage so the engine isn't sitting in dirty oil, take out or fresh-up the spark plug, and store the unit clean, covered, and dry — off the ground, away from living spaces, and never with fuel cans beside it. Keep any electric-start battery topped up. Then, before the next storm season, do a full service and a test-run under load so it's ready the day you need it.
