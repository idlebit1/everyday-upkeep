Season Your Cast Iron Pan

That slick black surface isn't paint and it isn't the metal — it's seasoning: thin layers of oil baked until they polymerize into a hard, natural non-stick coat that also seals the iron against rust. Cooking with a little fat builds it; harsh scrubbing and acid strip it. A thin-coat oven bake every month or so restores the patina, and the pan only gets darker, slicker, and better with each round.

🗓️ Light oil after every wash 🔥 Full oven re-season ~monthly 🧴 About $0–$10 of oil 🍳 A cared-for pan lasts generations

Why it matters

Seasoning is not a coating you buy — it's oil you bake on. Heat thin layers of fat past their smoke point and they polymerize: the molecules bond into a hard, plastic-like film locked to the iron. That film is two things at once — your non-stick surface and your rust armor — and every meal cooked in a little fat keeps adding to it. Here's what the patina is quietly doing for you:

Your natural non-stick surface

The slick, glassy black you see is polymerized oil — eggs slide, fish releases, a fond lifts with a splash of water. Bare iron is sticky and grabby; the seasoning is the whole reason a well-loved skillet cooks like a coated pan, with none of the worn-off Teflon.

Why food slides instead of sticks

The barrier that stops rust

Iron plus water plus air equals rust, fast — a bare or stripped pan can bloom orange overnight. The baked-on film seals moisture out, which is exactly why a seasoned pan must never soak in the sink or air-dry wet. Keep the patina intact and the iron underneath stays sound.

Bare iron rusts overnight

Acid & scrubbing strip it

A long simmer of tomato sauce, wine, or citrus eats into the patina; so does a soak in the sink, harsh scouring, or a trip through the dishwasher. The seasoning is tough but not permanent — it wears in spots, which is exactly why an occasional re-bake puts the layers back.

Tomatoes & soaking undo it

It outlives the cook

A cast iron pan has no parts to wear out — only seasoning to maintain. Tend the patina and the same skillet fries breakfast for fifty years, then your kids' breakfast after that. Every meal in a little fat builds the layer thicker; it genuinely improves with use.

Heirloom, not disposable

How often should you re-season it?

Two clocks run at once. Maintenance is a light oil rub after every wash — that's not optional, it's just part of drying. A full oven re-season is the bigger job, and "about monthly, or whenever it looks dull" is the safe default. Answer three questions to tune the bake interval to your pan:

1. How often do you cook in it?
2. What does the surface look like right now?
3. Do you often cook acidic foods in it? (tomato, citrus, wine, vinegar)

    How to season your cast iron pan

    Budget about an hour, most of it bake-and-cool time. The whole trick is in two ideas — drive off every trace of moisture, and apply oil so thin it looks like you wiped it all back off. Read the techniques, then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Before you start — a 450°F pan, an oven that smokes, and iron that rusts wet

    • Everything gets oven-hot. The pan, the handle, and the racks come out around 450–500°F. Use dry oven mitts on a dry, heat-safe surface — a damp mitt or trivet transmits heat straight to your hand, and a cold wet counter can crack a screaming-hot pan.
    • Thin coats only — this is the one rule people break. Oil applied too thick bakes into a gummy, sticky, sometimes sheety film instead of a hard one. Wipe it back off until the surface looks almost dry. More thin coats beat one thick coat, every time.
    • It will smoke a little — ventilate. You're baking oil past its smoke point on purpose; that's the polymerizing working. Crack a window, run the range hood, and don't be alarmed by the haze and smell.
    • Foil the rack below the pan. Bake the pan upside down and a sheet of foil on the lower rack catches the few drips, sparing your oven floor a baked-on mess.
    • Never the dishwasher, never left wet. Detergent and a long soak strip seasoning and the iron rusts within hours of sitting damp — hand-wash, dry, oil, done.

    🧰 What you'll need

    High-smoke-point oil (grapeseed, canola, vegetable, or flaxseed) Paper towels or a lint-free cloth An oven + foil for the rack below Mild soap + scrub brush or chainmail Optional: fine steel wool for rust Dry oven mitts

    📐 Five things worth seeing first

    Wash, then drive off all moisture

    Clean the pan, towel it, then set it on a burner a minute until the last damp sheen steams away. Seasoning over even slightly wet iron traps moisture under the film — it must be bone dry first.

    Rub a thin coat over everything

    A few drops of oil, worked over every surface inside and out — the cooking bowl, the sides, the bottom, and the handle. The whole pan is iron; the whole pan wants protecting.

    Then wipe it back off

    This is the secret. Take a fresh towel and buff the oil away until the pan only looks faintly damp, not glistening. What's left is microscopically thin — and that's precisely the layer that bakes hard instead of gummy.

    Bake upside down, ~1 hour

    Set the pan inverted on the upper rack at 450–500°F for about an hour, with foil on the rack beneath to catch drips. Upside down means no oil pools in the bowl — gravity keeps the coat even.

    Cool in the oven, then repeat

    Turn the oven off and let the pan cool inside — a slow cooldown sets the film and avoids thermal shock. For a deeper, darker finish, run the oil-and-bake two or three times; each pass lays down another thin layer.

    🎉 Done — that's a fresh layer of patina baked on. Light-oil after each wash, and the next full bake is due in about a month (or whenever it looks dull).

    When to strip & re-season from scratch

    A monthly bake maintains good seasoning, but sometimes the patina is too far gone to build on — bad layers have to come off before good ones go on. Strip back to bare metal and start fresh when you see any of these:

    • Flaking or peeling seasoning. Black bits lifting off into your food mean the film has lost its grip on the iron. Building on top just traps more loose layers — scrub the flakes off down to clean metal first.
    • Sticky, gummy, or tacky buildup. A surface that feels varnished or stays slightly tacky is the signature of oil applied too thick. It won't cure into non-stick on its own; take it back and redo it with proper thin coats.
    • Patchy, uneven gray zones. Bare gray rings where the seasoning has worn through (often a cooking-surface ring or a stripped center) cook unevenly and rust first. A full re-season levels everything out.
    • Heavy or pitting rust. A little surface orange wipes off, but widespread rust or pitting needs real abrasion. Scrub it back to gray metal — fine steel wool, a chainmail scrubber, coarse salt, or for the brave, an oven self-clean cycle — then re-season immediately so it can't re-rust.
    • A thrift-store or inherited mystery pan. Unknown gunk, layered grime, or decades of who-knows-what is worth a clean reset so you start from a surface you trust.

    Common questions

    What's the best oil for seasoning?

    Any neutral oil with a reasonably high smoke point works: grapeseed, canola, vegetable, sunflower, and refined avocado oil are all reliable and cheap. Flaxseed oil polymerizes into a famously hard, glassy finish and is beloved by enthusiasts, but it's pricier and more finicky — applied too thick it can flake. Plain old vegetable or canola oil is what most people should reach for. Avoid butter and unrefined olive oil for the bake itself; their low smoke points and solids make a stickier result. The bigger variable isn't which oil — it's how thin you wipe it.

    Wait — can I use soap now? I heard you never should.

    Yes, you can. The "never use soap" rule is an outdated myth from the era of harsh lye-based soaps that genuinely stripped seasoning. Today's mild dish soap won't dissolve a properly polymerized film — that film is plastic-like and chemically bonded to the iron, not a layer of grease sitting on top. A little soap and a soft brush or chainmail scrubber is perfectly fine for everyday cleaning. What actually harms seasoning is a long soak, the dishwasher, abrasive scouring, and leaving the pan wet — not a quick wash.

    Why does food still stick even after I seasoned it?

    Two usual culprits. First, the seasoning is still thin or uneven — non-stick comes from layers built up over many cooks, so a pan with one or two bakes may need a few more rounds (and a few fatty meals) before it's truly slick. Second, and more often: the pan wasn't hot enough. Cast iron needs a few minutes to preheat thoroughly; food dropped onto an under-heated pan bonds to the metal. Preheat over medium for several minutes, add fat, let it shimmer, then cook — and don't fight food that's stuck, as protein releases on its own once it's properly seared.

    How do I get rust off?

    Rust is fixable and never the end of a pan. For light surface rust, scrub with fine steel wool, a chainmail scrubber, or a paste of coarse salt and a little oil until you're back to clean gray metal. For heavy rust, you may need to scour aggressively or run an oven self-clean / lye-bath strip to bare iron. The critical part comes after: a freshly stripped pan is raw, exposed metal that will re-rust within hours, so dry it completely and re-season it the same day. Don't set a bare, scrubbed pan in the drainer and walk away.

    What's the everyday care between full re-seasons?

    Three moves, every time: wash, dry, oil. Rinse or wash the pan (mild soap is fine) while it's still warm, scrubbing off stuck bits with a brush or chainmail — for tougher fond, simmer a little water to loosen it. Dry it thoroughly, ideally with a minute back on the burner to chase off all moisture. Then rub the thinnest film of oil over the cooking surface and buff it nearly dry. That after-wash oiling is the real maintenance; the monthly oven bake is just a periodic top-up of the whole patina.

    Is the dishwasher ever okay?

    Never. The dishwasher combines everything that destroys cast iron: harsh detergent that strips the film, a long soak, high heat, and — worst of all — a wet pan sitting in a humid box, which is a recipe for rust. One cycle can undo months of seasoning and leave the pan orange. Cast iron is a hand-wash-only tool, full stop. The same goes for leaving it to soak in the sink; a quick wash and an immediate dry are the rule.
