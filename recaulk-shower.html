Re-Caulk Your Shower or Tub

That thin line of caulk where your tub or shower meets the wall and floor is the seal that keeps water out of the structure. It's also the first thing to give way — it cracks, shrinks, peels, and grows black mold no scrubbing will shift. Once it fails, water slips behind it and quietly rots the drywall, the studs, and sometimes the room below. The fix is a satisfying afternoon job: cut out every scrap of the old caulk, clean and dry the joint, tape crisp lines, and lay one smooth bead of 100% silicone. Do it about every two years — or the moment it's cracked or moldy — and the seal stays watertight and the corner looks new.

🗓️ Typically every 2 years ⏱️ ~1–2 hr, plus cure time 🧴 About $8–$20 in materials 🛡️ Keeps water out of the wall

Why it matters

Caulk is the soft, flexible seal that fills the gap where two hard surfaces meet — the tub and the wall, the wall corners, the base of the surround. It's the one part of the shower designed to move and to be replaced. When it fails, it stops being a small cosmetic flaw and becomes a water-management problem. Here's what fresh caulk is quietly buying you:

It's the wall's waterproof seal

Tile and the tub edge don't actually meet — there's a gap, and caulk is what seals it. That flexible bead is the only thing keeping the daily flood of shower water from running straight into the joint and behind the surround.

The last line of defense

A failed seal rots the wall

Once caulk cracks or peels, water wicks into the gap and stays there. Drywall swells and crumbles, studs and subfloor rot, and the leak can reach the ceiling of the room below — a hidden, expensive repair that started as a $10 line of caulk.

Hidden rot, big repair

Mold roots in — scrubbing loses

Mold grows into old, porous caulk, not just on top of it, so once it's settled in you can scrub and bleach for weeks and the black streaks come right back. Cutting the moldy caulk out and replacing it is the only thing that truly wins.

Replacing beats fighting

A clean, fresh-looking corner

Beyond the waterproofing, a fresh white bead transforms how the whole shower reads — gone are the gray, peeling, mildew-streaked lines that make a clean bathroom still look grubby. It's one of the highest-impact hours of upkeep there is.

~$8–$20, an afternoon

How often should you re-caulk?

Good silicone in a tub or shower lasts a long time — "every two years" is more of an outer-bound habit than a hard rule, and a well-installed bead can run longer. But the real trigger isn't the calendar at all: it's the caulk's condition. Cracked, peeling, or moldy means re-caulk now, whatever the date. Answer four questions:

1. What shape is the caulk in right now?
Run a fingernail along the bead. If it's split, lifting at an edge, or you can see a dark line or gap behind it, the seal is already broken — that's water's way in, and the cue to re-caulk regardless of when you last did it.
2. How heavily is this shower used?
Every shower wets the joint and flexes it as the tub takes weight and the surfaces expand. The more showers a day, the more cycles the bead absorbs — and the sooner it eventually fatigues, cracks, and lets mold in.
3. How well does the room dry out?
Caulk that never fully dries between showers is mold's ideal home, and constant damp shortens its life. A room that clears its steam quickly keeps the bead cleaner and lasting far longer than one that sits humid.
4. Any sign water's getting behind it?
Press the wall and tub edge near the joint. Anything spongy, a brown stain, a musty smell, or tile that shifts means water may already be behind the surface — re-caulk now, and look closely for damage that may need more than a new bead.

    How to re-caulk your shower or tub

    Hands-on time is an hour or two; the rest is the bead curing while the shower sits unused. The whole job lives or dies on two things — getting all the old caulk out, and starting with a surface that's truly clean and bone dry. Read the techniques first (especially the silicone-not-acrylic and bone-dry rules), then work the checklist. Your progress saves automatically.

    ⚠️ Before you start — the right caulk, a dry surface, and full cure

    • Use the right caulk. For a wet area, use 100% silicone or a quality kitchen-and-bath caulk labeled mildew-resistant — not plain acrylic or "painter's" caulk, which isn't truly waterproof and will fail fast in a shower.
    • The surface must be clean and BONE DRY. New caulk won't bond to a damp, dusty, soapy, or greasy joint. Get every scrap of old caulk out, wipe with rubbing alcohol, and let it dry completely — don't shower for a day or two beforehand if you can manage it.
    • Ventilate while you work. Silicone fumes (that vinegary smell) are strong in a small room — run the fan, open a window, and take breaks. Some people wear a mask.
    • Let it cure fully before water hits it. Most silicone needs about 24 hours to cure (check your tube — some are faster, some slower). Showering too soon ruins the seal. Plan the job for an evening or a day the shower can rest.
    • Cut away from yourself. A utility knife or razor scraper slips easily on a wet, glossy surface — always cut and scrape in a direction pointing away from your free hand and body, and keep fingers clear of the blade.

    100% silicone, mildew-resistant caulk A caulk gun Caulk-removal tool or razor scraper / utility knife Rubbing alcohol + a rag Painter's tape A caulk-smoothing tool (or your finger) Gloves Optional: a mildew/mold cleaner ~1–2 hr, plus ~24 hr cure

    📐 Five things worth seeing first

    Cut out all the old caulk

    Score along both edges with a razor or removal tool and pull the whole bead out in strips — get every scrap, even the bits tucked in corners. New caulk only bonds to the surface, not to old caulk, so anything left behind becomes a weak spot that fails first.

    Clean, kill mold, dry it out

    Scrub the empty joint clean, treat any mold with rubbing alcohol or a mildew cleaner, then wipe it down with alcohol to strip soap and grease. Now the crucial part: let it dry completely. Damp or dirty, and the new bead simply won't stick.

    Tape both sides for crisp lines

    Press a strip of painter's tape along each side of the joint, leaving an even gap the width you want the bead. Tape is the secret to a pro-looking, perfectly straight line — it catches the overspread so you don't have to be steady-handed.

    Lay one steady, even bead

    Load the gun, snip the nozzle to a small angle, and pull the trigger as you draw it smoothly along the joint — one continuous bead, no stops and starts. Aim for enough caulk to fill the gap and slightly overlap both sides; you'll tool it flush next.

    Smooth, peel the tape, cure

    Drag a wet fingertip or a smoothing tool along the bead to press it in and leave a neat concave line. Then peel both tape strips away immediately, while the caulk's still wet, for clean edges. Let it cure fully — about 24 hours — before any water.

    Signs it's overdue — and when to call a pro

    A fresh bead seals tight and looks crisp the moment it cures. These are the signals the caulk has failed and needs replacing now — and the cases where a new bead won't fix it, because the trouble has gone past the seal into the wall or a different repair entirely:

    • Caulk that's cracked, split, peeling, or gapping. The clearest sign. Any break in the bead — a crack, a curling edge, a gap you can see behind — means the seal is open and water is getting into the joint. Re-caulk now.
    • Black or pink mold streaks that won't scrub away. Mold rooted into the caulk can't be cleaned back out for good. When the streaks return within days of scrubbing, the caulk itself is the problem — cut it out and replace it.
    • Caulk that's gone hard, yellowed, or chalky. Old caulk loses its flex and grip even before it visibly cracks. If it's discolored and brittle, it's near the end — replacing it before it fails is cheaper than after.
    • Soft, spongy, or stained walls around the surround. Drywall that gives when you press it, a musty smell, or brown stains on the wall or the ceiling below mean water has already gotten behind the tile — there may be rot, and that's a pro's call before you re-caulk over it.
    • Active leaking or loose, shifting tile. Water actually dripping, or tiles that move or sound hollow, point to damage in the substrate behind them — a repair well past a new line of caulk. Bring in a pro before it spreads.
    • Failing grout, not caulk. Crumbling or missing grout between tiles is a different (related) repair — that's regrouting, not re-caulking. Caulk seals the moving joints (tub-to-wall, corners); grout fills the gaps between tiles.
    • A cracked tub or shower pan, or tile cracks. A crack in the pan, the tub, or the tile itself isn't a caulk problem and caulk won't reliably seal it — that needs proper repair or replacement, often professionally.
    • Pro tip: if you cut out the old caulk and find the gap is wide, irregular, or the surface behind it is soft, stop and look closer — a wide gap can mean the tub has shifted or the wall has moved, and that's worth diagnosing before you just bridge it with caulk.

    Common questions

    How often should I re-caulk a shower or tub?

    There's no fixed clock — good 100% silicone, well installed in a clean dry joint, can last well beyond two years, while a poor job or a damp, heavily used shower can fail in a year. "About every two years" is a sensible outer habit to check it, but the real trigger is condition, not date: re-caulk the moment the bead is cracked, peeling, gapping, or showing mold streaks that won't scrub out, whatever the calendar says. Drying the room out well after showers and using a mildew-resistant caulk both stretch how long each bead lasts.

    Silicone or acrylic-latex caulk for a shower?

    For the wet joints in a shower or tub, use 100% silicone — ideally one labeled mildew-resistant, made for kitchen and bath. Silicone is fully waterproof, stays flexible, and grips glossy surfaces like tile, glass, and tub material well, which is exactly what a constantly wet, slightly moving joint needs. Acrylic-latex (or "siliconized acrylic" / painter's) caulk is easier to tool and paintable, but it isn't truly waterproof and breaks down faster in standing water, so it's the wrong choice for the actual wet seal. The trade-off with pure silicone is that it can't be painted over and is a little fussier to smooth — but in a shower, waterproof wins. If you ever do want a paintable bead somewhere dry, that's where acrylic belongs.

    How do I get the old caulk out cleanly?

    Soften and slice, then pull. Score along both edges of the bead with a sharp utility knife or razor scraper (cutting away from yourself), then use a plastic caulk-removal tool to lift and peel the strips out — they often come away in long ribbons once both edges are cut. A dedicated caulk-softening gel can help with stubborn old silicone; let it dwell, then scrape. Get into the corners and pick out every last fragment with the tool or your fingernail, because new caulk bonds to the surface, not to old caulk — any leftover bits become weak points. Finish by wiping the bare joint with rubbing alcohol to remove residue, soap film, and grease.

    How do I get a smooth, professional-looking caulk line?

    Three things do it. First, tape both sides of the joint with painter's tape, leaving an even gap — the tape catches your overspread and guarantees straight edges. Second, lay one continuous bead with steady pressure and a steady pull, snipping the nozzle to a small angled opening sized to the gap. Third, tool it once: drag a wet fingertip (or a caulk-smoothing tool) along the bead in a single smooth pass to press it in and leave a slight concave shape, wiping excess on a rag as you go. Then peel the tape away immediately, while the caulk is still wet, pulling at an angle away from the bead. Don't go back and fuss at it once it starts skinning over — that's what causes the lumpy, smeared look.

    Can I just caulk over the old caulk?

    No — and it's the most common shortcut that guarantees failure. New caulk doesn't bond reliably to old caulk; it sticks to the clean surface underneath. Caulking over a failing bead traps the old mold and moisture inside, gives you a thick, lumpy line, and peels away soon after because there's nothing solid holding it. Always remove the old caulk completely, clean and dry the joint, and lay the new bead on bare, sound surface. The removal is the unglamorous half of the job, but it's the half that makes the new seal actually last.

    How long before I can use the shower again?

    Check your tube, but most 100% silicone needs roughly 24 hours to cure before it's safe to get wet — some "fast-cure" formulas are ready in a few hours, while a thick bead or a humid room can take longer. Curing is a chemical process (it's not just drying), and water hitting an uncured bead disrupts the seal and can leave it weak or peeling. The bead may feel firm to the touch well before it's fully cured, so don't trust the surface — go by the label's stated cure time, and when in doubt, give it the full day. Keeping the fan on helps it cure cleanly.

    How do I keep mold from coming back in the new caulk?

    Two levers: the caulk and the air. Use a mildew-resistant 100% silicone, which has additives that slow mold from taking hold. Then attack the moisture mold needs — run the exhaust fan during and 20–30 minutes after every shower (or crack a window), squeegee the walls and glass so water doesn't pool against the joint, and wipe the corner dry now and then. Mold grows where caulk stays wet, so a room that dries out fast keeps a fresh bead clean for years; a room that sits humid will mildew even good caulk. If your old caulk molded fast, the lasting fix is better ventilation as much as better caulk.
