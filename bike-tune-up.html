Bike Tune-Up

A bike wears out quietly: cables stretch, pads thin, bearings dry. An annual shop tune-up resets all of it — and a 5-minute pre-ride M-check is your job in between. Here's how often, what it costs, and exactly what to look at.

🗓️ Shop tune 1× a year for most riders 💵 $75–150 for a basic tune ⏱️ M-check: 5 minutes, no tools 💸 Skipping risks $400+ drivetrain bills

Why it matters

Bikes don't fail loudly — they drift. Each small bit of neglected wear makes the next part wear faster, and the cheap fix quietly becomes the expensive one. Here's what a yearly tune actually prevents (typical U.S. cost ranges):

Ghost shifting

Cables stretch in their first season and keep creeping after. The result: gears that jump, skip, and hunt mid-climb. A tune re-tensions and re-indexes everything.

$25 – $50 adjust, free in a tune

Worn brake pads

Pads wear past their grooves, then chew the rim or rotor they're supposed to grab. A $20–40 set of pads beats a $100 wheel — and beats a crash by even more.

$20 – $40 pads vs. crash

Dry bearings

Hubs, headset, and bottom bracket all spin on grease. Once it washes out, they grind metal on metal until the whole assembly needs replacing.

$100 – $300 replacement

Drivetrain cascade

A worn, dirty chain files down the cassette and chainrings to match its own stretch. Replace the chain on time and it costs $25; wait, and all three go together.

$150 – $400+ cascade

Safety failures

Brakes and headsets fail quietly — fading lever feel, a faint knock over bumps — until they fail suddenly at speed. A tune catches what your hands have stopped noticing.

Priceless, honestly

Resale value

A bike with a service history and a clean drivetrain sells fast and high. One that creaks and skips gets lowballed — buyers price in every repair they can hear.

$100s at resale

How often does your bike need a tune?

"Once a year" fits most riders, but mileage, weather, and how much you do at home all move the number. Answer three questions for a personalized interval:

1. How much do you ride?
2. What do you ride through?
3. Do you do your own chain cleaning + basic care?

    📅 Book early spring. Shops back up badly in May when everyone's bike comes out of the garage at once — a March or April appointment means days, not weeks, of waiting, and a tuned bike for the whole season.

    The cost of skipping it

    A tune-up looks expensive right up until you price the alternative. These are typical U.S. shop rates:

    🔧 Basic annual tune$75 – $150
    🛠️ Full overhaul (every few years)$200 – $350
    ⛓️ Cascading drivetrain replacement$150 – $400+
    🚑 Crash repairs (and the rest of it)$300 – $1,000+

    What keeping up looks like

    • $75–150 basic tune: gears indexed, brakes adjusted, drivetrain cleaned, everything torqued and trued.
    • $200–350 overhaul every 2–4 years: bearings repacked, cables and housing replaced, frame stripped and rebuilt.
    • $25 chain replaced on schedule, before it wears anything else.

    What skipping looks like

    • $150–400+ drivetrain cascade: stretched chain ruins the cassette, then the chainrings.
    • Seized seatposts and corroded cables: a $0 prevention (grease, fresh housing) becomes a shop-hours extraction job.
    • Crash repairs: a brake or headset that fails at speed costs wheels, bars, derailleur hangers — and sometimes skin.
    A decade of annual tunes runs roughly $1,000–1,800 — about the price of one mid-range replacement bike. Skipping doesn't save the money; it just changes what you spend it on.

    How to keep it tuned

    This task splits cleanly in two: the shop handles the precision work once a year, and you run a 5-minute M-check between visits. Here's both halves.

    ⚠️ Brakes and steering are not "ride it and see"

    • If a brake lever pulls to the bar, or the bike won't stop confidently, don't ride it — fix or shop first.
    • Anything loose at the handlebars, stem, or headset is a steering failure waiting for a pothole.
    • After any crash or hard drop, check the frame and fork before the next ride — especially carbon.

    🔧 What the shop does in a tune-up

    Drivetrain deep-clean & lube Derailleur indexing Brake adjust & pad check Wheel truing Hub, headset & BB bearing check Chain wear measurement Full-frame torque check

    🅼 Your job: the pre-ride M-check

    The M-check traces a letter M over the whole bike: front axle → up to the handlebars → down to the bottom bracket and pedals → up to the saddle → back to the rear axle. Follow the path and you touch every system that can fail on you — wheels, brakes, steering, drivetrain, seating. Do it before the first ride of any week; it takes five minutes and no tools.

    📐 Three checks worth knowing by feel

    Trace the M

    Front axle → handlebars → bottom bracket → saddle → rear axle. One path, every critical system. Five touch points, five minutes.

    Read the pad grooves

    Rim-brake pads have wear grooves molded in. Grooves clearly visible: fine. Grooves nearly flush: replace now, before the metal backing meets your rim.

    Tires & spokes by hand

    Scan sidewalls for cracks and threads showing. Then squeeze spoke pairs around each wheel — one that feels much looser than its neighbors needs a shop truing.

    When it's a shop job, not a checklist

    • Hydraulic brake bleeds. Spongy hydraulic levers mean air or old fluid in the line — a fluid-specific, mess-prone job shops do in 20 minutes.
    • Bearing replacement. Pressed headset, hub, and bottom bracket bearings need dedicated presses and pullers; improvising damages the frame.
    • Wheel building and major truing. Replacing spokes or fixing a real wobble is a craft. A bad home truing job usually ends with a worse wheel.
    • Suspension service. Forks and rear shocks need seal kits, suspension fluid, and torque specs on a strict service schedule (typically every 50–100 hours of riding).
    • Carbon inspection after a crash. Carbon can look perfect and be compromised underneath. After any significant impact, have a shop inspect it before you ride — frames fail without warning.

    Common questions

    What does a tune-up actually include?

    A basic tune typically covers: drivetrain cleaning and lube, derailleur indexing, brake adjustment and pad inspection, minor wheel truing, a bearing check (hubs, headset, bottom bracket), tire inspection, and a bolt-by-bolt torque check. A full overhaul adds complete disassembly, bearing repacking or replacement, and new cables and housing. Parts are extra in both cases.

    How much should a tune-up cost?

    Typical U.S. shop rates: $75–150 for a basic tune and $200–350 for a full overhaul, plus parts (pads, chains, cables). City shops and high-end bikes trend higher. If a quote includes a written checklist of what's covered, that's a good sign.

    Do e-bikes need tune-ups too?

    Yes — more, if anything. The motor's extra torque wears chains, cassettes, and brake pads noticeably faster than leg power alone. On top of the standard mechanical tune, ask for a firmware update check and a battery health look. Many shops offer e-bike-specific service tiers; use a shop certified for your motor system.

    Can I learn to do all this myself?

    Absolutely — bike maintenance is one of the most learnable home skills. Park Tool's free online repair guides and YouTube channel are the de facto textbook, and many local shops and co-ops run hands-on classes. Start with chain care, brake pads, and indexing; leave bearings and hydraulics to the shop until you've got tools and confidence.

    When in the year should I book the tune?

    Late winter or early spring — February through April. Every shop's repair queue explodes in May, and waits stretch from days to weeks. An off-peak booking gets faster turnaround, sometimes off-season pricing, and a fully sorted bike before the good weather starts. Year-round commuters do well with a fall tune instead, so the bike enters the wet season fresh.

    One tune a year. Easy to plan, easy to forget.
