Do a Monthly Mental Check-In

We service the car, descale the kettle, floss for the dentist, and rotate the tires — but the one thing we rarely put on a schedule is ourselves. And mental health doesn't announce itself. It drifts, quietly: a little less sleep, a little more stress, one more cancelled plan, until one day you realize you've been running on empty for months. A mental check-in is just routine maintenance for you — a gentle, honest pause once a month to ask "how am I, really?" and tend the basics: sleep, stress, connection, movement, and the things that bring you joy. It won't fix everything, and it isn't therapy. But it helps you notice when you're running low and do one small thing about it — or reach out for real help — before things get hard.

🗓️ Takes ~10 minutes, once a month 🌱 Catches low periods early 🫶 Costs nothing to start 💚 A kindness, not a test

Why it matters

You wouldn't drive 50,000 miles without an oil change, or skip the dentist for a decade — you'd know something was quietly wearing out. Your wellbeing is exactly the same, except it doesn't have a warning light. A monthly check-in is the warning light you build for yourself. Here's what a regular pause does:

It catches the slow drift

Mental health rarely falls off a cliff — it drifts. Stress, low mood, and burnout build so gradually you adapt to each small dip, until one day the gap is huge. A monthly pause lets you spot the trend early, while it's still a nudge and not a crisis.

A quiet slide you don't notice

The basics move the needle

Most low stretches trace back to the same handful of things: not enough sleep, no movement, too little sunlight, no real rest, and not enough connection. They're the "maintenance items" of a mind — and tending even one of them often lifts more than you'd expect.

The basics quietly running out

Naming it lowers the pressure

Feelings you can't name quietly steer you. Saying "I'm actually pretty worn down" — in a journal, a walk, or out loud to someone — takes the vague weight off your chest and turns it into something you can look at, understand, and gently do something about.

A fog you can't get a grip on

Small early steps beat crisis fixes

A short rest, a boundary, an honest conversation, or booking a therapist is far easier to do when you're merely tired than when you're in freefall. Catching it early means the fix is small and gentle — not an emergency you have to claw your way out of.

Waiting until it's a crisis

How often should you check in?

Once a month is a gentle, sustainable default for most people — frequent enough to catch a drift, light enough to actually keep up. But a hard stretch, a low mood, or feeling isolated all make a more frequent rhythm worth it. The questions below just suggest a cadence and remind you that help is always available. They do not score or diagnose your mental health — please be kind to yourself as you answer.

1. How have you been feeling lately?
There are no wrong answers here, and a hard week doesn't mean anything is broken. If things have felt heavy, checking in a little more often — and being gentle with yourself — is worth it.
2. What's life asking of you right now?
The hardest seasons are exactly when we drop our own maintenance — and when we need it most. During a big life load, checking in more often is a way of looking after yourself while you carry it.
3. How connected do you feel to support?
Isolation makes everything heavier and quieter. If you're feeling alone, more frequent check-ins — and gently reaching toward one person or a professional — can matter a great deal.
4. Do you already have ways of tending yourself?
If you usually push things down, a set monthly rhythm gives your feelings a scheduled, permitted place to surface — so they don't have to build up until they demand your attention all at once.

Many workplaces offer an EAP (Employee Assistance Program) with a few free sessions, and community clinics and therapists often work on a sliding scale.     How to do a monthly mental check-in

    There's no right way to do this, and it's meant to feel like care, not homework. Find ten quiet minutes, be honest and gentle with yourself, and work through the small steps below. Read the caring note first — it matters more than any technique — then move through the ideas and the checklist. Your progress saves automatically, just on this device.

    🌿 Five gentle ways to check in

    1. Ask honestly: "How am I, really?"

    Not the reflex "fine" — the real answer. Sit with it for a moment and name the feeling: worn down, restless, content, lonely, hopeful. Journaling or a quick voice note gives it somewhere to land. Naming it is most of the work.

    2. Check the basics

    Run down the maintenance items: sleep, movement, food, sunlight, connection, and real rest. Which one's running low? Nine times out of ten, a rough patch traces back to one of these — and that's good news, because those you can tend.

    3. Tend one thing

    Not everything — one thing. A walk outside, an earlier night, a boundary, less doomscrolling, or more of whatever quietly brings you joy. Small and doable beats ambitious and abandoned. One gentle nudge in the right direction is a win.

    4. Reach out

    Send the text. Call the friend. Book or keep the therapy appointment. Reaching out isn't a last resort or an admission of failure — it's normal, healthy maintenance, the same as any other. "I've been struggling a bit" is enough to start.

    5. Know the signs — and set the next one

    Learn the signs it's time for a professional (below), and save the crisis numbers in your phone before you ever need them. Then pick a gentle date for your next check-in — a recurring nudge means you never have to remember on your own.

    Signs it's time to reach out for professional help

    A check-in helps you notice — this is what to notice for. If any of these show up, please treat it as a nudge to talk to a doctor or a mental-health professional. Needing help isn't a failure of the check-in; it's exactly what the check-in is meant to catch. You deserve support long before things become unbearable.

    • Low mood, anxiety, hopelessness, irritability, or loss of interest lasting 2+ weeks. A hard week is human. When the heaviness settles in and stays — especially when things you used to enjoy stop feeling good — that's the two-week signal to reach out to a professional.
    • Changes in sleep, appetite, or energy. Sleeping far too much or too little, big shifts in eating, or a fatigue that rest doesn't fix are your body flagging that something needs attention. They're common, treatable, and worth mentioning to a doctor.
    • Withdrawing from people. Quietly cancelling plans, dodging messages, and pulling away from the people who care about you is one of the clearest early signs — and one of the easiest to explain away. If you notice it, gently reach back out.
    • Using alcohol or substances to cope. Leaning on a drink, a substance, or anything else to get through the day or numb the feeling is a sign the load has outgrown your current tools. A professional can help you find steadier ones, without judgement.
    • Feeling overwhelmed or unable to function. When ordinary tasks — work, chores, showing up — start to feel impossible, that's not laziness or weakness. It's a real signal that you're running past empty and it's time to get support.
    • Any thoughts of harming yourself. Please don't wait on this one. Reach out to a professional or a crisis line right away — in the US, call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), or text HOME to 741741. You are not alone, and things can get better with help.
    • Gentle reminder: you don't need to be "bad enough" to deserve help. If you're wondering whether to talk to someone, that wondering is reason enough. Therapy is maintenance, not a rescue — and starting early is a gift to your future self.

    Common questions

    How often should I check in on my mental health?

    Once a month is a gentle, sustainable rhythm for most people — often enough to catch a slow drift, light enough that you'll actually keep it up. Think of it like any other maintenance interval: frequent enough to prevent problems, not so frequent it becomes a chore. During a stressful stretch — a big loss, a move, a new baby, a rough patch at work — it's worth checking in more often, even weekly, because those are exactly the times we tend to drop our own care. And there's no rule against a quick, lighter check-in whenever you sense something shifting; the monthly one is just the anchor that makes sure it happens even when life is busy.

    What does a mental check-in actually involve?

    It's simpler than it sounds — no special training, app, or ritual required. Find ten quiet minutes and honestly ask yourself "how am I, really?", naming whatever comes up instead of settling for "fine." Then run through the basics: how's your sleep, movement, eating, sunlight, connection, and rest? Notice which one is running low. Pick one small thing to tend — a walk, an earlier night, a text to a friend, less scrolling. Reach out to someone if things feel heavy, and if the answers worry you, take it as a cue to talk to a professional. Journaling, a voice note, a walk, or a conversation are all fine ways to do it. The whole point is noticing and caring — gently, without judgement.

    Is this a substitute for therapy?

    No — and it's important to be clear about that. A monthly check-in is a supportive self-care habit, like flossing or a daily walk. It helps you notice how you're doing and tend the basics, but it can't diagnose anything, and it's no replacement for professional care. Think of it as the smoke detector, not the fire department: its job is to help you notice early and prompt you to get real help when you need it. If your check-in keeps turning up heaviness — or if low mood, anxiety, or hopelessness last more than a couple of weeks or start affecting your daily life — please treat that as your signal to see a doctor or therapist. The habit and the professional support work best together.

    How do I know when to see a professional?

    A useful rule of thumb is the two-week mark: if low mood, anxiety, hopelessness, sleeplessness, or loss of interest lasts more than about two weeks, keeps getting worse, or starts affecting your work, relationships, or ability to get through the day, it's time to talk to a doctor or a mental-health professional. The other key question is functioning — are ordinary things starting to feel impossible? You also never have to hit a threshold to deserve help; if you're wondering whether to reach out, that's reason enough. And any thoughts of harming yourself mean reaching out right now, not waiting — in the US, call or text 988, or text HOME to 741741. Getting help early makes everything easier, and it's a sign of strength, not weakness.

    Is therapy worth it, and how do I find affordable help?

    For many people, yes — therapy is one of the most worthwhile investments they make, and it's completely normal, not a last resort. If cost is a worry, there are more options than people realize. Start with your workplace: many employers offer an EAP (Employee Assistance Program) that includes a handful of free, confidential counselling sessions. Community mental-health clinics, university training clinics, and lots of independent therapists offer sliding-scale fees based on income. Online directories let you filter by "sliding scale" or "low cost," and a primary-care doctor can point you toward local resources. Group therapy and support groups are often free or low-cost too. Weighed against the toll of an untreated low period, even paid therapy usually pays for itself — and there is genuinely no shame in getting help, or in medication if a professional recommends it. That's maintenance too.

    What do I do in a crisis?

    If you're in crisis or having any thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out right now — you don't have to get through it alone, and help is available 24/7. In the US, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or text HOME to 741741 to reach the Crisis Text Line and talk with a trained counsellor. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number (911 in the US). If you can, tell one person you trust what's happening — you don't need the right words, just "I'm not okay" is enough. Outside the US, findahelpline.com lists free, confidential crisis services by country. Whatever you're feeling right now, it can change — and reaching out is the bravest, kindest thing you can do.
